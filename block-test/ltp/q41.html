Q41 · Only consequences count; intended v foreseen is religious

Paper 11, Section A · This year's statement style · question 41 of 60

“Whether an act is right depends only on its consequences. A doctor who withholds treatment knowing that his patient will die kills him as surely as one who gives a lethal injection, so the law should treat the two alike, banning both or permitting both. Theories that distinguish intended from foreseen harm are religious doctrines that have no place in a secular legal system.”

Critically evaluate and comment on this statement by reference to the theories of Finnis, Rawls and Hart covered in this course, and any relevant illustrations.

Pick 3

Why these three

What the question is testing. The hidden tension: sentence 3 bans a distinction as religious, but sentence 1's consequentialism is just as much a comprehensive doctrine. Sentence 2's example also changes two things at once: omitting against acting, and foreseeing against intending.

Sentence 2, "kills him as surely": 5.3. Acts are individuated primarily by intention, so a certain death can be a side effect. Concede Liu's point that the line is artificial. But a doctor who withholds treatment in order that the patient die intends death too.

"Treat the two alike": 7.2. Hart's paternalism answers clouded consent. That is a difference between the two cases which the statement ignores.

Sentence 3, "religious doctrines": 6.4. Double effect is Aquinas's, but for Finnis it is not the principle of practical reason. Rawls treats secular comprehensive arguments the same way as religious ones, so consequentialism has no better claim.

The close ones. 5.4 answers sentence 1, 5.7 makes the concession, and 6.7 says religious arguments are not excluded.

Verdict line (from the answer guide)

The statement undermines itself: its consequentialism is as contested a comprehensive doctrine as the one it bans, and its example runs acting and omitting together with intending and foreseeing; the intended/foreseen line is contestable, as Liu concedes, but it is argued from practical reason, so the question is whether it tracks a real moral difference, not whether a church once taught it.
