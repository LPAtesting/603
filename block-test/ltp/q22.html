Q22 · Ruritania: commercial surrogacy and adoption

Paper 6, Section A · This year's problem style · question 22 of 60

Ruritania is a country with a society and legal system substantially similar to Singapore’s. Commercial surrogacy is currently unregulated there: it is neither prohibited nor recognised, and Ruritanian couples increasingly travel abroad to arrange it. A Ruritanian Member of Parliament proposes a bill that would make it an offence, punishable by up to two years’ imprisonment, to pay or receive payment for surrogacy, whether in Ruritania or abroad, and would bar the courts from making adoption orders in favour of anyone who has done so. She argues that, first, paid surrogacy turns children into commodities and exploits poor women; second, the family is the foundation of society and must be protected by law; and third, most religious groups in Ruritania oppose it.

If you were also a Ruritanian Member of Parliament, how would you vote on this bill, and why? Further, suppose that the bill is passed without the adoption bar, and that a couple who paid for a surrogacy abroad applies to adopt the child, now three, whom they have raised since birth. The adoption statute requires the court to treat the child’s welfare as the first and paramount consideration, but also allows it to refuse an order that would be “contrary to public policy”. As a Ruritanian judge, how would you decide the application? Explain and justify your views with reference to the relevant theories and material covered in this course.

Pick 3

Why these three

What the question is testing. Two hats. The MP faces three grounds and a bill that jails parents for arrangements abroad. The judge has to read an open public-policy phrase against a paramount welfare clause. The catch is that the bill's punishment and its stated purpose pull apart.

MP, "exploits poor women": 7.2. Hart's paternalism answers defective consent, so financial pressure supports restricting brokers. It does not obviously support jailing those it protects.

MP, "the family is the foundation of society": 7.9. Protecting the family can mean punishing, regulating or promoting, and the justification rises with the coercion. Two years in prison needs more than the family's importance.

Judge, "contrary to public policy": 9.2. The phrase is open, but that is a dispute about law's grounds, not a gap. The child's welfare is principle; deterrence is policy for Parliament, already expressed in the offence. So grant the order.

The close ones. 12.4 gives the judge's ladder, 7.4 tests the family ground, and 7.10 disposes of religious opposition.

Verdict line (from the answer guide)

As an MP I would support a ban on commercial brokering and payment within Ruritania but vote against jailing parents for arrangements abroad and against the adoption bar; as a judge I would grant the order, because refusing it would serve the policy only by sacrificing the paramount interest of a child the policy was meant to protect.
