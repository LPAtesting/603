Q21 · Victors' justice and the grudge informer

Paper 6, Section A · This year's statement style · question 21 of 60

“When a tyranny falls, the new courts may punish its officials and informers only under laws that existed when they acted; anything else is victors’ justice, no better than the regime it replaces. Those who obeyed the tyrant’s laws did nothing legally wrong, and their moral guilt is for historians, not judges. The post-war German courts that convicted the grudge informers were therefore wrong.”

Critically evaluate and comment on this statement by reference to the theories of Hart, Fuller, Radbruch and Alexy covered in this course, and any relevant illustrations.

Pick 3

Why these three

What the question is testing. The hidden tension: sentence 1 allows punishment under existing laws, and the informer was convicted in 1949 under the 1871 Code. So sentence 1 does not condemn the German courts. Sentence 3 follows only if the Nazi statute was law, the very point in dispute.

Sentence 1, "laws that existed when they acted": 8.1. State the facts: she was convicted under an existing code. Hart's honest options, letting her go or a frankly retrospective law, mark where the statement is right.

Sentence 2, "did nothing legally wrong": 8.7. The sentence merges three duties. It holds legally for Hart, and for Radbruch only below the threshold. Whether they did anything morally wrong stays open, so moral guilt is not just for historians.

Sentence 3, "were therefore wrong": 8.3. Alexy's reply: a court refusing an evidently unjust statute treats it as law at first but finally not law. There is no covert retroactivity, and the rights consensus answers the victors' justice charge.

The close ones. 8.2 is Hart's best case, 8.4 shows the label matters, and 8.6 backs sentence 1.

Verdict line (from the answer guide)

The statement proves less than it claims: the informer was convicted under a law that existed when she acted, so the real question is whether the Nazi statute she sheltered behind was law. Hart's candour argues for a retrospective statute; Alexy shows a court can deny an evidently unjust statute legal character without retroactivity.
