Q26 · Ruritania: abortion limit and two Art 9 challenges

Paper 7, Section A · This year's problem style · question 26 of 60

Ruritania is a country with a society and legal system substantially similar to Singapore’s. Its law permits termination of pregnancy for any reason up to 24 weeks, and afterwards only to save the woman’s life or to prevent grave permanent injury to her health. A Ruritanian Member of Parliament proposes a bill that would lower the limit to 14 weeks, require counselling on alternatives such as adoption, and provide state support for women who carry their pregnancies to term. She argues that, first, her faith teaches that human life begins at conception; second, ultrasound images show a recognisably human form well before 24 weeks; and third, a poll shows that most Ruritanians, including many who support legal abortion, favour a lower limit.

If you were also a Ruritanian Member of Parliament, how would you vote on this bill, and why? Further, suppose the bill is passed. A women’s group challenges it, arguing that “personal liberty” in Ruritania’s equivalent of Article 9(1) includes a right to terminate a pregnancy up to viability. At the same time, a pro-life group argues that the new law still breaches the unborn child’s right to “life”, because it permits termination for any reason up to 14 weeks. As a Ruritanian judge, how would you decide each argument? Explain and justify your views with reference to the relevant theories and material covered in this course.

Why these three

What the question is testing. Two hats again: the MP's three grounds, and two opposite challenges to the same law. The catch is that the judge must reject both for the same reason.

MP, the vote: 6.5. TSH's hunter shows why permission settles the metaphysical question, so Parliament cannot stand aside. This bill resembles the pro-life middle she describes, and pro-choicers would reject it. Remember that Finnis's article tests Rawls's method.

Judge, "personal liberty": 11.2. LMS confines Art 9 to unlawful deprivation of life and detention, and YVK (2015) creates no rights out of whole cloth. So the women's group fails.

Judge, "life": 11.5. No broad historical consensus treats a 14-week limit as intolerable, either way. Where objective morality is not clear, a deliberative democracy takes a stand through its legislature.

The close ones. 6.4, 7.11 and 4.4 each dispose of one ground: faith, poll and ultrasound.

Trap to avoid. Faith pulls you to 7.10, but this MP offers more than faith.

Verdict line (from the answer guide)

As an MP I would [support the bill as a defensible middle that protects later-term life while supporting women / oppose it, as a 14-week limit without consensus]; as a judge I would reject both challenges, because neither side's reading of Art 9 meets the threshold for overriding Parliament on a question where reasonable people divide.
