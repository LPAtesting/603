Q15 · Easy cases make theory unnecessary

Paper 4, Section B · Past year, TSH style · question 15 of 60

“Hard cases make bad law, but easy cases make legal theory unnecessary. Most of what lawyers and judges do is routine, and in routine cases legal theory has no work to do.”

To what extent is this statement true? What makes a case “easy” or “hard”, and is that distinction itself free of legal theory? What are the implications of your answer for adjudication and for the legal practice of lawyers? Critically comment by reference to relevant theories and material covered in the course.

Pick 3

Why these three

What the question is testing. Three asks: how true is the statement, what makes a case easy or hard, and what follows for judges and lawyers? The catch: the line between easy and hard is itself drawn by a theory.

"Legal theory has no work to do": 2.6. Fish grants the kernel: citing theory in court looks like showing off. But citing theory and using it differ, since easy cases rest on a shared theory.

What makes a case "easy" or "hard": 9.9. Easy cases are easy because lawyers share a theory, and Dworkin adds that hard cases may be just those where fit yields no unique reading. The line is therefore not free of theory.

Adjudication, "routine cases": 9.8. A judge who says he simply applies the statute has taken a side. Morality is always presupposed but operative only in hard cases, so concede Hart's core of central meaning.

The close ones. 9.1 gives the realist view, 11.7 Hart's penumbra line, and 10.4 the lawyer's constant interpretation.

Trap to avoid. "Hard cases" pulls you to 3.6, which is about rules and standards.

Verdict line (from the answer guide)

Routine cases are routine because a shared theory has gone quiet, not because there is none; the line between easy and hard is drawn by that theory, so lawyers and judges are using it even when they do not cite it.
