Q38 · Ruritania: ousting review of security detention

Paper 10, Section A · This year's problem style · question 38 of 60

Ruritania is a country with a society and legal system substantially similar to Singapore’s. Its Internal Security Act allows the minister to detain without trial, for renewable two-year periods, anyone he is satisfied is acting in a manner prejudicial to national security. The courts have so far reviewed such detentions only for compliance with the Act’s procedures. After a court orders a detainee’s release because a procedural step was missed, the government proposes a constitutional amendment, needing a two-thirds majority, providing that “no detention under the Internal Security Act shall be called into question in any court on any ground”. One detainee has now been held for five years for social-media posts urging a general strike.

If you were a Ruritanian Member of Parliament, how would you vote on the amendment, and why? Further, if the amendment were passed and that detainee applied for release on the ground that the amendment itself is void, how would you decide the application as a Ruritanian judge? Explain and justify your views with reference to the relevant theories and material covered in this course.

Pick 3

Why these three

What the question is testing. Two hats on an ouster clause. The MP decides whether to remove the only check on detention, and the judge whether the amendment itself is void. The catch: a grave departure from legality may still fall short of the Radbruch threshold.

MP, "on any ground": 3.4. Fuller condemns uncontrolled administrative discretion, and enforcement only against critics approaches it. Discretion fits legality only if it is bounded and reviewable, and the ouster removes that.

Judge, "the amendment itself is void": 11.8. Kesavananda suggested the separation of powers as a basic feature, but it was rejected here. Liu says it is easier for natural law, and the Radbruch power is untested against a sitting legislature.

Judge, how to decide: 12.4. Climb the ladder. Validity, then interpretation: read the words as covering only detentions genuinely made under the Act. Refuse only at the threshold, and candour bars covert routes.

The close ones. 3.7 gives reciprocity and crisis, 11.1 the case law, and 8.5 the threshold.

Trap to avoid. Two-thirds pulls you to 11.6, but Art 156 is a far narrower shield.

Verdict line (from the answer guide)

As an MP I would vote against an ouster that frees discretion from every check; as a judge I would read "detention under the Internal Security Act" as covering only detentions genuinely made under the Act, and would not void the amendment itself, because on TSH's threshold this grave departure from legality is not yet intolerable injustice by broad historical consensus. I would say plainly that a regime using it against critics drifts toward Fuller's failure of law.
