Q14 · Ruritania: stripping citizenship by descent

Paper 4, Section A · This year's problem style · question 14 of 60

Ruritania is a country with a society and legal system substantially similar to Singapore’s. After a terrorist attack carried out by two naturalised citizens of Bordurian descent, a Ruritanian Member of Parliament proposes a constitutional amendment and an accompanying bill. The bill would strip citizenship from every naturalised citizen of Bordurian descent (about 40,000 people, most of whom have lived in Ruritania for decades) and require them to leave within a year. The amendment, which needs a two-thirds majority, provides that no law relating to citizenship may be challenged as inconsistent with the fundamental liberties. The MP argues that national security must come first, that the measure is general and published in advance, and that a recent poll found 71% support for it.

If you were also a Ruritanian Member of Parliament, how would you vote on the amendment and on the bill, and why? Further, if both were passed and a person affected by the bill brought a challenge before you as a Ruritanian judge, would you uphold and apply the bill? Explain and justify your views with reference to the relevant theories and material covered in this course.

Why these three

What the question is testing. Two hats again, with the MP's three grounds and a shielding amendment. The catch: this bill may reach the Radbruch threshold, unlike the usual Ruritania problem, so the judge's answer changes too.

MP, "general and published in advance": 3.3. The MP answers the wrong question. Legality is thin: a regime can meet all eight requirements and still be wicked.

MP and judge, "uphold and apply the bill": 8.8. A law targeting people by descent asks whether the statute even attempts justice. Where equality is deliberately betrayed it lacks the nature of law. Confine the limb to deliberate, facial betrayal, and test it by broad historical consensus.

The amendment, "a two-thirds majority": 11.8. A vote cannot found legitimacy on its own. Kesavananda's basic features were rejected here, so the judge's route is natural law and the Radbruch formula, a power untested against a sitting legislature.

The close ones. 11.6 is the model for the shield, 7.11 deals with the poll, and 12.5 adds the risk effect.

Trap to avoid. Ruritania sends you to 12.6, but its verdict on the alcohol bill is that the law is harsh, not intolerable.

Verdict line (from the answer guide)

As an MP I would vote against both, because the bill betrays equality and the amendment exists only to shelter it; as a judge I would refuse to apply the bill as intolerably unjust by broad historical consensus, conceding the democratic objection and TSH's own warning that the formula must stay a narrow backstop.
