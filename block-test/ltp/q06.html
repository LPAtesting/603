Q6 · Ruritania: assisted dying

Paper 2, Section A · This year's problem style · question 6 of 60

Ruritania is a country with a society and legal system substantially similar to Singapore’s. A Ruritanian Member of Parliament proposes a bill that would allow a doctor to prescribe a lethal drug to an adult who is terminally ill with less than six months to live, has been assessed as mentally competent by two doctors, and has made a written request twice, fourteen days apart. She argues that, first, a competent adult has the right to decide when and how his own life ends; second, studies from jurisdictions with similar laws show that most applicants cite loss of autonomy and dignity rather than pain; and third, the safeguards ensure that no one is pressured. Opponents reply that elderly and disabled people will come to feel a duty to die, and that the bill crosses a line that the law should never cross: the intentional killing of the innocent.

If you were also a Ruritanian Member of Parliament, how would you vote on this bill, and why? Further, if the bill were passed and a disability-rights group challenged it as inconsistent with Ruritania’s equivalents of Articles 9(1) and 12 of Singapore’s Constitution, how would you decide the challenge as a Ruritanian judge? Explain and justify your views with reference to the relevant theories and material covered in this course.

Pick 3

Why these three

What the question is testing. Two hats. As MP, the proposer's three grounds and the opponents' two replies; as judge, Arts 9(1) and 12. The catch: the best MP answer turns on intention and on consent, and neither decides the judge's question.

MP, "the intentional killing of the innocent": 5.3. The opponents' line is Finnis's absolute: intending death chooses directly against life, whatever the request. Qualify with Liu: the line is artificial and a heavy limit on autonomy.

MP, "a duty to die": 7.2. Hart's paternalism is about defective consent. Pressure on the elderly and disabled is exactly that worry, so it tests whether the safeguards work.

Judge, "Articles 9(1) and 12": 11.2. The Act survives the readings of "law", and the Radbruch floor is met only if a broad historical consensus condemns it. None does, so uphold.

The close ones. 11.3 handles Art 12 in a line, 4.5 answers the autonomy ground, and 12.6 frames the two hats.

Trap to avoid. The studies need an ought-premise (4.4); do not treat them as deciding the vote.

Verdict line (from the answer guide)

As an MP I would [vote against, on Finnis's absolute and Hart's worry about pressured consent / vote for, with stronger independent safeguards]; as a judge I would uphold the Act, because neither reading of Art 9(1) nor Art 12 reaches a contested moral choice that no broad historical consensus condemns.
