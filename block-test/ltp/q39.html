Q39 · The duty to obey lapses only when law ceases to be law

Paper 10, Section B · Past year, TSH style · question 39 of 60

“Citizens have a moral obligation to obey the law because they benefit from a system that others uphold. That obligation does not lapse merely because a particular law is unjust; it lapses only when the law ceases to be law.”

To what extent do the theories covered in this course support this view? When, if ever, may a citizen, an official or a lawyer disobey the law? Critically comment by reference to relevant theories and material covered in the course.

Why these three

What the question is testing. Is the duty grounded in fair play, and does it lapse only when law ceases to be law? Then: when may a citizen, an official or a lawyer disobey? Each actor needs its own block.

The citizen, "a moral obligation to obey": 8.7. Liu's pigeon feeder gives fair play as the ground. The table then shows how far the theorists support the statement: for Hart the moral duty can lapse while the law stands, so he rejects "only when".

The official: 12.5. Officials owe fidelity to law, not deference to rank. Alexy's risk effect reaches the official, and Thunder's line closes off pleading the role.

The lawyer: 10.11. Ask Liu's three questions in order. Pepper's limit is the law; if the formula denies a racist statute legal character, even his lawyer may refuse.

The close ones. 3.7 shows the duty fading with reciprocity, 8.2 is Hart's best line, and 8.3 gives Alexy.

Verdict line (from the answer guide)

The obligation is real and grounded in fair play, but the statement ties it too tightly to validity: on Hart's view a valid law can be too evil to obey, and on Fuller's the duty fades with reciprocity before the law disappears entirely.
