Q10 · Ruritania: lax enforcement, lawyer then judge

Paper 3, Section A · This year's problem style (lawyer and judge variant) · question 10 of 60

Ruritania is a country with a society and legal system substantially similar to Singapore’s. Its Coastal Waters Act makes it an offence for any vessel to discharge waste containing more than 50 mg of oil per litre within 20 km of the coast. The enforcement agency announces publicly that, given its limited resources, it will prosecute only discharges above 100 mg per litre.

(a) You are in-house counsel to Bluefin Shipping. Its managers ask you what the law requires and at what level of discharge the company can “safely” operate, making it clear that cleaner discharge would cost the company millions a year. What would you advise, and what, if anything, would you say to them beyond the law?

(b) Two years later, under new leadership and without any public announcement, the agency prosecutes a small local fishing company for discharging 80 mg per litre. You are the trial judge. The company argues that it relied on the agency’s announcement, and that the prosecution is selective because larger operators discharging at similar levels have not been charged. How would you decide the case?

Explain and justify your views with reference to the relevant theories and material covered in this course.

Pick 3

Why these three

What the question is testing. Two roles on one set of facts. In (a) counsel must say what the law requires and what lies beyond it. In (b) a judge decides whether a public announcement and uneven prosecution defeat a charge that is, on its face, made out.

Part (a), "what the law requires": 10.2. Enforcement risk is accurate advice, but realism empties the limit. Liu's point decides it: truthfulness requires both figures, and the risk that the policy changes.

Part (a), "beyond the law": 10.3. Dialogue is the real addition. A corporate officer is the easy case for it, since cost, not hostility, is the limit.

Part (b), "relied on the agency's announcement": 3.2. Open non-enforcement strains congruence, and Tan Seng Kee is the analogy: an official assurance may bind until clear notice. A silent reversal gives none.

The close ones. 3.4 and 7.7 answer selectivity, and 10.1 marks the limit of objection.

Trap to avoid. Do not treat the reversal as retrospective: the 50 mg offence was always there.

Verdict line (from the answer guide)

As counsel I would state the 50 mg rule, the announced threshold and the risk that it changes, and recommend compliance in a frank dialogue; as judge I would hold that the agency's public assurance bound it until clear notice, so I would not convict on these facts.
