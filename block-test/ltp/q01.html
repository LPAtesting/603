Q1 · LP v NLT only matters in wicked regimes

Paper 1, Section A · This year's statement style · question 1 of 60

“The debate between legal positivism and natural law theory matters only in wicked regimes. In a broadly just society such as Singapore’s, the two schools will always reach the same answer, because the positive law already embodies the community’s morality. A law student who masters the cases and statutes has therefore learned everything that matters about what the law is.”

Critically evaluate and comment on this statement by reference to the theories of Hart, Dworkin, Fuller and Radbruch covered in this course, and any relevant illustrations.

Pick 3

Why these three

What the question is testing. The statement uses a natural-law premise, that law "already embodies the community's morality", to reach a positivist conclusion, that cases and statutes tell you "what the law is". It then calls the debate idle. Each of the best three answers one sentence.

Sentence 1, "only in wicked regimes": 8.5. It is the only block on Radbruch's formula in a stable democracy. Concede that the formula is a narrow check, then use TSH: Arts 9 and 12 may import it, so the debate has a home in Singapore.

Sentence 2, "always reach the same answer": 2.5. Dworkin: in Riggs, judges in a broadly just system split over what the law required, and in McLoughlin the Lords reached one result for split reasons. The same answer does not mean the same grounds.

Sentence 3, "masters the cases and statutes": 9.8. Line 1: the student has already taken a side on what counts as law. Lines 2 and 3 give the fair verdict: the schools agree where morality is only presupposed, and part company where it is operative.

The close ones. 8.2, 11.7 and 3.2 each give a good line for the Hart, sentence 3 and Fuller points, but each comes second for its sentence. Use them inside the paragraphs the best three open.

Trap to avoid. The prep page's trigger table sends "always" to floor v ceiling (10.5, 11.7). Here "always" governs "reach the same answer", so use presupposed v operative (9.8).

Verdict line (from the answer guide)

The statement is right that a just system rarely forces the choice, but wrong that it never does: hard cases and contested constitutional terms turn on a theory of law's grounds, so a student who knows only the cases can answer only the easy questions.
