Q7 · Dworkin's best light is conservatism

Paper 2, Section B · Past year, TSH style · question 7 of 60

“Dworkin’s demand that judges show the law in its best light is conservatism dressed up as principle. Whatever fits the existing legal materials best will always be the dominant ideology of the past, and the principles that challenge it will always be filed away as exceptions.”

Is this criticism sound? What are its implications for how judges should adjudicate and for how lawyers should argue hard cases? Critically comment by reference to the relevant theories of adjudication and the illustrations covered in this course.

Pick 3

Why these three

What the question is testing. Is the crits' conservatism charge sound, and what follows for judges and lawyers? The catch is the word "always": fit is a threshold, not the whole test.

The criticism, "conservatism dressed up as principle": 9.3. State the charge at its best: fit crowns the past's dominant principle and files the rest as anomalies. But condemning hidden interests needs a standard that is not merely subjective.

How judges should adjudicate, "will always be filed away": 9.4. Stare decisis is itself contested. Relaxed precedent yields when "sufficiently wrong", and interpretation must contain a doctrine of mistake, so a Dworkinian judge can demote a dominant principle.

How lawyers should argue, "hard cases": 9.7. Dworkin is text-first and the crits goal-first, so counsel argues fit and value. Victory to the crits follows only if value is subjective.

The close ones. 2.5 adds the lawyer's two-argument line, 9.5 shows fit is a threshold, and 9.10 states the duty under attack.

Trap to avoid. "Dominant ideology" pulls you to 3.9, which defends thin legality, not a theory of judging.

Verdict line (from the answer guide)

Conservatism is a risk of the fit dimension, not its necessity: the value dimension and the doctrine of mistake let a Dworkinian judge demote the past's dominant principle, though fit often leaves the choice to political morality the crits rightly say is contested.
