Q5 · Punishment should match the community's condemnation

Paper 2, Section A · This year's statement style · question 5 of 60

“The criminal law exists to express a society’s moral condemnation. The severity of a punishment should therefore match the gravity of the wrong as the community sees it, whatever harm the conduct does. Only then will citizens see the law as just, and only a law that is seen as just will be obeyed.”

Critically evaluate and comment on this statement by reference to the theories of Hart, Devlin, Fuller and Finnis covered in this course, and any relevant illustrations.

Pick 3

Why these three

What the question is testing. Three sentences: criminal law expresses condemnation, severity should match it, and only then will law be obeyed. The hidden tension: sentence 2 sets harm aside, and sentence 3 then justifies the rule by its consequences.

Sentences 1 and 2, "the gravity of the wrong as the community sees it": 7.8. This is Stephen's thesis, not Devlin's. Hart's two separate questions split whether to punish from how severely, and the slavery point shows a community can err.

Sentence 2 with Fuller, "whatever harm the conduct does": 3.5. A fixed scale treats like cases alike by any standard at all. It is just only if the line tracks real differences in harm or fault, which is the factor the statement excludes.

Sentence 3, "only a law that is seen as just will be obeyed": 8.7. Perceived justice is not the reason to obey. The three duties come apart, and Liu's pigeon feeder shows fair play, not a sense of justice, grounds the moral duty.

The close ones. 7.3 clears Devlin, 3.7 gives Fuller's reciprocity, and 5.3 caps what gravity can license.

Trap to avoid. Severity pulls you to 8.6, but timing is not in issue.

Verdict line (from the answer guide)

Condemnation is part of what criminal law does, but it cannot set severity on its own: how severely to punish is a separate question that needs reasons of harm, fairness and proportion, and the community's sense of gravity is evidence to be tested by critical morality, not a measure to be obeyed.
