Q55 · Trust ordinary people's moral judgment

Paper 14, Section B · Past year, TSH style · question 55 of 60

“The common law has always trusted the moral judgment of ordinary people: juries decide what is reasonable, dishonest or obscene. Legislators should trust that judgment too, and judges should apply it whenever the law refers to public morality.”

Pick 3

Why these three

What the question is testing. Whose moral judgment should count, in making law and in applying it? The statement is Devlin's. The catch: ordinary judgment is good evidence, but it is not the standard.

Sentence 1, "the moral judgment of ordinary people": 7.3. Devlin's man in the jury box: what twelve at random might after discussion be expected to agree on. But unanimity fails on contested issues, and Hart says critical morality must test the result.

Sentence 2, "legislators should trust that judgment too": 7.1. Hart's real question is whether the bare fact of condemnation is any reason to punish. Both sides moralise, so ordinary judgment does not end the argument.

"Judges should apply it": 11.5. On contested moral questions LMS left Hart v Devlin to the Legislature, and TSH's proviso is the Radbruch threshold. Rajeevan is the local authority.

The close ones. 7.11 gives the poll, 9.6 Frank's fact scepticism, and 6.1 Rawls's reasonable citizen.

Verdict line (from the answer guide)

Ordinary moral judgment is good evidence and an indispensable check on judges' idiosyncrasy, but not the standard: it must itself be tested by critical morality, and where the law refers to public morality, judges should apply its considered, not its momentary, form.
