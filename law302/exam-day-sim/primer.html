How to use this page

Read it once in about 45 minutes, or work through it in about 2 hours with notes. Pick the mode in the bar at the top and start the clock; Notes mode also opens a worked example under each archetype. Tick Done as you finish each one.

Chips are Menu boxes: E7 is a box, ✗ B1 a cross (its one-line dismissal, for that contract only), E8.5 a scorecard row. A box's title prints the first time it appears; point at a chip to see it again.

In the worked examples, Alpha Pte Ltd, a Singapore company, is always the client; Rurico, Beta, Gamma and Delta are foreign parties.

The method in four moves

  1. Spot the archetype: in Question 1 for each contract and defendant, then each foreign proceeding, then each judgment; in Question 2 for each issue.
  2. Write its boxes in order, stage by stage.
  3. Adjust from the order slip where the facts differ; a cross applies to that contract only.
  4. Scan the one-off boxes no archetype carries (listed under Likely sets).

Question 1 · one archetype per contract (1 to 7), then 8, then 9 or 10

Both finals give two or three clauses to compare, each with its own date. First list every contract (date, clause, governing law) and every foreign proceeding, as the March 2026 framework does. Per contract: clause, CCAA, service, exercise, SICC, ASI. Judgments last. Drop the SICC step if the question keeps you to the General Division, and the ASI step if no foreign proceeding is to be stopped. A cross (x) applies to that contract only.

Whole-question traps
  • Calling Spiliada "the strong cause test". Strong cause applies only where someone breaks a clause.
1

Singapore clause inside the CCAA

Pattern Dated on or after 1 October 2016, and says "exclusive" or has no label. Defendant abroad.

Tell-tale facts In a hypo it looks like a contract signed after 1 October 2016, between parties in different countries, with a clause naming the Singapore courts that either says "exclusive" or says nothing about exclusivity.

Write in this order
  1. ClauseA1Any jurisdiction clause: what it promises, then A6No label: "the parties submit to the jurisdiction of ..." (no label) or A2Says "exclusive": Singapore courts ("exclusive")
  2. CCAAB1CCAA gate: five conditions, B2CCAA applies and Singapore is the chosen court (s 11) (service out is inside B2, not D8Singapore clause, defendant abroad)
  3. Exercisenone, skip E
  4. SICCF1International and commercial, F2Primary jurisdiction under a written jurisdiction agreement, F3"Singapore High Court" clause: check the date, F4Serving the defendant abroad from the SICC, F5Exercise in the SICC: O 2 r 3, no Spiliada
  5. ASIG1ASI starter, and the basis, G2Amenability, G9ASI in a CCAA case, G7Vexation or oppression, and injustice to the respondent, G10ASI sought from the SICC

Adjust Says "non-exclusive", or dated before 1 October 2016: archetype 2 (promise) or 3 (no promise). Says "exclusive" and its own law gives a promise: G3 in place of G7. Floating clause, Singapore already elected: B1.5, then B2. Transfer between the General Division and the SICC: F7.

Often with 3 or 2 (same clause on an earlier contract), 4 (a contract naming a foreign court); then 9

Worked example opens in Notes mode

Contract Y: dated 2018, no label. Inside the CCAA and deemed exclusive. Archetype 1.

  1. A1 and A6: cl 16 has no label. Construed under Ruritanian law it carries no promise, but its effect on Singapore's jurisdiction is for Singapore law.
  2. B1: no label, so deemed exclusive under s 3(2).
  3. B2: Singapore is the chosen court, so under s 11 it must hear the case.
  4. Stage E: skipped. Rurico's witnesses and its Ruritanian action are irrelevant under s 11(2).
  5. F1 to F5: the action is international and commercial; cl 16 names the High Court and post-dates 1 October 2016, so it includes the SICC.
  6. G1, G2, G9, G7, G10: Rurico is amenable once served. Singapore is the chosen court and Ruritania is outside the Convention, so a contractual ASI is the starting point.
Traps
  • Running Spiliada or strong cause in a CCAA case. Under s 11 Singapore must hear it.

Remember Inside the CCAA, Singapore must hear the case. Skip stage E, serve out under B2, and treat the ASI with care if the clause is exclusive only because the CCAA deems it so.

2

Singapore clause with a promise, outside the CCAA

Pattern Says "non-exclusive", pre-dates 1 October 2016, names a second court, or binds one side to submit wherever the other sues. The overseas defendant resists.

Tell-tale facts In a hypo the clause is usually labelled "non-exclusive", or it is old, or it names two courts ("the courts of Utopia or the Singapore High Court"), or it makes one party submit wherever the other chooses to sue.

Write in this order
  1. ClauseA1, A4Says "non-exclusive": Singapore courts (A2, A7Asymmetric clause: one side may sue anywhere or A8Floating clause, or two courts named by wording)
  2. CCAA✗ B1, B4Outside the CCAA: say why, then the common law
  3. ServiceD4Defendant abroad: service out and the three requirements, D5Gateway: name the sub-limb of PD para 63(3), D8, D7Serious question to be tried
  4. ExerciseE1Which test, and who bears the burden, E7Strong cause: starter and nature of the test, E8Strong cause: the Eleftheria factors with the burden on the defendant
  5. SICCF1, F2, F3, then F4 and F5 (F6Transfer from the General Division: is the SICC "more appropriate"? if there is no primary jurisdiction)
  6. ASIG1, G2, G4Singapore non-exclusive clause: is suing abroad a breach?, then G7 without G6Vexation ASI: sufficient interest (G3Contractual ASI: a Singapore clause is breached if the clause is exclusive)

Adjust Claim out of time abroad but alive here: E8.5. Defendant served in Singapore: D1 or D2 in place of the Service step. The contract says how to serve: D9. A director or a tort claim outside the clause: archetype 6 for that defendant.

Often with 4 (a second contract naming a foreign court), 6 (a director or tort claim outside the clause), 7 (the defendant has a desk or agent here); then 9

Worked example opens in Notes mode

Contract Z: dated 2019, but it says "non-exclusive". Outside the CCAA. Singapore law governs and gives the clause a promise not to object. Archetype 2.

  1. A1 and A4: cl 17 says "non-exclusive". Under Singapore law, its governing law, "submit" means Rurico waived any objection to Singapore (Shanghai Turbo, Briggs).
  2. Cross B1 and write B4: the clause is expressly non-exclusive, so s 3(2) is displaced and the CCAA does not apply, despite the 2019 date.
  3. D4, D5, D8, D7: Rurico is abroad and cannot be served here, so service out under PD para 63(2).
  4. E1, E7, E8: Rurico's objection breaks its promise, so Rurico bears strong cause.
  5. F1 to F5: international and commercial; a non-exclusive clause is still a written jurisdiction agreement.
  6. G1, G2, G4, G7: suing in Ruritania is no breach of a non-exclusive clause, so the ASI rests on vexation.
Traps
  • Invoking s 3(2) for a clause the facts call "non-exclusive". Per Chong (seminar), that is a recurring exam error: the CCAA does not apply.

Remember Outside the CCAA, a Singapore clause with a promise puts strong cause on the defendant who objects, for service out and for a stay alike.

3

Singapore clause with no promise

Pattern No label, outside the CCAA; the contract's foreign governing law gives the clause no contractual effect.

Tell-tale facts In a hypo it is a Singapore clause with no label, outside the CCAA (usually because it is too old), in a contract governed by a foreign law, and the facts tell you that the foreign law treats the clause as a procedural device with no contractual effect.

Write in this order
  1. ClauseA1, A6
  2. CCAA✗ B1, B4
  3. ServiceD4, D5, D8, D7
  4. ExerciseE1, E3Forum conveniens on service out: burden on the claimant, E4Spiliada stage 1: connecting factors, E9The SICC as a Spiliada factor, E5Spiliada stage 2: substantial justice, E6Second bite: claimant fails stage 1 on service out
  5. SICCF1, ✗ F3, F6
  6. ASIG1, G2, ✗ G3, G6, G7

Adjust Same clause dated on or after 1 October 2016: archetype 1. Governing law does give the clause a promise, or is not pleaded or proved (K6): archetype 2.

Often with 1 (same clause, later date) and 5; then 9

Worked example opens in Notes mode

Contract X: dated 2015, before the CCAA date. Outside the CCAA. Ruritanian law gives the clause no promise. Archetype 3.

  1. A1 and A6: cl 15 has no label. Construed under Ruritanian law, it contains no promise, so Rurico breaches nothing by objecting (Chong's answer scheme).
  2. Cross B1 and write B4: concluded in 2015, before 1 October 2016, so outside the CCAA.
  3. D4, D5, D8, D7: Rurico is abroad, so service out. Name a gateway for each claim and plead a second in the alternative.
  4. E1, E3, E4, E9, E5, E6: plain Spiliada with the burden on Alpha. Stage 1: the factory, the witnesses and Ruritanian law point to Ruritania.
  5. F1, cross F3, F6: a pre-2016 High Court clause gives no primary jurisdiction.
  6. G1, G2, cross G3, G6, G7: no promise, so no contractual ASI. Singapore is unlikely to be the natural forum, and parallel proceedings alone are not vexatious, so the ASI probably fails.
Traps
  • Putting the burden on the claimant when the defendant is breaking a promise (archetype 2), or on the defendant when there is no promise (archetype 3).

Remember A Singapore clause with no promise is a factor, not a bargain. The claimant runs plain Spiliada and carries the burden.

4

Foreign court clause, claimant sues here anyway

Pattern Exclusive foreign clause, or the other side elected a foreign court under a floating clause.

Tell-tale facts In a hypo the claimant has signed a clause sending disputes to a foreign court, then sues in Singapore because the foreign court is slow, or far away, or because it thinks the other side has no real defence.

Write in this order
  1. ClauseA1, A3Says "exclusive": a foreign court (A8 if floating, A7 if one-sided)
  2. CCAAB1, then B3CCAA applies and a foreign court is chosen (s 12) (inside: stop there unless a s 12 exception applies) or B4 (outside)
  3. ServiceD4, D5, D10Foreign clause, defendant abroad, D7
  4. ExerciseE1, E7, E8 with the burden on the claimant

Adjust Slow chosen court: E8.3. "No real defence": E8.6. Clause said never to have been agreed: A10. Clause is only non-exclusive ("may be heard by"): A5, then archetype 6. Defendant served in Singapore: D1 or D2 in place of the Service step. The foreign court has already ruled or given judgment: archetype 5 or 10.

Often with 2 or 1 (a second contract with a Singapore clause); then 10 if the foreign court has given judgment

Worked example opens in Notes mode

Contract 1: an exclusive Utopian clause, and Alpha, the claimant, sues in Singapore. Archetype 4.

  1. A1 and A3: cl 20 says "exclusively to the courts of Utopia".
  2. B1, then B4: Utopia is not a Contracting State, so the temporal row fails and the CCAA does not apply.
  3. D4, D5, D10, D7: Beta is in Utopia with no office here, so Alpha needs approval to serve out.
  4. E1, E7, E8: strong cause is on Alpha. The six-year Utopian trial was foreseeable when Alpha signed (row E8.3, slow court fails).
Traps
  • Putting strong cause on the defendant in archetype 4 because the defendant brings the application. The claimant is in breach, so the claimant bears it.
  • Running a common law stay when the clause chooses a Contracting State's court within the CCAA. Under s 12 Singapore must stay or dismiss unless an exception applies.

Remember The claimant who sues here in breach of a foreign clause carries strong cause, even though the defendant brings the application. And inside the CCAA, s 12 stops the case before strong cause is reached.

5

Asymmetric clause + foreign HCCCA ruling

Pattern One side must sue in X, the other anywhere. X took the case. The client wants Singapore.

Tell-tale facts In a hypo the clause is one-sided: one party must sue only in the courts of one country, and the other may sue anywhere. The restricted party has sued in its permitted court.

Write in this order
  1. ClauseA1, A7
  2. Foreign rulingB5The foreign court has already ruled that the HCCCA applies, H8Issue estoppel from the foreign ruling (with H2Common law: the four requirements; on the merits, H3International jurisdiction: presence, H4International jurisdiction: submission, H5Final and conclusive, H7Defences to recognition and enforcement)
  3. If no estoppelB1, B4, then D4, D5, D8, D7, E1, E7, E8 and F1, F2, F4, F5
  4. If estoppelB3
  5. ASIG1, G2, G4, G3, G5Twist: the applicant's own ASI application breaks a clause

Adjust No foreign ruling yet: skip the two ruling steps. Judgment already given abroad: archetype 10. Client seeks a negative declaration: D6. Clause has no words of submission: Exercise step from archetype 6.

Often with 1 and 3 (the sister contracts); then 9 and 10 in the alternative

Worked example opens in Notes mode

Contract 2: a one-sided clause. Alpha is the free side, and the Ruritanian court has already ruled that the Hague Convention applies. Archetype 5.

  1. A1 and A7: cl 21 is one-sided. Rurico may sue only in Ruritania, and Alpha submits there.
  2. B5 and H8, with H2 to H5 and H7: the Ruritanian court ruled that the Convention applies.
  3. No estoppel: B1, B4. Singapore decides for itself. Cl 21 is asymmetric, so it is not exclusive under the CCAA (CHLOE V), and the common law decides.
  4. D4, D5, D8, D7: Rurico is abroad. The nexus is factor (r), because Rurico agreed to submit to any court in which Alpha sues.
  5. E1, E7, E8: Rurico agreed to submit, so if it objects, it bears strong cause.
  6. F1, F2, F4, F5: international and commercial; argue whether cl 21 is a written agreement to submit to the SICC.
  7. If the court finds an estoppel instead: B3. Singapore must stay or dismiss under s 12.
  8. G1, G2, G4, G3, G5: Rurico is amenable once served. Its suit in Ruritania breaches nothing, so G3 fails.
Traps
  • Treating a one-sided clause as inside the CCAA. Per CHLOE V it is not exclusive under the CCAA.
  • Forgetting the G5 twist: the free side's own anti-suit application may break its promise to submit abroad.

Remember First ask whether the foreign Hague ruling binds Singapore; probably not, because each Contracting State decides for itself. Then remember the twist: the free side's own anti-suit application may break its promise to submit abroad.

6

No clause binds this claim or party

Pattern No clause, a foreign non-exclusive clause, or a director or tort claim outside it. Defendant abroad.

Tell-tale facts In a hypo it is usually a director or employee sued personally in tort beside the company's contract claim, or a contract with no clause at all, or a clause naming a foreign court only non-exclusively.

Write in this order
  1. ClauseA9Is this claim, or this party, inside the clause? or A5Says "non-exclusive": a foreign court (✗ A1 if there is no clause)
  2. CCAA✗ B1, B4
  3. ServiceD4, D5, D7 (D6The Singapore claim is for a negative declaration if the claim is a negative declaration)
  4. ExerciseE1, E3, E4, E5, E6
  5. SICCF1, F6
  6. ASI if askedG1, G2, ✗ G3, G6, G7

Adjust Defendant served in Singapore: archetype 7 for service, and E2 in place of E3. Foreign suit on the same dispute already running: E4.14, E4.15 and E10. Second defendant joined to the claim against the first: D5.9. Bias or a state-linked opponent: E5.7.

Often with 2 (the co-defendant who is bound by the clause), 7 (try service here first); then 9

Worked example opens in Notes mode

Claim A: cl 18 binds Alpha and Rurico, not Mr Beta, and Mr Beta is abroad. Archetype 6.

  1. A9: cl 18 is in the contract between Alpha and Rurico.
  2. Cross B1, then B4: there is no choice of court agreement between Alpha and Mr Beta, so the CCAA does not apply.
  3. D4, D5, D7: Mr Beta lives in Ruritania and has never been to Singapore, so he cannot be served here.
  4. E1, E3: no clause is broken, so Spiliada, with the burden on Alpha because Mr Beta was served out.
  5. E4: the parties cancel out, Alpha in Singapore and Mr Beta in Ruritania.
  6. E5 and E6: if stage 1 points to Singapore, stage 2 does not arise.
  7. F1, F6: the action is international and commercial, but Mr Beta never agreed to the SICC, so the only route is transfer, if the SICC is more appropriate.
  8. Anti-suit, if asked: suppose Mr Beta sued Alpha in Ruritania for a declaration that he is not liable.
Traps
  • Holding a director to the company's clause. Privity comes first, and per Chong the clause cannot saddle a non-party with a burden.
  • Forgetting that a gateway is needed for each claim against each defendant.

Remember No clause binds this defendant, and he is abroad. Serve out through a gateway for each claim, then win Spiliada with the burden on the claimant.

7

Defendant served in Singapore asks for a stay

Pattern Unregistered foreign company with a shop, desk or agent here; or an officer or individual present.

Tell-tale facts In a hypo it is a foreign company that never registered in Singapore but has a shop, a desk, or an agent here; or a foreign company's officer who happens to be in Singapore; or an individual defendant who is present here.

Write in this order
  1. Clause✗ A1 (A9 or A5 if a clause exists but does not bind)
  2. ServiceD1Defendant can be served in Singapore, D2Foreign company never registered here, with an office or agent here
  3. ExerciseE1, E2Stay after service in Singapore: Spiliada starter, E4, E5
  4. SICC if askedF1, F6

Adjust Not present here: archetype 6. A clause governs the dispute: take the Exercise step from archetype 2 or 4. Stage 2 facts used so far: 5-year delay (E5.1), damages cap (E5.2), bias or danger (E5.7), cost (E5.11).

Often with 6 (if presence fails) and 2 (if a clause binds)

Worked example opens in Notes mode

Claim B: no clause in the film contract, and Gamma has a desk and an officer in Singapore. Archetype 7.

  1. Cross A1: the film contract has no jurisdiction clause, so no party is in breach by suing here or abroad.
  2. D1, D2: Gamma never registered, so the s 376 route is unavailable, and Alpha relies on s 16(1)(a)(i) and must prove presence.
  3. E1, E2: jurisdiction exists as of right, so Gamma can only seek a stay.
  4. E4: Alpha in Singapore and Gamma in Utopia cancel out.
  5. E5, if stage 1 goes to Gamma: the five-year Utopian trial is disregarded unless egregious (row E5.1), and the damages cap, a legitimate juridical advantage, is not conclusive on its own (row E5.2).
  6. SICC, if asked: no written jurisdiction agreement, so transfer only.
Traps
  • Skipping the threshold. Always state first whether the defendant can be served in Singapore.
  • Treating a foreign company's desk or agent as presence without the three limbs: a fixed place, more than a minimal period, and the company's own business.

Remember Prove presence limb by limb, serve as of right, and then the defendant who wants a stay carries stage 1.

8

ASI where Singapore is not the chosen court

Pattern Clause for a foreign court, suit in a third country, or a foreign ASI against the Singapore action.

Tell-tale facts In a hypo the client has a clause naming a foreign court. The other side sues somewhere else, in a third country. Or the other side goes to a foreign court and asks it to stop the client's Singapore action.

Write in this order
  1. ClauseA1, A3
  2. ASIG1, G2, G8Clause for a foreign court, or Singapore is neither forum
  3. HCCCA clauseG9
  4. VexationG6, G7

Adjust The other side asks a foreign court to stop the Singapore action: the anti-anti-suit pointer in G11 (G4 first only if there is a Singapore clause). Respondent is not a party to the clause: G11.

Often with 2 (a contract with a Singapore clause beside it); then 9

Worked example opens in Notes mode

Contract 3: the clause names Suburbia, the suit is in Utopia, and Alpha asks Singapore, a third court, to step in. Archetype 8.

  1. A1 and A3: cl 22 is an exclusive clause for Suburbia.
  2. G1, G2: Alpha seeks an anti-suit injunction against Gamma's Utopian action and names its basis.
  3. G8: the general answer is that Alpha should ask the Suburbian court, the chosen court, which it has not done.
  4. G9: Suburbia is a Contracting State and Utopia is not.
  5. G6, G7: Singapore is unlikely to be the natural forum of a dispute under a Suburbian-law contract with a Suburbian clause, and natural forum alone would not be enough anyway.
  6. Conclude both ways. Alpha's best points are the missing remedy in Suburbia and its Singapore assets.
Traps
  • In archetype 8, skipping the first answer: the applicant should ordinarily go to the chosen court, and Singapore should not be an international busybody.

Remember When the clause belongs to another court, the first answer is "go to that court". Singapore steps in only with a real interest of its own.

9

Foreign judgment outside the CCAA

Pattern Damages, declaration or default judgment from a court that was not the chosen court: enforce, or estoppel?

Tell-tale facts In a hypo it is a foreign court that took jurisdiction on its own rules, often a rule Singapore would not recognise, such as a bank account or a short visit, and then gave judgment.

Write in this order
  1. RegimeH1Which regime (✗ H1 if the court was not a chosen court under the HCCCA)
  2. RequirementsH2, H3, H4, H5
  3. KindH6Money judgment, or a declaration? (money: enforce; declaration: ✗ H6, then H8)
  4. DefencesH7
  5. EstoppelH8

Adjust Singapore has granted an ASI: H7.1. Foreign suit broke a clause: H7.3 to H7.5. No notice, or default after service: H7.7, H7.8. Conflicting Singapore judgment: H7.9. Old, part-paid or punitive award: H11. Debtor wants enforcement abroad stopped: H10.

Often with whichever of 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 or 6 has a foreign suit; 10 in the alternative

Worked example opens in Notes mode

Judgment A: there was no jurisdiction clause at all, so the Ruritanian court was not a chosen court. Archetype 9.

  1. Cross H1: there was no jurisdiction clause, so the Ruritanian court was not a chosen court under the Hague Convention, and CCAA Part 3 does not apply.
  2. H2: the judgment is on the merits. It decided Alpha's liability for the invoice, and a default judgment counts although Alpha did not take part in the trial.
  3. H3: Alpha was not present in Ruritania. It had no fixed place of business there.
  4. H4: Alpha did not submit. It appeared only to object to jurisdiction, lost and took no further part, which is what happened in Nimbus.
  5. H5: a default judgment can be final, and the open appeal period does not as such defeat finality, but Ruritanian law must be checked.
  6. H6: the judgment is for a fixed sum plus costs, so it would be enforceable as money if the other requirements held.
  7. H7: no defence needs to be run, because requirement (b) already fails.
  8. H8: nothing to decide here, since Rurico seeks enforcement.
Traps
  • Treating the foreign court's basis of jurisdiction, such as a bank account, as enough. Singapore's own conflict rules decide international jurisdiction, and residence is not even one of the four grounds.
  • Treating an objection to jurisdiction as submission. Objecting, losing and taking no further part is not submission (Nimbus).

Remember Singapore's rules decide international jurisdiction. Presence or submission, judged by Singapore's conflict rules, and an objection to jurisdiction is not submission.

    Flowchart Does a jurisdiction clause cover this claim and this defendant? No: no clause, or the claim or the party is outside it › Can the defendant be served in Singapore? No: abroad

  7. A Ruritanian company never registered in Singapore, but for two years it has rented an office here where its own staff sell its products. Alpha serves it at that office; it asks for a stay.

    Show the archetype

    7Defendant served in Singapore asks for a stay

    Served here as of right if presence is proved: the defendant applies for a stay and carries stage 1.

    Flowchart Does a jurisdiction clause cover this claim and this defendant? No: no clause, or the claim or the party is outside it › Can the defendant be served in Singapore? Yes: a shop, desk, agent, officer or individual here

  8. Alpha's contract with Gamma has an exclusive clause for the courts of Suburbia. Gamma sues Alpha in Utopia. Alpha asks the Singapore court to restrain the Utopian action.

    Show the archetype

    8ASI where Singapore is not the chosen court

    Singapore is not the chosen court: the first answer is "go to the Suburbian court".

    Flowchart Which court does the clause point to? A foreign court; or the suit is in a third country

  9. With no jurisdiction clause in play, a Ruritanian court took jurisdiction because the debtor had a bank account there, and gave a money judgment in default. The creditor wants to enforce it in Singapore.

    Show the archetype

    Not a chosen court under the Hague Convention: the common law requirements, then defences.

    Flowchart Did that court act as the chosen court under the HCCCA? No

  10. An exclusive clause names the courts of Utopia. The Utopian court, saying it is the chosen court under the Hague Convention, gave a default judgment declaring the contract void.

    Show the archetype

    10Judgment of a court acting under the HCCCA

    A judgment from a court acting as the chosen court: decide the Convention question for Singapore first.

    Flowchart Did that court act as the chosen court under the HCCCA? Yes, and it has given judgment

  12. No choice of law clause. The contract was signed at a trade fair in Singapore; the goods were delivered and paid for in Ruritania, in Ruritanian currency.

    Show the archetype

    12Valid express clause, or no clause

    No clause: look for an implied choice, then the objective proper law, led by where both parties perform.

    Flowchart Is there a choice of law clause? No clause

    13Ban passed after the contract

    A ban after the contract, at a place the contract requires performance: Ralli Bros.

    Flowchart What stands in the way of the contract? A ban passed after the contract was made

  14. From the start, the buyer meant to resell the goods in Utopia without the licence Utopian law requires. The seller did not know.

    Show the archetype

    14Plan to break a foreign law, known to one side

    Flowchart What stands in the way of the contract? A plan to break a foreign law: smuggling, tax evasion, no licence

    A side deal said to taint the contract sued on: the two tainting steps, step 2 under both laws.

    Flowchart What stands in the way of the contract? A second contract, or an illegal act, beside this one

    Flowchart Which tort? Negligence, or another tort
