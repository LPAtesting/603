Clause triage: what the jurisdiction clause promises, and the CCAA

⚡ BIG IDEA

Read the jurisdiction clause first and ask what it promises. That tells you who, if anyone, is in breach, and breach decides the test later (strong cause or Spiliada). If the Hague Convention applies through the CCAA, it overrides everything: the chosen court must hear the case and other courts must step aside.

J1.1 CHARACTERISING THE CLAUSE (write first: what [clause X] promises decides who, if anyone, is in breach)
  • Three Promises (must write) A choice of court agreement may contain (1A) a promise to submit to [the chosen court]; (1B) a promise not to object to its exercise of jurisdiction, e.g., on [FNC grounds]; and (2) a promise not to sue [anywhere else]. (1A) and (1B) are prorogation; (2) is derogation.
  • EJC v NEJC An EJC contains all three, so [claimant] suing in [a non-chosen court] breaches (2), and [defendant] objecting to [the chosen court] breaches (1B). Per Bambang Sutrisno (in Shanghai Turbo (CA)), a NEJC contains (1A) but not (2); whether it contains (1B) is construction under [its proper law].
  • How Exclusivity Is Decided Per Shanghai Turbo (CA), [clause X] is construed under [its governing law], but its effect on Singapore's jurisdiction is for [the lex fori]. Per C&Y, "exclusive" is the conclusion, not the starting point: labels get their ordinary meaning, but many clauses are [unlabelled / paired with an FNC waiver / asymmetric].
  • Wording Indicators In [clause X], ["exclusively"] → EJC; ["shall be heard by"] → mandatory, so likely exclusive under [the proper law]; ["may be referred to" / "submit to the non-exclusive jurisdiction of"] → NEJC.
  • SICC Clause If [clause X] names [the SICC], s 18F SCJA presumes it exclusive absent express contrary words, though whether s 18F applies where [foreign law] governs a non-CCAA clause is debated (J5.2.2).
J1.1.1 Singapore NEJC: the Contractual Approach (must write)
  • Rule Presently, [defendant] "submits to the non-exclusive jurisdiction of the Courts of Singapore" but now [seeks a stay / applies to set aside service] on [FNC grounds]. Per Shanghai Turbo (CA), adopting Noble Power, Singapore takes the contractual approach (left open in Orchard Capital), not the general approach under which [a NEJC] is merely a Spiliada factor.
  • Construction Under Singapore law, "submit" means consenting to [the named court]'s jurisdiction and waiving any objection to its exercise (Yeo's "narrow waiver of objection"). Otherwise [clause X] would be "devoid of purpose" (Briggs, adopted in Shanghai Turbo (CA)), and this holds even if [Singapore] had no jurisdiction without [clause X].
  • Consequence Any challenge by [defendant], whether [setting aside service] or [a stay], even in favour of [another named court], is an attempt to escape [clause X], so [defendant] must show strong cause (J4.3).
  • Old Cases Overruled Per Shanghai Turbo (CA), High Court cases treating [a Singapore NEJC] as a mere Spiliada factor were wrongly decided. The CA went "one step further" than Bambang Sutrisno, whose clause had [an express FNC waiver].
  • Open Per Shanghai Turbo (CA), whether the same applies to [a NEJC] in [a standard form / non-negotiated contract] was left open; by analogy with Vinmar, probably yes (J4.3).
J1.1.2 Proper Law and Foreign NEJCs
  • Proper Law of the NEJC (±) Whether [clause X] contains (1B) is for [the proper law]. If [law X] is unproved, the presumption of similarity reads in the same waiver. If [Ruritanian law] is proved and implies no waiver, [clause X] contains only (1A), so [defendant]'s FNC objection is no breach and Spiliada applies (J4.1; J4.2).
  • Foreign NEJC: No Breach Either Way [Claimant] sues here though [clause X] names [the courts of X] non-exclusively. Per Shanghai Turbo (CA), the "wide waiver of objection" is rejected, so suing here is no breach, and [defendant]'s objection is none either, as [Singapore] is not named. Plain Spiliada applies (J4.1).
  • Weight of a Foreign NEJC Per Shanghai Turbo (CA), [clause X] shows only that [X] is an appropriate forum, not clearly more appropriate, so it carries little weight; how much depends on whether it was [closely negotiated / chosen for neutrality].
  • Drafting Around the Defaults (practice) For [a Singapore NEJC] without the waiver, add ["without prejudice to each party's right to object on FNC grounds"]; to make [a foreign NEJC] bind, add [a promise not to sue elsewhere].
  • FNC Waiver Per Bunge, where [the Singapore EJC] carries [an FNC waiver, e.g., "no Party will argue to the contrary"], grounds founded on convenience cannot amount to strong cause, so [defendant]'s reliance on [witnesses / documents abroad] fails.
J1.1.3 Floating and Multi-Forum Clauses
  • Floating Jurisdiction Clause Valid Per Shanghai Turbo (CA) and C&Y, a floating jurisdiction clause is valid at common law if not too uncertain, unlike [a floating choice of law clause], which is void (C2). It needs an election mechanism, e.g., [one party's option / a prescribed event].
  • Severance Presently, [clause X] couples [a floating choice of law] with [a jurisdiction choice]. Per Shanghai Turbo (CA), the void law part is severable where the two are joined only by ["and"] and [the contract has a severance clause]; contrast The Iran Vojdan, where [the forum choice] was parasitic on [the law choice].
  • No Mechanism → Both Courts Per Shanghai Turbo (CA), ["Singapore/or PRC"] with no election mechanism is a NEJC to both [Singapore] and [PRC], each party submitting to whichever is seised. Letting [the claimant] elect at suit was rejected as encouraging a race to sue.
  • Several Named Fora Per Shanghai Turbo (CA), the waiver applies to each named forum, so [defendant]'s objection in either [Singapore] or [X] is a breach.
J1.1.4 Clause Bank (handout exercise)
  • Foreign EJC → Strong Cause on Claimant ["exclusively to the courts of [X]"], ["exclusively to the courts of [X] or [Y]"] (two fora, all others excluded) and ["shall be heard by the courts of [X]"] (mandatory, so likely exclusive). [Claimant] suing here breaches, so [claimant] bears strong cause, whether [served in, defendant seeking a stay] or [served out] (J3.2).
  • Foreign NEJC → Spiliada ["may be heard by the courts of [X]"] or ["the non-exclusive jurisdiction of the courts of [X]"]: no one breaches by suing here, so Spiliada, with the burden set by [service in / out] and [set-aside / stay] (J3.2; J4).
  • Singapore NEJC → Strong Cause on Defendant ["the non-exclusive jurisdiction of the courts of Singapore"] or ["of the courts of [X] or Singapore"] (the Shanghai Turbo pattern): [defendant] objecting to [Singapore] breaches, so [defendant] bears strong cause.
  • Asymmetric, With Submission ["[A] may sue [B] in any court, [B] submitting to it; [B] may sue [A] only in [Y]"]. If [A] sues here, [B] agreed to submit, so [B] bears strong cause to stay; if [B] sues here, [B] breaches its [Y]-only promise and bears strong cause.
  • Asymmetric, Without Submission (±) If [clause X] merely lets [A] sue [B] [anywhere], without [B] submitting to that court, [A] suing here is permitted but [B] never agreed to submit, so [B]'s stay goes to Spiliada (Week 10 handout). [B] suing here still breaches [the EJC for Y] → strong cause on [B].
  • Protective Promise ["all submit to [X]; under no circumstances shall [B] be subjected to any other court"]. [A] suing [B] here breaches the second limb → strong cause on [A]; [B] suing [A] here breaches nothing → Spiliada.
J1.2 DOES THE CCAA APPLY? (s 8)

(Three principles: [the chosen court] must hear; [a non-chosen court] must decline; [its judgment] must be recognised (J7.5). Gateways: exclusive; one Contracting State; concluded after entry into force; international; civil or commercial. Write only those in issue.)

J1.2.1 Exclusive Choice of Court Agreement (s 3)
  • Express EJC (+) Per s 3(1), [clause X] is exclusive: concluded [in writing / by accessible electronic means], it designates [the courts of State] for disputes in connection with [contract], to the exclusion of any other court.
  • Deemed Exclusive (+) Per s 3(2), [clause X] (["the parties submit to the jurisdiction of the [State] courts"]) is deemed exclusive unless the parties expressly provide otherwise. At common law the same unlabelled clause could go either way under [its proper law] (J1.1).
    • Rebuttal (−): [clause X] [says "non-exclusive" / allows suit "in any other competent court"], so s 3(2) is displaced and [the CCAA] does not apply.
    • Trap: per Chong (seminar), where the facts call [clause X] [non-exclusive], do not invoke s 3(2): [the CCAA] does not apply, a recurring exam error.
  • Asymmetric Clause (−) Presently, [clause X] gives [State B] exclusive jurisdiction but lets [the lender] sue [the borrower] [anywhere]. Per CHLOE V, following the Hartley–Dogauchi Report (VCLT art 32), such a clause is not exclusive under the CCAA, so s 12 cannot be engaged even on [the court's own motion], and even though [defendant] conceded the point.
    • Support: Per Etihad (EWCA) (obiter), the Convention probably excludes [asymmetric clauses] like [clause X], the Diplomatic Session having rejected a Swiss proposal to cover clauses exclusive "for some or all of the parties".
    • Counter (+): Earlier first-instance dicta (Commerzbank v Liquimar; Etihad (EWHC)) read the Convention's wording as wide enough for [clause X], but [the lender]'s reliance on them is weak after CHLOE V.
    • Consequence: At common law [clause X] stays exclusive against [the restricted party] (per C&Y), so J1.1 applies.
  • Floating Clause (±) Presently, [clause X] lets [party] choose between [Court A] and [Court B]; per C&Y, CCAA coverage is unclear. (−) Before election there is no chosen court, so arguably no s 3(1) agreement. (+) Once [notice of election] was served on [date], [clause X] crystallised into a mutually exclusive choice of [Court A], probably within the CCAA.
    • Unilateral election (±): s 3 requires agreement between the parties, but arguably [party]'s election suffices where [all parties agreed it could elect / [other party] received written notice and did not object].
  • More Than One State (−) [Clause X] designates [Singapore or England], not the courts "of one Contracting State" (s 3(1)), so it is outside the CCAA, though [possibly exclusive at common law].
  • Separability (+) Per s 5, [clause X] is independent of [contract], so its validity cannot be challenged solely because [contract] is said to be [void for misrepresentation / terminated].
J1.2.2 Contracting State and Date
  • Temporal Scope [State of chosen court] is a Contracting State, and [clause X] was concluded on [date], after the HCCCA entered into force for it (Singapore: 1 October 2016). [Check the HCCH status table.]
    • UK clauses (±): the HCCCA applied to the UK via the EU from 1 October 2015 and in its own right from 1 January 2021; continuity across Brexit is contested, so [an English clause] concluded on [a date between them] is arguable either way.
J1.2.3 International Case (s 4)
  • International (+) Per s 4(1), [the case] is international unless the parties reside in the same Contracting State and everything else, apart from the chosen court's location, is connected only with that State. [Defendant] resides in [State], so the exception fails.
  • Company Residence Per s 6, [a company party] resides at its [statutory seat / place of incorporation / central administration / principal place of business], so [defendant] resides in [State].
  • Wholly Domestic (−) Both parties reside in [Singapore], and [contract] was negotiated and performed wholly here; only [the chosen court in State B] is foreign. So [the case] is not international and the CCAA does not apply (per C&Y).
J1.2.4 Civil or Commercial Matter (ss 9–10)
  • Civil or Commercial (+) Per C&Y, "civil or commercial" is autonomous, mainly excluding public and criminal law. [A claim for price] under [supply contract] is commercial, and insurance is not excluded merely by its subject matter (s 9(6)).
  • Excluded (−) Per s 9(1), the CCAA excludes [consumer] and [employment / collective] agreements; per s 9(2), matters such as [insolvency / carriage of goods / competition / personal injury / non-contractual property damage / rights in rem in land / IP validity]; per s 9(3), arbitration.
    • IP (±): non-contractual infringement of [patent / trade mark] (not copyright) is excluded unless it could have been brought as a breach of [contract] between the parties (s 9(2)).
    • Preliminary question (+): per s 9(5), [IP validity] raised only as a preliminary question or [defence] does not exclude the case; per s 9(7), [a State] being a party does not either.
  • Interim Measures (+) Per s 10, interim measures are outside the CCAA, so Singapore may still grant [a Mareva / an interim ASI] whatever [the chosen court] (J6.5.3).
J1.2.5 Validity, Existence and Capacity
  • Validity Presently, [party] says [clause X] is invalid for [misrepresentation / duress]. Per ss 11(1) and 12(1)(a) and the Hartley–Dogauchi Report, validity is for the law of [the chosen court's State], including its choice of law rules, a single reference point that deters forum shopping.
    • Example: if [Singapore] is chosen and [contract] is governed by [French law], Singapore's choice of law rules point to French law; even then, per s 5, [clause X] survives unless attacked directly.
  • Existence (Consent) The CCAA is silent, but per the Hartley–Dogauchi Report (and C&Y), whether [party] consented to [clause X] is also for the law of [the chosen court], avoiding a validity and existence split.
  • Capacity If [the chosen court's law] treats capacity as validity or existence, it governs. As a non-chosen court, [Singapore] may also refuse a stay if [party] lacked capacity under Singapore law (s 12(1)(b)), also a ground to refuse recognition (s 15(1)(b); J7.5).
J1.3 SINGAPORE IS THE CHOSEN COURT (s 11)
  • Must Hear (must write) [Clause X] designates [the Singapore High Court / the SICC] and the CCAA applies (J1.2). Per s 11(1), [Singapore] must not decline unless [clause X] is null and void under Singapore law, including its choice of law rules.
  • No FNC, No Lis Pendens Per s 11(2), [Singapore] cannot decline in favour of [State B], so neither Spiliada nor strong cause applies, and per C&Y no other s 11(3) ground has been prescribed. [Defendant]'s points that [witnesses / governing law] point to [State B], or that proceedings are pending there, are irrelevant.
  • Null and Void (−) [Defendant] argues [clause X] is null and void because [it was procured by fraud / [defendant]'s agent lacked authority]. That is for Singapore law including its choice of law rules (s 11(1)), and per s 5 the alleged invalidity of [contract] does not by itself affect [clause X].
  • Internal Allocation (±) Per s 11(4), Singapore's [subject-matter / value / internal allocation] rules are unaffected. But per s 11(5), on a discretionary transfer of [the case] to [another Singapore court], the court must take the parties' choice into account; otherwise, per art 8(5) HCCCA, [a foreign court] may refuse recognition against [a party] that objected in time (J5.4.2).
  • Serving Abroad: General Division (+) [Claimant] seeks approval on the "has the jurisdiction" ground (O 8 r 1(1) ROC 2021), created partly for CCAA cases, so [no nexus] and [no forum conveniens] inquiry arises (per C&Y; J3.1.1). If [contract] permits service out, no approval is needed (O 8 r 1(3); J3.1.5).
    • Open (±): whether [defendant] may still argue [the claim] is outside the CCAA, and so resist "has the jurisdiction", should be raised inter partes (J3.1.1).
  • Serving Abroad: SICC (+) No permission is needed to serve [defendant], [a party to the written jurisdiction agreement] (O 5 r 6(2)(a) SICC Rules; J5.2.4).
J1.4 SINGAPORE IS NOT THE CHOSEN COURT (s 12)
  • Must Stay or Dismiss (must write) [Claimant] sued here, and [defendant] seeks a stay because [clause X] designates [the courts of State B]. Per s 12(1), "despite any other written law or rule of law", [Singapore] must stay or dismiss unless exception (a)–(e) applies; per s 12(2), a stay on other grounds remains possible.
  • Two Stages Per 6DM, the court asks (1) whether there is an exclusive agreement not designating [a Singapore court] for these proceedings, then (2) whether [an s 12(1) exception] applies.
  • Standard Per 6DM, [defendant] must show a good arguable case at stage 1, not the arbitration prima facie standard, because the CCAA has [no kompetenz-kompetenz equivalent] and [no supervisory appeal structure], and a good arguable case matches the robust common law stay analysis.
J1.4.1 Stage 1: An EJC That Does Not Designate Singapore
  • Ambiguous Clause (+) [Clause X] refers to ["the local courts" / "the courts of the Seller's country"]. Similar to 6DM, where "the local courts" in an English-law distribution agreement meant England because it renewed an earlier English-clause agreement on a related party's template, [clause X] means [State B] given [prior dealings / the template / the governing law].
  • Clause Covers Singapore or Is Outside the CCAA (−) [Claimant] answers that [clause X] [designates Singapore / is asymmetric / is non-exclusive]. Then s 12 is not engaged (CHLOE V), and [the stay] is decided at common law: [strong cause for an EJC / Spiliada for a foreign NEJC] (J4).
J1.4.2 Stage 2: The Exceptions (s 12(1)(a)–(e))
  • Starter Per the Hartley–Dogauchi Report (and C&Y), exceptions (c) and (d) apply "only in the most exceptional circumstances" and are read narrowly. If [an exception] applies, [Singapore] may exercise jurisdiction under its own law, and [defendant]'s stay falls to the common law (J4).
  • (a) Null and Void (±) [Claimant] argues [clause X] is null and void for [ground] under [State B]'s law, including its choice of law rules, not Singapore law. [Claimant] must prove [State B law] as fact, or the presumption of similarity applies (C1.6.3); by analogy, [the s 12(1)(c) argument] in 6DM failed for want of evidence of English law.
  • (b) Incapacity (+) [Claimant] lacked capacity to conclude [clause X] under Singapore law, including its choice of law rules, because of [e.g., the company's constitution] (s 12(1)(b)).
  • (c) Manifest Injustice: Rule (−) Per 6DM, adopting the Hartley–Dogauchi Report, manifest injustice covers, e.g., [no fair trial for bias or corruption in State B], [party-specific reasons precluding suit there] or [an agreement procured by fraud]. Per Brand & Herrup (in 6DM), it must be clear, extremely serious and highly probable, not speculative.
  • (c) Public Policy: Rule (−) Per 6DM, public policy means [basic norms], not [the chosen court's technical departure from a forum mandatory rule], and [clause X] is not disregarded merely because it would not bind under [Singapore domestic law].
  • (c) Fragmentation (−) Similar to 6DM, where the risk that a related individual could not be joined in England failed s 12(1)(c), [claimant]'s argument that [third party] cannot be joined in [State B] fails. The test is "very different" from, and far higher than, fragmentation as strong cause at common law (Vinmar), and [claimant] led no evidence of [State B law].
  • (c) Domestic Policy (−) Similar to Motacus (in 6DM), where England's policy of fast construction adjudication did not justify ignoring a Paris EJC, absent any evidence that enforcement could not proceed in Paris, [claimant]'s reliance on [Singapore policy on X] fails. Parliament could have made [an art 21 declaration].
  • (c) Exceptional Case (+) [Claimant] leads cogent evidence that [State B's courts are not independent / [claimant] faces arrest there / [clause X] was procured by fraud], the kind of case the Hartley–Dogauchi Report contemplates.
  • (d) Cannot Reasonably Be Performed (±) Per s 12(1)(d), this needs exceptional reasons beyond the parties' control, arguably akin to frustration. (+) [War in State B / its courts ceasing to function] means [the case] cannot be heard there. (−) Mere [delay / expense] is not enough.
  • (e) Chosen Court Declined (+) [The State B court] has [declined jurisdiction / dismissed [claimant]'s claim on jurisdictional grounds], so [Singapore] may hear [the case] under its own rules without breaching the Convention.
  • Case Management (−) Per 6DM, any risk of [inconsistent outcomes with related proceedings] can be met by a limited case-management stay of [those proceedings], especially at an early stage (J4.5).
  • Conclusion [No exception applies, so [the action] is [stayed / dismissed] under s 12(1) / exception [(x)] applies, so [defendant]'s stay is decided at common law (J4.1)].
J1.5 SCOPE OF THE AGREEMENT (write before strong cause: if [the claim] or [the party] is outside [clause X], ordinary Spiliada applies instead (J4.1 / J4.2))
  • Governing Law Per C&Y, subject-matter and party scope are for [the proper law of clause X], not the lex fori. If [law X] is unproved, Singapore construction applies by the presumption of similarity, with [contra proferentem] as a fallback where [the governing law] recognises it.
J1.5.1 Subject-Matter Scope
  • Broad Construction (+) [Claimant] argues [the tort / statutory claim] is outside [clause X]. Per Fiona Trust, applied to jurisdiction clauses in Trisuryo and Bunge, rational businessmen are presumed to want all disputes arising out of [their relationship] decided in one forum unless [the words] clearly exclude them.
  • Reframing Rarely Escapes (+) Per The Jian He (and Rickshaw), [claimant] may frame its claim in [tort / equity], but reframing [a contractual dispute] rarely escapes a widely drafted [clause X].
  • Separate Agreement Within Scope (+) Similar to Trisuryo, where a trust claim resting on a separate oral agreement fell within the Indonesian EJC because it contradicted the deeds' warranty that the shares were free of others' rights, [claimant]'s claim under [side agreement] falls within [clause X] since it [contradicts / turns on] [a term of contract].
  • No Presumption at the Outset (−) Per COSCO (CA), the inquiry does not start from a presumption that all competing claims go to one forum; it depends on [the nature of the claims] and [clause X's express words], "forum fragmentation [being] a fact of life". Where [the claim] falls outside, fragmentation is accepted (Asiana).
  • Tests Are Tools (±) Per COSCO (CA), the causative-connection, closely-knitted and parallel-claims tests are tools, not formulae. A [tort claim] that can be recast in contract plainly falls within, and so may one met by [contractual cross-claims] from the same incident (The Angelic Grace), e.g., [an allision during cargo loading].
  • Jurisdiction Clauses Too Though COSCO (CA) concerned [an arbitration clause], scope is construed the same way for [an EJC] (Fiona Trust; per C&Y), so [defendant] may rely on it for [the tort claim].
  • Global Partners (+, persuasive) Per Global Partners (NSWCA), "in connection with" in [clause X] cannot be read down, and an EJC is construed as liberally as [an arbitration clause], securing one coherent forum for [all disputes from the relationship]. 1Start here: J1 in plain English

Almost every Question 1 contract contains a jurisdiction clause: a term saying which court should hear disputes. J1 is the file you open first for every contract. It does three jobs:

  1. Read the clause and work out exactly what each party promised.
  2. Check whether the Hague Convention applies (through Singapore's CCAA). If it does, it overrides the ordinary rules.
  3. Check the clause's reach: does it cover this claim and these parties, and does it validly exist?

Everything later in Question 1 depends on the answer. If someone broke the clause, the later test is strong cause (J4.3). If nobody broke it, the test is Spiliada (J4.1).

★ IN PLAIN ENGLISH

A jurisdiction clause is like agreeing in advance where a match will be played. "We play at your home ground" is one promise. "And I won't complain that your ground is too far away" is a second. "And I won't try to play this match anywhere else" is a third. Whether someone has broken their word depends on which of these promises they actually made. J1 is about reading the agreement carefully before you accuse anyone.

✎ WORKED EXAMPLE
Why the clause comes first: one dispute, two clauses

Alpha (Singapore) sells machines to Delta (Hong Kong). Delta refuses to pay. Alpha sues in Singapore. Delta asks the Singapore court to stop the case and send it to Hong Kong.

  • Version 1: the contract says "the courts of Hong Kong shall have exclusive jurisdiction". Alpha promised not to sue anywhere except Hong Kong, and it has sued in Singapore. Alpha is in breach, so Alpha must show strong cause to keep the case here. That is hard.
  • Version 2: the contract says "the courts of Hong Kong may hear disputes". Alpha promised nothing about Singapore. No one is in breach, so the court runs Spiliada, and Delta (if served here) must show Hong Kong is clearly more appropriate.

Same parties, same goods, same courts. The only change is one word in the clause, and it flips who carries the burden. That is why J1 is the first file you open.

  • Under a foreign NEJC, suing in Singapore breaks nothing (no promise (2)), and the defendant objecting in Singapore breaks nothing either (Singapore is not the named court).
★ IN PLAIN ENGLISH

Think of three separate signatures on the clause. An EJC has all three. A non-exclusive clause has the first, maybe the second, never the third. You can only accuse someone of breaking a promise they actually signed.

A memory trick for the two Latin-ish words: pro-rogation points towards the chosen court ("yes, this court"); de-rogation points away from every other court ("not there").

✎ WORKED EXAMPLE
The three promises in action

Clause: "The courts of Singapore shall have exclusive jurisdiction." Alpha (Singapore) and Delta (Hong Kong) fall out.

  1. Delta sues Alpha in Hong Kong. Delta broke promise (2): it sued in a court other than the chosen one. Consequence in Singapore: Alpha can seek an ASI on the contractual basis (J6.3.1).
  2. Alpha sues Delta in Singapore, and Delta applies for a stay saying Hong Kong is more convenient. Delta broke promise (1B): it objected to the chosen court. Consequence: Delta must show strong cause (J4.3).
  3. Alpha sues Delta in Singapore and Delta simply defends. Delta has kept promise (1A). No breach by anyone.

Now change the clause to "the courts of Singapore may hear disputes" (a Singapore NEJC under Singapore law). Step 1 is no longer a breach: there is no promise (2). Step 2 is still a breach: per Shanghai Turbo, a Singapore NEJC carries promise (1B). Step 3 is unchanged.

Per C&Y, "exclusive" is the conclusion, not the starting point: labels get their ordinary meaning, but many clauses are unlabelled, paired with an FNC waiver, or asymmetric.

An unlabelled clause

Clause: "All disputes shall be submitted to the courts of Japan." The contract is governed by Japanese law. Is it exclusive?

  1. Which law construes it? Its governing law, Japanese law (Shanghai Turbo (CA)).
  2. Is Japanese law proved? If an expert gives evidence on how Japanese law reads "shall be submitted", follow it.
  3. If not proved, the presumption of similarity applies: the court reads it as Singapore law would. "Shall" is mandatory language, so it is likely exclusive.
  4. What follows is for Singapore law (the lex fori): if exclusive, a claimant suing in Singapore is in breach and must show strong cause.

A foreign NEJC: nobody is in breach

A foreign NEJC is like a restaurant voucher that says "valid at Restaurant X". It tells you X will serve you. It does not stop you eating somewhere else, and it does not prove X is the best restaurant in town.

✎ WORKED EXAMPLE
"Singapore or PRC"

Why "international" is defined backwards

The CCAA does not list what counts as international. It describes the one case that is not: everyone and everything sits in one State, and the only foreign element is the court the parties picked. ⚠ TRAP
Choice of court ≠ choice of law

A choice of court clause is a promise (so breach can found an ASI and damages). A choice of law clause is usually only a declaration of which law governs (Ace Insurance v Moose), so suing in a court that will apply another law is not a breach.

✎ WORKED EXAMPLE
Everything a broken Singapore EJC can do

Delta sues Alpha in Ruritania despite a Singapore EJC. Alpha spends $200,000 defending there. What can Alpha do in Singapore?

  1. Stop it: seek an ASI on the contractual basis. Alpha need not show vexation; Delta must show strong reasons why the ASI should not issue (J6.3.1).
  2. Claim damages: the cost of defending abroad. In Singapore damages "can", not "must", be awarded (Tradewaves): Alpha should normally seek its costs in Ruritania, and must prove the "unusual features" of Union Discount v Zoller to recover them here.
  3. Resist the Ruritanian judgment if Delta wins and tries to enforce it in Singapore: arguably a recognition defence (C&Y), as long as Alpha did not submit in Ruritania (J7.2.6).

Contrast: if the contract had only said "governed by Singapore law" with no court clause, Delta suing in Ruritania would break no promise at all (Ace Insurance v Moose).
