Existence when the defendant is abroad: service out

⚡ BIG IDEA

A defendant outside Singapore can be brought in only through O 8: without approval if the contract allows service out, otherwise with approval on the "appropriate court" ground, where the claimant must show (a) a good arguable case of nexus through a para 63(3) factor, (b) Singapore is forum conveniens, and (c) a serious question to be tried.

J3.0.2 Threshold: Service In or Service Out? (s 16(1)(a)(i) v (ii); O 7 v O 8)

([defendant] abroad but served here, or [claimant] relies on O 7 r 2(1)(b) or r 4 against [a foreign company]; the forward-link from J2.1.5)

  • The Text Per s 16(1)(a)(i) SCJA, jurisdiction is founded on service "in Singapore in the manner prescribed"; per (ii), on service "outside Singapore in the circumstances authorised by and in the manner prescribed". So the limbs divide either by where [service occurs] or by where [defendant] is.
    • Rules: O 7 is headed "Service in Singapore" and O 8 "Service Out", which [claimant] will cite for the first reading and [defendant] will call a mere [procedural label].
  • Why It Matters (must write) The answer decides (i) whether [claimant] must prove Adams v Cape presence of [the foreign company] to use O 7 r 2(1)(b) (officer) or O 7 r 4 (agent) (J2.1.5); and (ii) whether [claimant] can reach [defendant] abroad without the O 8 controls.
  • COSCO: Where Service Occurs Per COSCO, [the foreign company] that agreed to be served through [its Singapore solicitors] was served in Singapore under O 7 r 2(1)(d) ("in any manner agreed"), founding jurisdiction under s 16(1)(a)(i).
    • Reasons: what counts is where service is effected, not whether [defendant] is Singapore-based; nothing in O 7 confines it to local defendants; requiring O 8 approval only to then serve [solicitors] here wastes court time (the Ideals); Shanghai Turbo and Rothschild confirm it.
    • Note: the judge accepted that [the foreign company] could equally be served under O 7 r 2(1)(b) on [its chairman] while he is here — the step the critics attack.
  • Yeo: Where the Defendant Is Per Yeo (YPH Lecture), limb (i) asks only for the manner of service because [defendant]'s presence is the nexus, whereas (ii) adds "circumstances authorised", delegating [the nexus] to the Rules. So the limbs divide by where [defendant] is.
    • If COSCO were right: [the foreign company] could be caught by serving [its officer] on holiday or in transit (criticising B Atmaram v Essa Industries: a Pakistani company's chairman served on a visit).
    • Further: substituted service here could reach [an absent defendant] without O 8, contrary to Consistel, and [claimant] could even apply to dispense with service altogether.
    • Solicitors: per Yeo, service on [solicitors] instructed to accept it is submission (s 16(1)(b)); confine COSCO to that reasoning. It matters because submission has a scope: [defendant] who reserved [its objections] can still put the O 8 requirements in issue.
    • O 7 r 4: per Yeo, it addresses [absent principals], supplies its own nexus ([agent]'s control of the affairs the action relates to) and needs permission, so it fits limb (ii); the Rules' in/out division need not match the SCJA's.
    • No nexus, no (ii): per Yeo, substituted service and service on [officers of foreign companies] set out no nexus, so cannot rest on (ii) and cannot reach [an absent defendant] at all.
    • Policy: per Yeo, the 1993 amendments confirmed the pre-1963 structure (territorial service as of right, extraterritorial service on connecting factors, submission); reading (i) to cover [absent defendants] "sends the wrong signal" about [Singapore]'s reach.
  • Beranek: Constructive Presence Per Beranek, the limbs divide by whether [defendant] is present, actually or constructively, when [served].
    • COSCO reached the right result for the wrong reason: [the foreign company] was constructively present through [solicitors authorised to accept service].
    • O 7 r 4 rests on limb (i): [the principal] is deemed present through [agent]. The rule asks about [agent]'s authority, not whether Singapore is appropriate; were it service out, it would sit in O 8.
    • O 7 r 2(1)(b) requires [the company itself] to be present, not merely [its officer].
  • Lecturer's Position (submit) The limbs divide by where [defendant] is. Authorising [Singapore solicitors] to accept service is submission (s 16(1)(b)), a waiver of the O 8 hoops, not "deemed presence": [the solicitors] do not carry on [defendant]'s business here (Adams v Cape).
    • O 7 r 2(1)(b) and r 4: per Chong (mid-term feedback), in line with Beranek, both arguably rest on limb (i), not (ii) (cf COSCO), so [the company] must be present and [the officer or agent] is merely the mode.
    • Rosenberg: [agent] must have power to contract, so [company] trades "by" and not merely "through" him — a strong sign [company] is present (J2.1.5). Per the slides, r 4 also reaches [a natural person] principal (J2.1.2).
    • Anomaly: per Chong (citing Toh Kian Sing), if [agent]'s control means [company] is present, how is it "absent" under O 7 r 4(a)? Unresolved; flag it in one line.
  • The Chairperson-on-Holiday Problem Presently [the dispute] is wholly foreign ([contract made and goods delivered in X]) and [the chairperson] is merely on holiday here. Per Chong (mid-term feedback), the defendant is [the company], not [the chairperson].
    • So: serving [him] under O 7 r 2(1)(b) founds jurisdiction only if [the company] is itself present (Adams v Cape; J2.1.5); Maharanee of Baroda (transient presence) is for [a natural person defendant].
    • His presence: only the practical precondition for handing [him] the papers. Without [company] presence, this is service on [an absent company], which belongs in O 8 (Week 6 slides).
    • Alternative: if [he] is here doing [the company]'s business, use O 7 r 4, meeting all of r 4(1), including control of the affairs [the action] relates to.
    • Bottom line: otherwise [claimant] could skip both Adams v Cape and O 8 by serving [the CEO] on holiday.
  • Which Route Is Most Onerous (practice) Per the slides, O 7 r 2(1)(b) is as of right, O 7 r 4 needs permission (not as of right), and O 8 r 1(1) "appropriate court" needs approval on three requirements, burden on [claimant]. So advise [claimant] to serve [defendant] in wherever it can (J2.1.7).
J3.1 EXISTENCE OF JURISDICTION: SERVICE OUT (s 16(1)(a)(ii) SCJA; O 8 ROC 2021)
J3.1.1 Starter

Presently, [defendant] is in [X] and cannot be served here (J3.0.2). Per s 16(1)(a)(ii) SCJA, jurisdiction exists where [defendant] is served outside Singapore "in the circumstances authorised by and in the manner prescribed by" the ROC: jurisdiction with approval, not as of right.

  • Approval Per O 8 r 1(1), [the originating process] may be served out on [defendant] with approval where the court "has the jurisdiction" or "is the appropriate court".
  • Application Per O 8 r 1(2) and PD para 63(1), (4), [claimant] applies by summons without notice, with an affidavit stating why, where [defendant] is or may be found, and whether [the process]'s validity needs extending.
  • No Approval Needed Per O 8 r 1(3), none where [the contract] allows service out (J3.1.5); per O 8 r 1(4), approval for the originating process carries [later court documents] with it.
  • Route 1: "Has the Jurisdiction" Per C&Y, this presumably covers [an exclusive choice of court agreement] designating Singapore under the CCAA, where the chosen court must hear [the case] and cannot decline for forum non conveniens (CCAA s 11; J1.3). Per Chong (answer scheme), [the CCAA contract] goes on this ground.
    • Why (Yeo): read with s 16(1)(a)(ii), the limb is circular, so "has the jurisdiction" must mean jurisdiction under other written law (s 16(2) SCJA), i.e., [the General Division] as exclusively chosen court in [a Hague case].
    • Puzzle: per C&Y, if [the court] already "has" jurisdiction, it is unclear why approval is needed or on what criteria [the AR] decides.
    • Other chosen-court cases: per Yeo, where [the General Division] is chosen outside the CCAA, use "appropriate court", easily met since [defendant] must show strong cause (J3.2).
    • Agreement ≠ jurisdiction: per Yeo (citing British Wagon v Gray), [consent] alone confers no jurisdiction; an agreement to submit is not submission under s 16(1)(b) but a nexus for service out on [defendant].
    • Exercise challenge: per C&Y, outside the CCAA [defendant] may still challenge exercise (forum non conveniens, or strong cause for [a non-CCAA EJC]), the burden being on [defendant], as in a service-in case (J4).
    • SICC: per SICC Rules O 5 r 6(2), no permission is needed to serve [a party to a written jurisdiction agreement], or where [the agreement] allows service out; otherwise per O 5 r 6(1) the SICC must "have the jurisdiction" and be "appropriate" (J5).
  • Route 2: "Appropriate Court" Per PD para 63(2), restating Zoom's common law requirements, [claimant] shows (a) a good arguable case of sufficient nexus; (b) Singapore is forum conveniens; (c) a serious question to be tried against [defendant].
    • Together: per Recovery Vehicle 1, the three are assessed for consistency, not in isolation, so a finding on [the gateway] must square with [the merits].
  • Thresholds [Claimant] needs a good arguable case on [the nexus]: "the better of the argument" (Vinmar), more than a prima facie case (Bradley Lomas), but not "a much better argument" or a balance of probabilities. It needs only a serious question on [the merits] (Bradley Lomas).
  • Why Extraterritorial Jurisdiction (handout Q) Per Week 6 notes, disputes with [absent defendants] strongly connected with Singapore ([a Singapore contract / a tort here / Singapore assets]) should be triable here; otherwise [claimant] is forced abroad even where Singapore is the natural forum.
  • From Exorbitant to Appropriate Traditionally service out was "exorbitant": jurisdiction is territorial and serving [defendant] in [X] intrudes on [X]'s sovereignty, hence circumspection and the burden on [claimant] (Consistel).
    • Now: per Abela v Baadarani and Four Seasons v Brownlie (adopted by C&Y), there are no "muscular presumptions" against serving [defendant] in [X]: the decision is "a pragmatic one in the interests of efficient conduct of litigation in an appropriate forum" — "extraordinary", not "exorbitant".
    • Why (Yeo): what reaches [X] is notice of [the proceedings], not a sovereign command. Singapore courts still say "exorbitant" but mean comity-based caution; keep the caution of Société Générale v Dreyfus before hauling [a foreigner] here.
  • Why the Reform (CJC Report) Per the CJC Report, r 1(1) prescribes criteria rather than a list, so [claimant] need not scrutinise "the long list ... in the hope of fitting into one or more descriptions", and no [category that should be heard here] is accidentally omitted.
    • Cautious: per C&Y, Singapore did not adopt forum conveniens as [the sole test], but is bolder than [England], whose PD gateways remain closed.
  • Why Keep the Gateways Per Foxton J (adopted by C&Y), they supply "an objective, justifiable and ... internationally recognised nexus", which matters for comity, for recognition of [the Singapore judgment] in [X], and in the competitive dispute resolution market; the natural forum test alone "is rather more judgmental".
    • Distinct roles: per Four Seasons v Brownlie, gateways set the legal maximum of [jurisdiction]; forum conveniens only limits it and never enlarges [the court's reach].
  • One Gateway (Yeo, evaluative) Per Yeo, Singapore now has one gateway, "sufficient nexus", within the court's bounded discretion, so [a claim] outside every listed factor may still qualify. Technically [a claim] within a factor could still lack sufficient nexus, but since the reform liberalises, that is probably not intended.
    • Critique: per Yeo, it risks being "an open territory with no fence", since almost any [claim] fits (n); Singapore may answer by weighing substantive connections at the forum stage, or by a "real and substantial connection" test within [sufficient nexus].
    • Territoriality survives: per Week 6 notes, in residual form: [the factors] supply a recognised nexus, comity counsels caution, [Singapore judgments] need presence or submission abroad, and the Moçambique rule remains.
  • Is the Reform Significant? (evaluative) Partly. Per C&Y, the heads survive almost word for word, and Li Shengwu had already made (n) near open-ended. The real change is conceptual: a non-exhaustive list, no pro-[defendant] construction, appropriateness rather than [fitting a statutory box], and room to expand.
  • Round 1: Without Notice Only [claimant] is before [the AR]. Per C&Y, [claimant] bears the burden and owes full and frank disclosure of matters against it ([defendant]'s links with [X], the foreign proceedings, gateway weaknesses).
    • Non-disclosure: per Zoom and C&Y, material non-disclosure by [claimant] can found a set-aside unless [defendant] waived it.
  • Round 2: Inter Partes [Defendant] applies to set aside under O 6 r 7(5)(a) / r 12(4)(a) (5 weeks if served out); jurisdiction falls with the service. Per Zoom and Li Shengwu, the legal burden stays on [claimant] on all three requirements, though practically higher now [defendant] contests.
    • Flexibility: per C&Y (citing NML Capital and IM Skaugen), absent abuse [claimant] may rely on [a new cause of action / gateway] not in the without-notice application, and the court weighs [developments after approval].
    • Evidential burden: [defendant] must evidence its own counter-arguments, e.g., that [the contract] is governed by [X] law. Practice: per Chong (seminar), attack on all fronts, even where [one requirement] looks conclusive.
  • Nexus Per Claim Per C&Y and IM Skaugen (CA), a factor must be shown for each claim against each [defendant], so [contract and tort] claims need one each. One factor per claim suffices, the factors being disjunctive (Li Shengwu, citing Tassell v Hallen).
    • No aggregation: per IM Skaugen (CA), [distinct claims] cannot be pooled, and an assignment does not merge [the assigned claims] into one aggregate claim.
    • Exception: per C&Y, (a) turns on [defendant], not the claim. Approval may be granted on terms or for part of [the claim].
  • Interpreting the Factors Per C&Y: read [the factors] more liberally now they sit in a PD; no need to resolve doubts in [defendant]'s favour; old O 11 case law still helps; English (PD 6B) and NSW cases help with care.
    • Choice of law: per C&Y, Singapore choice of law rules decide whether [a "contract" exists] or [a claim is "founded on a tort"] (C2; C3).
  • Defendant's Options When Served Out (handout) Presently, [defendant] has been served in [X] and must choose:
    • Ignore: risks [a default judgment], though it may be unenforceable in [X], where its assets are, if [X] requires presence or submission.
    • Set aside (existence): no nexus, no serious question, Singapore not forum conveniens, or non-disclosure; burden on [claimant]; usually [defendant]'s best first move (J4.2).
    • Stay (exercise): forum non conveniens or [a foreign EJC / arbitration clause]; burden on [defendant] (J4.1).
    • Defend on the merits (submission), or go on the offensive: sue in [X] or seek an ASI against [claimant] (J6).
    • What decides it: where the burden lies, the strength of [the nexus], where [defendant]'s assets are, costs, and the submission risk.
  • Recognition Implication (evaluative) Per Week 6 slides, O 8 jurisdiction is not internationally recognised at common law, which looks only to presence in, or submission to, [the adjudicating forum] (J7). So [the Singapore judgment] may be unenforceable in [X] or wherever [defendant]'s assets lie; weigh this before preferring Singapore.
J3.1.2 Requirement (a): Good Arguable Case of Nexus (the para 63(3) Factors)

(select the factor(s); plead several in the alternative, then the non-exhaustive fallback; mark each as merits-engaging or neutral for J3.1.4)

  • (a) Domicile, Residence, Business or Property: Nature [Claimant] seeks relief against [defendant], who is [domiciled / ordinarily resident / carrying on business / has property] in Singapore. Per Elan Impex, [claimant] must show a good arguable case basis to seek relief from [defendant].
    • Catch-all: the connection is with [defendant], not [the claim], so [contract, tort and restitution] claims can all be lumped under (a).
    • Neutral: (a) says nothing about [the merits], so requirement (c) must be proved separately against [defendant] (J3.1.4).
  • (a) Domicile Per C&Y, Singapore law decides domicile. Per Peters Roger, [defendant]'s domicile of origin ([father's if legitimate and born in his lifetime / mother's otherwise]) persists until a domicile of choice is acquired by voluntary residence plus an intention to remain and make a home in [country].
    • Citizens: the Women's Charter s 3(5) presumption that [Singapore citizens] are domiciled here may guide [the court], though arguably it is confined to family matters (C&Y).
    • Company: per Gasque v IRC, [company] is domiciled in [its place of incorporation].
  • (a) Ordinary Residence Per Levene v IRC, residence "with some degree of continuity and apart from accidental or temporary absences", voluntarily adopted with settled purpose (Tjong Very Sumito); here [defendant]'s [years in Singapore / employment here] [suffice / do not].
    • Companies: per C&Y, no uniform meaning; the tax test (central management and control) is inapt for [amenability], and it is strongly arguable that Adams v Cape presence of [company] establishes ordinary residence.
  • (a) Carrying on Business Per C&Y, it applies to [individuals] as well as [companies], with no fixed-place or minimal-period requirement on the wording, so it appears enough that [defendant] is carrying on business here when permission is sought.
  • (a) Property Per Lehman Brothers, [the property, e.g., shares / a bank account] need not relate to [the claim]; per Bunge it need not be substantial (a share in a Singapore subsidiary sufficed). But: per C&Y, insubstantial [property] weakens the forum analysis under (b).
  • (b) Injunction [Claimant] seeks an injunction ordering [defendant] to do or refrain from [X] in Singapore. Per Lakshmi Anil Salgaocar v Chilton, it must be part of the substantive relief with a reasonable prospect of grant.
    • Not enough: an interlocutory [Mareva] (Karaha Bodas), or a mandatory injunction compelling [defendant] to perform [the agreement] abroad (C&Y).
    • ASI: per its wording, (b) covers only conduct "in Singapore", and per C&Y interlocutory injunctions are excluded, so it does not obviously carry [an ASI against the X proceedings]. Per Chong (answer scheme), seek [the ASI] as ancillary relief in [the substantive action] served out, so amenability comes from that service (J6).
  • (c) Necessary or Proper Party: The Anomaly [Foreign defendant] (D2) is a necessary or proper party to the claim against [anchor defendant] (D1), duly served. Per AK Investment, this head is anomalous: it rests on no territorial connection between [the claim] and Singapore (The Brabo).
    • Caution: per AK Investment, [D2] must not be brought in "as a matter of course" (The Golden Mariner) merely because the alternative is [two suits].
  • (c) The Anchor Claim Per AK Investment, that D1 is sued only to bring in [D2] is not fatal (The Brabo; Derby v Larsson), but the claim against [D1] must not be "bound to fail" (otherwise D1's presence is a device). A point of law going to [jurisdiction] is normally decided now.
    • Duly served: per C&Y, if [D1] has not been duly served when the application is made, it is dismissed, and approval wrongly given is set aside unless [claimant] obtains retroactive leave; irregular service of [D1] may be curable.
    • Collapse: per Elan Impex, if the action against [D1] is struck out, [D2] ceases to be a necessary or proper party.
  • (c) Is D2 a Proper Party? Per AK Investment (adopting Massey v Heynes), ask whether, had [D1] and [D2] both been here, both would have been proper parties: the claims must involve "one investigation", be "closely bound up" or share a "common thread" ([the same transaction / conspiracy]).
    • Merits: per AK Investment, a serious issue against [D2] is the summary judgment test (Seaconsar); per J H Rayner, [D2] with a good defence ([limitation / no privity]) cannot be a proper party.
    • Limits: per C&Y, [an additional trustee] who is not a joint trustee of the same trust is not necessary; England bars chaining (serving [D3] off [D2] served under the same head). (c) is neutral, so run J3.1.4.
    • Application: per Chong (AY23-24 feedback), [D2]'s pre-contractual misrepresentation and [D1]'s post-contractual breach of [the supply contract] share no common question of law or fact, so [D2] is not a proper party (unless [D1] is sued vicariously).
  • (d) Contract: Two Requirements Per C&Y, [claimant]'s claim must [enforce / rescind / annul / recover damages in respect of] a contract, and [the contract] must be (i) made in Singapore or through an essential step here; (ii) made through [an agent] here for a principal abroad; (iii) governed by Singapore law; or (iv) contain a Singapore jurisdiction term.
  • (d) Existence and Denial of the Contract Per Tecnomar, [claimant] needs only a good arguable case that [the contract] exists, so [defendant]'s denial of [formation / authority] is not fatal.
    • Which law: per C&Y, formation is tested by [the contract]'s proper law under Singapore PIL, not Singapore domestic law, so a contract valid under [X] law counts though it lacks consideration.
    • Claim denying the contract: per C&Y, once unclear, but now largely academic: the list is non-exhaustive and may be read generously, so [a claim that the contract is void] qualifies if one of [limbs (i)–(iv)] is shown.
  • (d) "A Contract": Parties Per C&Y, unsettled whether [the contract] must be between [claimant] and [defendant]. Against: Chen v BHNV held (d) "plainly inapplicable" against [a non-party defendant].
    • For: per C&Y, [an assignee / subrogated insurer / third party entitled to enforce under the proper law] steps into the original party's shoes and may rely on (d) against [defendant].
  • (d)(i) Made in Singapore Per C&Y, it is open whether domestic law or choice of law rules locate formation. Assuming domestic law, per Transniko, a contract by [telephone / instantaneous communication] is made where [offeror] receives acceptance, and one accepted by conduct where [the conduct] occurred.
    • Counterparts: per C&Y, a contract signed in counterpart by [claimant] and [defendant] may be made in two places.
    • Essential step: includes negotiations culminating in [the contract]. Per DJS Solutions, a contract negotiated over [WhatsApp] by [claimant]'s director, who was based here and ran [claimant]'s business from Singapore, was made here although he travelled.
  • (d)(ii) Through an Agent Here Per National Mortgage, [agent] needs only authority to negotiate, not to contract. Per DJS Solutions, [the relief] must relate to the contract made by or through [defendant]'s agent, not merely one [agent] introduced.
  • (d)(iii) Governed by Singapore Law Per Shanghai Turbo and Recovery Vehicle 1, apply Singapore choice of law rules ([express / implied / objective proper law]: C2) to [the contract].
    • Not decisive: per Amin Rasheed, [the policy] was impliedly governed by English law, yet leave to serve out was refused because [Kuwait] was the appropriate forum (J4.2).
  • (d)(iv) Jurisdiction Term Per Vizcaya Partners (adopted by C&Y), an agreement to submit to [Singapore] is not inferred from a Singapore governing law clause; [claimant] needs [cl X] itself to give the Singapore court jurisdiction. Where [the clause] exists, see (r) and J3.2.
  • (d) Neutral Factor Each limb is a connecting factor that says nothing about [breach], so requirement (c) must be proved separately (J3.1.4). Hence [claimant] who says no contract was formed may still use (d), needing only a good arguable case of [the Singapore connection].
  • (e) Breach in Singapore [Defendant] [failed to deliver / pay here], or broke [the contract] in [X] in a way that made performance in Singapore impossible. Per C&Y, this turns on place of breach and place of performance, the latter fixed by [an express term] or the proper law.
    • Alternative places (−): per C&Y (citing Recovery Vehicle 1), where [the obligation] could be performed in [Singapore or X], non-performance is not a breach "in Singapore".
    • Repudiation: per Mutzenbecher, the place of breach is arguably where [defendant]'s repudiatory act, e.g., [the wrongful sale], occurred.
  • (e) Multiple or Continuing Breaches Where [goods] were due here but [defendant] wrongfully [sold them in X], there is both a failure to perform here and a repudiatory act abroad. J3.1.5 Service Out Without the Court's Approval (O 8 r 1(3))

(write where [the contract] says anything about service of process)

  • Rule Per O 8 r 1(3), approval "is not required if service out of Singapore is allowed under a contract between the parties", so [claimant] may serve [defendant] in [X] without the three requirements.
    • Basis: per C&Y, party autonomy; an incremental step from the old O 10 r 3 (contractual local method of service, even on [an overseas defendant], for [claims under that contract]).
    • Scope: per C&Y, it presumably applies where [the parties] submitted to Singapore by contract and the contract permits service out; a bare [Singapore clause] with no service provision must go through r 1(1) (J3.2).
    • SICC contrast: it mirrors SICC Rules O 5 r 6(2) (J5.2.4), but not exactly: r 1(3) asks for [a contract allowing service], not [a choice of court agreement].
  • NW Corp: Read Widely Per NW Corp, [a clause] choosing [English law], referring disputes to the Singapore courts and providing for "service of process by registered mail" fell within r 1(3), so posting [the originating process] to [defendant] in Hong Kong needed no approval.
    • Reasons: the rule says "out of", not "outside", Singapore, so no place of [service] need be named; a contract "allows" service out if it agrees to service of Singapore process on [a foreign defendant] not ordinarily here.
    • Purpose: per NW Corp, r 1(3) spares [claimant] a redundant approval where [defendant] has consented to jurisdiction being founded by service, consistent with the Ideals of expedition and efficiency.
    • Context: the clause named the Singapore courts in the same breath, and [the contract] recorded [defendant]'s Hong Kong place of business, so "service of process" could only mean service of Singapore process abroad.
  • What "Allowed" Means (Yeo, evaluative) Per Yeo, three readings: [the contract] allows (i) [an agreed method] that can work in or out of Singapore; (ii) a method expressly or impliedly usable for service out; or (iii) service out without prior approval.
    • Choice: (i) clashes with O 8 r 2(1)(a), which contemplates service out "in the manner agreed" after approval, so [a bare agreed method] is not enough; (ii) is most persuasive, since [defendant] cannot claim surprise; (iii) fits party autonomy and differs little from (ii).
    • Rationale: per Yeo, an agreement allowing service out is an implied agreement to submit, so [defendant]'s submission may be qualified, e.g., by [a reservation of objections].
  • GD v SICC (Yeo) Per Yeo, agreeing to [SICC jurisdiction] alone allows service out without approval (SICC Rules O 5 r 6(2)(a)), but agreeing only to [General Division jurisdiction] does not, even in a Hague case; Yeo argues the latter should also suffice.
  • Consequence for the Defendant Per NW Corp, service under r 1(3) founds jurisdiction without approval, so [defendant] cannot dispute its existence, only its exercise on natural forum grounds, where the burden is [defendant]'s own (J4.1).
    • Clause naming Singapore: where [the contract] also names the Singapore courts, [defendant]'s forum challenge is itself a breach, so it must show strong cause (J3.2; J4.3).
  • Related Routes Where [the agreed mode] is service in Singapore, it is service in under O 7 r 2(1)(d) (COSCO; J3.0.2). Per C&Y, r 2(1)(d) is looser than old O 10 r 3: [the action] need not relate to the contract containing the agreed method, and the agreement need not be [a contract] (no consideration).
    • Solicitors: where [defendant] instructs Singapore solicitors to accept service, it is treated as service in (C&Y), better analysed as submission (Yeo; J2.2.2). Per C&Y, the practical difference between [service in by agreement] and [r 1(3)] is now small.
J3.1.6 Mode of Service: the Hierarchy of Service

(write whenever [claimant] has served, or wants to serve, [defendant] by substituted service or on solicitors)

  • Two Sequential Steps (must write) Per Beranek, ask first about the right to serve [defendant], then about the mode of serving [the originating process].
    • Right: [defendant] present here → as of right (s 16(1)(a)(i)); [defendant] absent → only through O 8, by approval (r 1(1)) or agreement (r 1(3)).
    • Mode: personal service on [defendant] (O 6 r 4, per C&Y), or, if impracticable, substituted service (O 7 r 7, per Yeo) or dispensation with personal service (O 7 r 1(2), per Xu Xiangrong) on [the facts].
  • Consistel: The Hierarchy Per Consistel, where [defendant] left Singapore before [the originating process] was issued, [claimant] must first seek permission to serve out; substituted service arises only if personal service is impracticable — "a hierarchy of service which must be adhered to".
    • Reasons: personal service is not impracticable merely because [defendant] is abroad; comity requires circumspection over [an absent defendant]; otherwise [claimant] would bypass the court's scrutiny of forum conveniens, making the service-out rules otiose.
    • Not retrospective: that substituted service in fact brought [the claim] to [defendant]'s notice does not validate it after the event.
  • Consistel's Exceptions (±) Per Consistel, substituted service without first serving out is allowed where [defendant] (i) left Singapore to evade [service] (Re Urquhart), or (ii) moves constantly between countries, so personal service is futile (Ng Swee Hong).
    • Applied: neither applied in Consistel: [the defendants] had long lived in [the UAE], made enquiries when told of the attempted service, and were not peripatetic.
    • Status: Beranek doubted the exceptions, but it is an AR decision and Consistel is High Court, so per Chong (seminar), [claimant] and [defendant] should both follow Consistel.
  • Substituted Service Out Per Janesh s/o Rajkumar ("CHEFPIERRE"), the court may order substituted service out of jurisdiction on [an unknown defendant] ([by email / NFT airdrop / social media]), using its inherent power though no rule expressly provides for it (Yeo).
    • So: Janesh goes to the mode, not the right; [claimant] still needs approval under O 8 r 1(1) to serve [defendant], and Consistel is untouched.
  • The Shortcut Cases (−) Three cases let [claimant] reach [an absent defendant] by service in Singapore:
    • COSCO: service on [defendant]'s Singapore solicitors, who confirmed authority to accept [service], is service in under O 7 r 2(1)(d) and s 16(1)(a)(i) — better explained as submission (Yeo) or constructive presence (Beranek) (J3.0.2).
    • Xu Xiangrong v Fu Xianwei: no approval to serve out is needed before personal service on [a foreign defendant] is dispensed with, where ordinary service is then effected here ([by email to his Singapore solicitors]), following Madison Pacific (approval is needed only if [the document] is served out).
    • Xu caveat: where [defendant] is abroad and has not submitted, the court should still consider forum "for good order", with circumspection given comity towards [X].
    • Malayan Banking v Zhang Zhencheng: a notice of appointment of [solicitor] filed to challenge jurisdiction is not submission (by parity with O 6 r 12(5)), so [claimant] cannot then serve [that solicitor].
    • Malayan Banking caveat: the alternative prayer to dispense with personal service on [defendant], raised only on appeal, was rejected, but [a fresh application] is not barred.
  • Evaluation Per Chong (seminar), Consistel's hierarchy (followed in Beranek) is the correct framework, and COSCO and Malayan Banking are wrong. They and Xu Xiangrong each let [claimant] reach [an absent defendant] while skipping step 1, the scrutiny of nexus and forum (J3.0.2).
    • Application: presently, [claimant] [did / did not] obtain approval before [the substituted service / dispensation], so service [is / is not] liable to be set aside.
  • Full and Frank Disclosure Per C&Y, because the application is without notice, [claimant] must disclose matters against approval ([defendant]'s connections with [X], the foreign proceedings, gateway weaknesses). Material non-disclosure can found [a setting aside] (Zoom).

1Start here: J3 in plain English

When the defendant is not in Singapore and has not submitted, the court has no automatic power over it. The claimant must get permission to serve the originating claim abroad. This is service out: s 16(1)(a)(ii) SCJA with O 8 ROC 2021.

★ IN PLAIN ENGLISH

Serving someone in Singapore is like tapping a player already on your pitch. Serving someone abroad is like asking another country's player to come and play here: you need a good reason. The court asks three things before it lets you send the invitation: Is there a real link to Singapore? (nexus) Is Singapore the best place for this match? (forum conveniens) Is there a genuine dispute worth playing? (serious question).

▤ Which bar applies to which requirement, lowest first

  3. 3Balance of probabilities (the trial itself): more likely than not, decided on all the evidence. Never the test at the service-out stage.
★ IN PLAIN ENGLISH

Why different bars? The court decides service out early, on affidavits, before any witness is cross-examined. It cannot decide the whole case at that point. So it asks for a firm showing on the thing that justifies reaching abroad (the link to Singapore) and only a light showing on the merits (that there is something real to try).

★ IN PLAIN ENGLISH

On a stay, Spiliada has two halves: the defendant shows another court is clearly better (stage 1), then the claimant can still say "but I would suffer injustice there" (stage 2). On service out, the claimant carries the forum burden from the start. The open question is whether a claimant who loses stage 1 still gets its stage 2 argument. One side says no: if Singapore is not the right forum, the court should never reach abroad at all. The other side says yes: a claimant facing real injustice abroad should not be shut out just because more connecting factors point elsewhere. Write both, and note the CA has left it open.

J3.1.5 Service Out Without the Court's Approval (O 8 r 1(3))

(write where [the contract] says anything about service of process)

  • Rule Per O 8 r 1(3), approval "is not required if service out of Singapore is allowed under a contract between the parties", so [claimant] may serve [defendant] in [X] without the three requirements.
    • Basis: per C&Y, party autonomy; an incremental step from the old O 10 r 3 (contractual local method of service, even on [an overseas defendant], for [claims under that contract]).
    • Scope: per C&Y, it presumably applies where [the parties] submitted to Singapore by contract and the contract permits service out; a bare [Singapore clause] with no service provision must go through r 1(1) (J3.2).
    • SICC contrast: it mirrors SICC Rules O 5 r 6(2) (J5.2.4), but not exactly: r 1(3) asks for [a contract allowing service], not [a choice of court agreement].
  • NW Corp: Read Widely Per NW Corp, [a clause] choosing [English law], referring disputes to the Singapore courts and providing for "service of process by registered mail" fell within r 1(3), so posting [the originating process] to [defendant] in Hong Kong needed no approval.
    • Reasons: the rule says "out of", not "outside", Singapore, so no place of [service] need be named; a contract "allows" service out if it agrees to service of Singapore process on [a foreign defendant] not ordinarily here.
    • Purpose: per NW Corp, r 1(3) spares [claimant] a redundant approval where [defendant] has consented to jurisdiction being founded by service, consistent with the Ideals of expedition and efficiency.
    • Context: the clause named the Singapore courts in the same breath, and [the contract] recorded [defendant]'s Hong Kong place of business, so "service of process" could only mean service of Singapore process abroad.
  • What "Allowed" Means (Yeo, evaluative) Per Yeo, three readings: [the contract] allows (i) [an agreed method] that can work in or out of Singapore; (ii) a method expressly or impliedly usable for service out; or (iii) service out without prior approval.
    • Choice: (i) clashes with O 8 r 2(1)(a), which contemplates service out "in the manner agreed" after approval, so [a bare agreed method] is not enough; (ii) is most persuasive, since [defendant] cannot claim surprise; (iii) fits party autonomy and differs little from (ii).
    • Rationale: per Yeo, an agreement allowing service out is an implied agreement to submit, so [defendant]'s submission may be qualified, e.g., by [a reservation of objections].
  • GD v SICC (Yeo) Per Yeo, agreeing to [SICC jurisdiction] alone allows service out without approval (SICC Rules O 5 r 6(2)(a)), but agreeing only to [General Division jurisdiction] does not, even in a Hague case; Yeo argues the latter should also suffice.
  • Consequence for the Defendant Per NW Corp, service under r 1(3) founds jurisdiction without approval, so [defendant] cannot dispute its existence, only its exercise on natural forum grounds, where the burden is [defendant]'s own (J4.1).
    • Clause naming Singapore: where [the contract] also names the Singapore courts, [defendant]'s forum challenge is itself a breach, so it must show strong cause (J3.2; J4.3).
  • Related Routes Where [the agreed mode] is service in Singapore, it is service in under O 7 r 2(1)(d) (COSCO; J3.0.2). Per C&Y, r 2(1)(d) is looser than old O 10 r 3: [the action] need not relate to the contract containing the agreed method, and the agreement need not be [a contract] (no consideration).
    • Solicitors: where [defendant] instructs Singapore solicitors to accept service, it is treated as service in (C&Y), better analysed as submission (Yeo; J2.2.2). Per C&Y, the practical difference between [service in by agreement] and [r 1(3)] is now small.

★ IN PLAIN ENGLISH

O 8 r 1(3) is a pre-agreed delivery address. If the parties already agreed in their contract how court papers may be sent abroad ("by registered mail to the address below"), the court does not need to give permission again: the defendant gave it in advance. The price for the defendant is that it can no longer argue the court lacks power; it can only argue the court should not use it.

▤ On your pre-write: J3.1.6 Mode of Service: the Hierarchy of Service (word for word)
J3.1.6 Mode of Service: the Hierarchy of Service

(write whenever [claimant] has served, or wants to serve, [defendant] by substituted service or on solicitors)

  • Two Sequential Steps (must write) Per Beranek, ask first about the right to serve [defendant], then about the mode of serving [the originating process].
    • Right: [defendant] present here → as of right (s 16(1)(a)(i)); [defendant] absent → only through O 8, by approval (r 1(1)) or agreement (r 1(3)).
    • Mode: personal service on [defendant] (O 6 r 4, per C&Y), or, if impracticable, substituted service (O 7 r 7, per Yeo) or dispensation with personal service (O 7 r 1(2), per Xu Xiangrong) on [the facts].
  • Consistel: The Hierarchy Per Consistel, where [defendant] left Singapore before [the originating process] was issued, [claimant] must first seek permission to serve out; substituted service arises only if personal service is impracticable — "a hierarchy of service which must be adhered to".
    • Reasons: personal service is not impracticable merely because [defendant] is abroad; comity requires circumspection over [an absent defendant]; otherwise [claimant] would bypass the court's scrutiny of forum conveniens, making the service-out rules otiose.
    • Not retrospective: that substituted service in fact brought [the claim] to [defendant]'s notice does not validate it after the event.
  • Consistel's Exceptions (±) Per Consistel, substituted service without first serving out is allowed where [defendant] (i) left Singapore to evade [service] (Re Urquhart), or (ii) moves constantly between countries, so personal service is futile (Ng Swee Hong).
    • Applied: neither applied in Consistel: [the defendants] had long lived in [the UAE], made enquiries when told of the attempted service, and were not peripatetic.
    • Status: Beranek doubted the exceptions, but it is an AR decision and Consistel is High Court, so per Chong (seminar), [claimant] and [defendant] should both follow Consistel.
  • Substituted Service Out Per Janesh s/o Rajkumar ("CHEFPIERRE"), the court may order substituted service out of jurisdiction on [an unknown defendant] ([by email / NFT airdrop / social media]), using its inherent power though no rule expressly provides for it (Yeo).
    • So: Janesh goes to the mode, not the right; [claimant] still needs approval under O 8 r 1(1) to serve [defendant], and Consistel is untouched.
  • The Shortcut Cases (−) Three cases let [claimant] reach [an absent defendant] by service in Singapore:
    • COSCO: service on [defendant]'s Singapore solicitors, who confirmed authority to accept [service], is service in under O 7 r 2(1)(d) and s 16(1)(a)(i) — better explained as submission (Yeo) or constructive presence (Beranek) (J3.0.2).
    • Xu Xiangrong v Fu Xianwei: no approval to serve out is needed before personal service on [a foreign defendant] is dispensed with, where ordinary service is then effected here ([by email to his Singapore solicitors]), following Madison Pacific (approval is needed only if [the document] is served out).
    • Xu caveat: where [defendant] is abroad and has not submitted, the court should still consider forum "for good order", with circumspection given comity towards [X].
    • Malayan Banking v Zhang Zhencheng: a notice of appointment of [solicitor] filed to challenge jurisdiction is not submission (by parity with O 6 r 12(5)), so [claimant] cannot then serve [that solicitor].
    • Malayan Banking caveat: the alternative prayer to dispense with personal service on [defendant], raised only on appeal, was rejected, but [a fresh application] is not barred.
  • Evaluation Per Chong (seminar), Consistel's hierarchy (followed in Beranek) is the correct framework, and COSCO and Malayan Banking are wrong. They and Xu Xiangrong each let [claimant] reach [an absent defendant] while skipping step 1, the scrutiny of nexus and forum (J3.0.2).
    • Application: presently, [claimant] [did / did not] obtain approval before [the substituted service / dispensation], so service [is / is not] liable to be set aside.
  • Full and Frank Disclosure Per C&Y, because the application is without notice, [claimant] must disclose matters against approval ([defendant]'s connections with [X], the foreign proceedings, gateway weaknesses). Material non-disclosure can found [a setting aside] (Zoom).
  • Not retrospective. That substituted service in fact reached the defendant does not validate it.
  • Janesh ("CHEFPIERRE"): substituted service out by email or NFT airdrop on an unknown defendant goes to the mode, not the right; approval under O 8 r 1(1) is still needed.
  • The shortcut cases (−): COSCO (service on solicitors here), Xu Xiangrong (dispensing with personal service without approval to serve out), Malayan Banking (a notice of appointment is not submission, so its solicitor cannot be served).

Hypo: the Thai buyer

Facts. Alpha (Singapore) agreed by WhatsApp from its Singapore office to sell machines to Beta (Thailand), delivery FOB Singapore.   2. Route. No clause allows service out, and no CCAA clause, so approval on the "appropriate court" ground (J3.1.1).
