How conflict of laws works, and how to use the router
Every conflict question asks one of three things: can a Singapore court hear this case, which country's law decides it, and will Singapore give effect to a foreign judgment. Question 1 tests the first and third, contract by contract; Question 2 tests the second, issue by issue.

Question 1 · Jurisdiction, injunctions and foreign judgments

J1Q1 · J1
Clause triage: what the jurisdiction clause promises, and the CCAA
Read the jurisdiction clause first and ask what it promises. That tells you who, if anyone, is in breach, and breach decides the test later (strong cause or Spiliada). If the Hague Convention applies through the CCAA, it overrides everything: the chosen court must hear the case and other courts must step aside.

J2Q1 · J2
Existence when the defendant is in Singapore: service in and submission
The court has jurisdiction as of right over a defendant served in Singapore or who submits. For every route, name two things together: the founding provision (which part of s 16 SCJA) and the mode of service (which rule). Only a never-registered foreign company needs the Adams v Cape presence test.

J3Q1 · J3
Existence when the defendant is abroad: service out
A defendant outside Singapore can be brought in only through O 8: without approval if the contract allows service out, otherwise with approval on the "appropriate court" ground, where the claimant must show (a) a good arguable case of nexus through a para 63(3) factor, (b) Singapore is forum conveniens, and (c) a serious question to be tried.
30 min · 9 flowcharts · 3 illustrations · 18 questions
J4Q1 · J4
Exercise: Spiliada, forum conveniens and strong cause
Once the court has jurisdiction, ask whether it should use it. If someone broke a jurisdiction clause, the party in breach must show strong cause. If no one did, run Spiliada: is another forum clearly more appropriate (stage 1), and would the claimant be denied substantial justice there (stage 2)? Who bears the burden depends on service in or out, and stay or set-aside.
27 min · 8 flowcharts · 2 illustrations · 18 questions
J5Q1 · J5
The SICC: written agreements, transfer and joinder
The SICC is a division of the High Court for international commercial cases. A case gets there in three ways: a written jurisdiction agreement (primary jurisdiction), transfer from the General Division ("more appropriate"), or joinder. Once there under a written agreement, Spiliada and strong cause disappear: the SICC declines only if hearing the case is contrary to its international and commercial character or an abuse of process.
21 min · 7 flowcharts · 2 illustrations · 11 questions
J6Q1 · J6
Anti-suit injunctions: stopping a party suing abroad
An ASI is an order against a person, not a foreign court, telling it to stop suing abroad. Name your basis first, because the elements differ. Contractual: amenability, breach of the clause, no strong reasons. Vexation: amenability, sufficient interest (usually natural forum), vexation, no undue injustice. Abuse of process: amenability and Singapore's self-evident interest in protecting its own process.
24 min · 7 flowcharts · 3 illustrations · 16 questions
J7Q1 · J7
Foreign judgments: enforcement, recognition and AEIs
First ask whether the party wants to enforce the foreign judgment (a sword, to recover money) or have it recognised (a shield, or an estoppel). Then pick the regime: CCAA if the foreign court was the chosen court in a Hague case; REFJA is not examinable; otherwise the common law, where the judgment must be on the merits, from a court with international jurisdiction (presence or submission), final and conclusive, and for a definite sum, with no defence such as fraud, natural justice or public policy.

Question 2 · Choice of law

C1Q2 · C1
Choice of law methodology: characterise, connect, apply
Choice of law is a three-step machine: characterise the issue (contract? tort? property? procedure?), pick the connecting factor for that category, and apply the law it points to. Procedure always stays with Singapore law. Foreign law must be pleaded and proved like a fact; if no one pleads it, Singapore law applies. Two overlays can override the chosen law: Singapore's mandatory rules and its fundamental public policy.
25 min · 6 flowcharts · 2 illustrations · 18 questions
C2Q2 · C2
Contract choice of law: the proper law and illegality
A contract is governed by its proper law, found in three stages: an express choice (virtually conclusive if bona fide, legal and not against public policy), else an implied choice, else the law of closest and most real connection. Formation, form and capacity have their own rules. Illegality can enter by seven routes: the proper law, forum public policy, Foster v Driscoll, Lemenda, Ralli Brothers, tainting, and reliance as a defence.
29 min · 9 flowcharts · 3 illustrations · 20 questions
C3Q2 · C3
Tort choice of law: double actionability and the flexible exception
A tort claim must be actionable under BOTH Singapore law (as if everything happened here) AND the law of the place where the tort was in substance committed. The claimant proves the first limb; the defendant must plead and prove that the foreign law gives no liability. A strictly applied flexible exception can displace either law, or both, where it is purely fortuitous.
