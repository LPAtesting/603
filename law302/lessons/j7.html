Foreign judgments: enforcement, recognition and AEIs

⚡ BIG IDEA

First ask whether the party wants to enforce the foreign judgment (a sword, to recover money) or have it recognised (a shield, or an estoppel). Then pick the regime: CCAA if the foreign court was the chosen court in a Hague case; REFJA is not examinable; otherwise the common law, where the judgment must be on the merits, from a court with international jurisdiction (presence or submission), final and conclusive, and for a definite sum, with no defence such as fraud, natural justice or public policy.

J7.1 COMMON LAW: REQUIREMENTS FOR ENFORCEMENT

(Use where neither the CCAA nor REFJA applies, and for recognition (J7.3). Burden on [creditor]. Presence first; if it fails, submission.)

J7.1.0 Starter

Per C&Y (cf Ong Han Nam), [creditor] must show that [the X judgment] is (a) on the merits; (b) given by a court with international jurisdiction over [debtor]; (c) final and conclusive; and (d) a fresh money judgment for a definite sum that is not a tax, fine or penalty. The same apply to recognition, save (d).

J7.1.1 Judgment on the Merits

Per The Sennar (No 2), [the X judgment] is on the merits as [the X court] [found the facts / stated the law / applied it to [liability / quantum]], not merely [a point of procedure].

  • Default Judgment (+) Per C&Y, [the default judgment] is on the merits although [debtor] did not appear and [the X court] may not have considered [the evidence]; non-participation goes only to fraud (J7.2.2) or natural justice (J7.2.3).
  • Interlocutory Judgment (±) Per C&Y, [the X ruling on [issue]] counts if it finally decides that issue and is not on a "procedural" issue. Per The Sennar (No 2), a ruling that [the claim is barred by [a Y jurisdiction clause]] is substantive, but one on [service / security for costs] is not.
  • Limitation Ruling (+) Per s 5 FLPA, [the X court]'s dismissal of [the claim] as time-barred under [X / Y / Singapore] law is deemed on the merits, so it can be [enforced / pleaded as res judicata against [party]].
  • Judgment on a Judgment (−) [The X judgment] merely declares [the Y judgment] enforceable in [X]. Per Sang Cheol Woo (not following Morgan Stanley v Pilot Lead (HK)), it is unenforceable at common law, and per REFJA s 3(4)(c) not registrable. [Creditor] must enforce [the Y judgment] directly.
  • Why Not (−) Per C&Y, [the X judgment] (i) never examined [the issues], so is not on the merits; (ii) decides only its territorial effect in [X] (Merck: no estoppel, J7.3.2.4); and (iii) would launder [the Y judgment], Singapore testing [the X court] instead of [the Y court].
  • Contrary View (+) [Creditor] relies on Desert Palace (HC), which saw no difficulty in enforcing [a judgment on a judgment]; per C&Y it did not grapple with the theory, so [the X judgment] remains [unenforceable].
J7.1.2 International Jurisdiction of [the X court]

[Creditor] must prove that [debtor] was present in [X] (J7.1.2.1–J7.1.2.3) or submitted to [the X court] (J7.1.2.4–J7.1.2.5).

  • Singapore's Rules (−) Per C&Y, the test is Singapore's conflict rules, narrower than Singapore's own bases, with no mirror image. So [the X court]'s [service-out jurisdiction] does not count, and its own finding on [jurisdiction] creates no estoppel (J7.3.2.3).
  • Four Grounds Per Republic of India v Deutsche Telekom (SGCA), [debtor] must have been [present in [X]] or have [claimed or counterclaimed / voluntarily appeared / agreed in advance to submit]. Residence is not listed (J7.1.2.2).
  • Closed Categories (−) Per Schibsby v Westenholz, the grounds are closed. [Creditor] cannot rely on [X]'s real and substantial connection with [the claim], a Canadian ground (Beals v Saldanha) Singapore has not adopted.
J7.1.2.1 Presence: Timing and Federal States
  • Time of Presence Per Adams v Cape, [debtor] must be present in [X] at the time of suit, meaning when [the X process] was served ([date]), not issued ([date]).
  • Federal Systems (±) Per Adams v Cape (obiter, "with varying degrees of doubt"), presence anywhere in [the federation] may suffice for [a federal court], but [a state court of [state]] needs presence in [that state]. (−) Per C&Y, this is questioned, federal judgments being "foreign" in other [US states].
J7.1.2.2 Presence: Natural Persons
  • Temporary Presence (+) Per Adams v Cape (obiter), voluntary presence suffices even if temporary, so [debtor] served during [a two-day conference / a transit stop] in [X] was present.
  • Involuntary Presence (−) Per C&Y, presence must be voluntary; [debtor] [lured to [X] by [creditor]'s fraud / brought there under duress] was not present.
  • Virtual Presence (−) Per Lucasfilm, presence must be physical; [debtor]'s [website targeting [X] consumers / adverts in [X] magazines] did not make it present.
  • Residence Without Presence (±) (+) Per United Malayan Banking (obiter) and C&Y, [debtor]'s residence in [X] suffices a fortiori, being a stronger tie than [fleeting presence]. (−) English law stresses presence (Rubin), and India v Deutsche Telekom omits residence, so [debtor], absent from [X] at service, was not subject to [the X court].
  • Contrast REFJA (−) Per s 5(3)(a)(iv) REFJA, residence is required and mere presence is not enough, so [debtor]'s [business trip] to [X] would not suffice if [X] were gazetted (J7.4.3).
J7.1.2.3 Presence: Companies
  • Test Per Adams v Cape, [debtor company] is present in [X] if, for more than a minimal period, its own business was carried on at or from a fixed place of business [of its own, at its own expense / of [representative]], through [its servants / [representative]]. Per Littauer Glove, a reasonably permanent place suffices.
  • Short but Fixed (+) Per Dunlop v Cudell, a nine-day trade-fair stand was a fixed place of business, so [debtor]'s [booth / showroom] at [event] for [duration] qualifies.
  • No Fixed Place (−) Per Littauer Glove, [debtor]'s [representative] merely staying at [a hotel] for [a few days] taking orders did not carry on business at a fixed place.
  • Whose Business? Factors (±) Per Adams v Cape, [representative] must carry on [debtor]'s business, not its own. Ask whether [debtor] [acquired the premises for [representative] / paid a fixed salary, not commission / controlled the business]. Most weight goes to whether [representative] could bind [debtor] without specific prior authority.
  • Own Business (−) As in Adams v Cape (Cape's Illinois marketing companies, NAAC, AMC and CPC, carried on their own business, so Cape was not present in the US), [representative] [owned its premises / earned commission / needed approval before binding [debtor]].
  • Go-Between (−) Per Vogel, [representative] who merely [introduced customers / passed on orders] without authority to bind [debtor] carried on its own business.
  • Non-Trading Debtor Per Adams v Cape, if [debtor] is [a non-trading body, e.g., [charity / State entity]], the same test applies, substituting its [corporate activities] for "business".
J7.1.2.4 Submission by Conduct

(use if presence fails)

  • Test Per Nimbus, [debtor] submitted only by a step that necessarily waived its objection to [the X court]'s jurisdiction, judged practically, as [business people] would, not theoretically. Per IGCF (PC), adopting Rubin, a step "only necessary or only useful" if [the objection] was waived is submission.
  • Claimant or Merits Defence (+) Per C&Y and Malaysia Marine, [debtor] submitted by [suing in [X] (including related counterclaims or set-off against it) / filing a defence on the merits].
  • Merits Plea With Jurisdiction Challenge (±) Per C&Y, (+) pleading [the merits] where [X] procedure permits but does not require it is submission. (−) If [X] procedure requires both and [debtor] maintained its objection throughout, there is none, (+) unless [debtor] could withdraw [the merits plea] after losing on jurisdiction and did not.
  • Jurisdictional Objection (−) As in Nimbus (objecting in Colombo under the Sri Lankan Arbitration Act, losing and then taking no part, was not submission), [debtor]'s [objection under [clause] / challenge to [service / nexus]] in [the X court] was not submission.
  • Setting Aside on FNC Grounds (+) Per Henry v Geoprosco, [debtor]'s application to set aside [service out] because [X] was forum non conveniens asked [the X court] to decline a jurisdiction it had, so was submission. Nimbus assumed Henry is law without deciding.
  • Henry Should Not Be Followed (−) Per IGCF (PC), Henry's existence/exercise line is "unrealistic and irrational", no common law jurisdiction follows it, and REFJA s 5(3)(a)(i)(C) reverses it. Per C&Y, a failed set-aside on any ground is a protest (Re Dulles), so [debtor]'s [FNC challenge] in [X] was not submission.
  • Stay Application (±) (+) On Henry's logic, [debtor]'s stay application accepted [the X court]'s jurisdiction. (−) Per IGCF, a stay [for arbitration / on FNC grounds] is a protest, unless [debtor] sought more, e.g., [consequential relief on the merits].
  • ROC Analogy (±) (−) [Debtor] cites O 6 r 7(6) and O 6 r 12(5) ROC 2021: challenging the exercise of Singapore's jurisdiction is not submission. (+) Per C&Y, this may not carry over: there is no mirror image, and international jurisdiction uses narrower criteria than [Singapore's own].
  • Whose Law (±) Per Giant Light Metal (approving Akai), Singapore law decides, with [X] procedure as context. Per C&Y it would be strange to find submission where [X] law would not treat [debtor]'s [step] as such, though Rubin left this open.
  • Inchoate Submission (±) [Debtor] submitted to [original claim], and [creditor] says consent should be imputed for [related claim]. Per Giant Light Metal, weigh (a) the connection between the claims, a matter of degree that may span separate later proceedings between [the same parties]; and (b) fairness to both, disregarding [technical procedural impediments].
  • Inchoate: Application (+) As in Giant Light Metal (same parties and claims refiled after settlement talks failed, separate case numbers being a technicality) and Murthy v Sivajothi (submission to a Florida foreclosure action extended to related fraud cross-claims), [related claim] is [the same claim refiled / a related cross-claim].
  • Composition Plan (−) Per Humpuss, [debtor]'s unilateral inclusion as a creditor in [the X composition plan] is not, by itself, submission to [the X court].
J7.1.2.5 Submission by Agreement
  • Express Agreement (+) Per United Malayan Banking, [debtor] submitted in advance by agreeing in [clause] to the jurisdiction of [the X court / the courts of [X]].
  • Implied Agreement (±) (−) Per Malaysia Marine and other RECJA cases, it must be express. (+) Per Vizcaya Partners (PC), it may be implied if [debtor] actually consented in advance to [the X court]; it need not be contractual, and whether it exists is for [the proper law of [contract]]. Per C&Y, whether Singapore follows is open.
  • Insufficient Indicia (−) Per Vizcaya Partners, [debtor] did not impliedly submit merely because it [chose [X] law and did business in [X], even if these founded jurisdiction under [X] procedural law / held shares in an [X] company / made or was to perform [the contract] in [X]].
J7.1.3 Final and Conclusive

Per Sang Cheol Woo, [the X judgment] is final and conclusive if (a) [the X court] cannot vary, reopen or set it aside, though [the X appellate court] may hear an appeal; and (b) [the X court] regards it as res judicata. Per Humpuss and Carl Zeiss, this is tested by [X] law, proved by [expert evidence].

  • Default Judgment (+) Per Humpuss and Eleven v Boxsentry, [the default judgment] is final if given final effect until set aside, although [debtor] may still apply to [the X court] to set it aside.
  • Interlocutory Judgment (±) Per Lakshmi, [the X interlocutory ruling on [issue]] is final if it finally decides [the parties]' rights on that issue, but not if [provisional / decided on a good arguable case and reopenable at trial] (First Laser).
  • Appeal Pending (±) (+) Per C&Y, a pending appeal to [the X appellate court] does not as such defeat finality, though Singapore may [stay enforcement]. (−) But as in Sang Cheol Woo (a provisionally enforceable Seoul High Court judgment was not res judicata under Korean law until the Supreme Court ruled), check [X] law.
  • Limitation Knock-On (+) Per Sang Cheol Woo, the six-year period then runs from when [the X judgment] became final under [X] law, i.e., [the final appellate decision on [date]], consistent with REFJA s 4(1)(b) (J7.2.8).
J7.1.4 Fresh Money Judgment for a Definite Sum
  • Definite Sum (+) Per C&Y, [the sum] must be fixed or arithmetically ascertainable, and [a costs order] is enforceable if final and definite, even where [the merits judgment] has no money order.
  • Fresh Obligation (±) Per Poh Soon Kiat, whether [the X judgment] imposes a fresh obligation to pay [sum] depends on [X] law. As in Poh Soon Kiat ("shall remain liable" in a later Californian fraudulent-transfer judgment was surplusage), where [the later X order] restates liability under [the earlier judgment], [creditor] must sue on [the earlier judgment].
  • Non-Money Judgment (−) Per C&Y, [an order for specific performance / an injunction] is not enforceable at common law, a relic of indebitatus assumpsit. [Creditor] must register under REFJA or the CCAA (J7.4.1; J7.5.1), or sue afresh and plead [the X judgment] as res judicata (J7.3.1).
  • Reform (±) Per C&Y, following Merck's call for convergence, the common law should enforce [non-money relief] as REFJA, the CCAA and Canada (Pro-Swing v Elta) do; the indirect route via [res judicata] already reaches [the same result], less efficiently.
  • Money Order Among Other Orders (+) Per Giant Light Metal, a definite-sum order made among [other orders] founds an action in debt. As in Giant Light Metal (refund due once the buyer made the generator sets available for collection), [creditor] has [made [the goods] available], so [the refund] is payable.
  • Severance of Mixed Orders (±) (−) Per Giant Light Metal (obiter), severance is confined to [penal or revenue] elements. (+) Per C&Y, [the money order] should be severable whenever it is not interdependent with [the non-money order].
  • Tax, Fine or Penalty (−) [The X judgment] for [tax / a fine / a penalty] payable to [the X State] is not enforceable (J7.2.5).
J7.1.5 Reciprocity (Left Open)

[Debtor] argues that [X] does not enforce Singapore judgments, so [the X judgment] should not be enforced.

  • Exam Weight Per Chong (seminar), one line suffices: Merck left [reciprocity] open and later cases have not discussed it, so [the X judgment] is not barred on that ground.
  • For Reciprocity (−) Per Merck, the point is open, and reciprocity is "entirely consonant" with comity, the CA's preferred basis for recognition, and with [the CCAA, REFJA and MORA]. So [debtor] says [X]'s [refusal to enforce Singapore judgments] should bar [the X judgment].
  • Against Reciprocity (+) Per C&Y, the common law enforces [private rights], not sovereignty; reciprocity is waning globally, rarely induces [X] to reciprocate, and penalises [creditor] for policy it cannot control. The statutes are [negotiated schemes] layered on the common law floor, so the comparison is inapt.
  • Liberal If Adopted (+) Per Merck, absence of reciprocity would rarely obstruct recognition in practice, and any requirement would be liberal, e.g., [de jure / presumed reciprocity], which [X] likely satisfies.

J7.2.1 Mistake: No Review of the Merits
  • General Rule (+) Per Godard v Gray and Ralli v Angullia, [debtor]'s claim that [the X court] erred in [fact / law] on [issue], even on Singapore law, is irrelevant; Singapore does not sit on appeal from [the X court].
  • Error of Singapore Law (±) [Debtor] says [the X court] misapplied Singapore law on [issue]. 1Start here: J7 in plain English

Sometimes a court abroad has already decided the dispute. Question 1 ends by asking what that foreign judgment is worth in Singapore. A Singapore court does not automatically obey a foreign judgment: it gives effect to it only if the foreign court had the right kind of jurisdiction and nothing unfair happened.

★ IN PLAIN ENGLISH

A foreign judgment is like a match result from another league. Singapore will count that result if the other referee had authority over the player (the player was in that league's country, or agreed to play there), the result is final (not a half-time score), and it is a clear score (a sum of money). It will not re-referee the match. But it will refuse to count the result if the match was fixed (fraud), a player was never told the match was on (natural justice), or counting it would be outrageous here (public policy).

Use 1 is enforcement: Beta wants money, so all four common law requirements apply, including a definite sum (J7.1). Use 2 is recognition: Beta only wants the Hong Kong judgment treated as deciding the matter, so there is no money requirement (J7.3). The same judgment can be both, depending on who is using it and for what. Always open your answer by saying which one the facts raise.

The laundering problem

Beta wins a judgment against Alpha in Country A. Alpha was never present in A and never submitted, so that judgment would fail requirement (b) in Singapore. Beta then goes to Country B, whose court simply declares the A judgment enforceable there, and brings B's order to Singapore.

If Singapore enforced B's order, the defect in the A judgment would disappear: the judgment would be "laundered" through B. That is why a judgment on a judgment is not enforceable (Sang Cheol Woo). Beta must bring the original A judgment and meet the requirements for it.

Singapore's rules, not the foreign court's

International jurisdiction is tested by Singapore's conflict rules, which are narrower than Singapore's own jurisdiction rules (no mirror image). So the foreign court's service-out jurisdiction does not count, and its own finding on jurisdiction creates no estoppel. Per India v Deutsche Telekom, there are four grounds: presence; claiming or counterclaiming; voluntary appearance; prior agreement to submit. The categories are closed (Schibsby v Westenholz): no "real and substantial connection" ground (Beals v Saldanha, a Canadian ground Singapore has not adopted).

★ IN PLAIN ENGLISH

"No mirror image" means Singapore is stricter with foreign courts than with itself. Singapore lets its own courts reach defendants abroad by service out (J3). But when a foreign court does the same thing to a Singapore defendant, Singapore will not count that judgment unless the defendant was present in that country or submitted to its court. A foreign court's long-arm reach is its own business; it does not bind Singapore.

What counts as a "judgment"A final decision on the merits, consent or default judgment, costs, and non-money judgments (s 2(1)); interim relief is excluded (s 10; Motacus). Judicial settlements are enforced like judgments (s 20).
ProcedureApply under s 13(1) and O 37 without a fresh action; serve the order abroad without permission (O 37 r 6(4)). No limitation period, but if no longer enforceable in the State of origin, Singapore cannot enforce it (s 13(2)).
Narrower than the common lawExhaustive grounds, "manifestly", procedural fraud only, binding findings; so an intrinsic fraud plea fares worse than at common law (C&Y).
A non-chosen court's judgmentOutside the CCAA, which does not bar its enforcement under REFJA or common law; argue the common law breach defence (J7.2.6).
✎ WORKED EXAMPLE
CCAA recognition in practice

A 2020 clause chose the Ruritanian courts exclusively, and Ruritania is a Contracting State. The Ruritanian court orders Alpha to pay US$1m and to deliver up certain moulds. Beta applies in Singapore under s 13(1) and O 37.

  • Both parts can be recognised and enforced: the CCAA covers non-money judgments too (s 2(1)), unlike the common law.
  • Alpha says the Ruritanian judge got the facts wrong. Irrelevant: no review of the merits.
  • Alpha says Beta's witness lied at trial. That is intrinsic fraud, and the CCAA allows only procedural fraud (s 14), so Alpha does worse here than it would at common law.
  • Alpha says it never received notice of the Ruritanian claim in time to defend. That is a mandatory ground for refusal (s 14).

Presently, [debtor] seeks an AEI restraining [creditor] from [relying on / enforcing] [the X judgment] in [X / Y / worldwide]. Per Sun Travels and C&Y, beyond the usual ASI requirements (J6), [debtor] must show exceptional circumstances tied to unconscionability.

  • Why Caution (−) Per Sun Travels, an AEI implies [debtor] delayed in not seeking an ASI earlier, wastes [the X court]'s time and costs, stops [other courts] deciding recognition themselves, and interferes with execution in [X]; it is akin to nullifying [the X judgment].
  • Breach or Vexation Not Enough (−) Per Sun Travels, neither [creditor]'s breach of [the EJC / arbitration clause] nor [vexatious conduct] suffices alone, and party autonomy is no separate basis (Arkhangelsky creates no "agreement" category).
  • Fraud (+) Per Sun Travels, [creditor]'s fraud in obtaining [the X judgment], e.g., [perjury / forged evidence], is an exceptional circumstance, as in Ellerman Lines v Read (reliance on a Turkish judgment procured by [the salvor]'s perjury was restrained).
  • No Knowledge (+) Per Sun Travels (citing Ecobank), [debtor] did not know [the X judgment] was being sought until [it was delivered], e.g., [obtained secretly or too quickly], so its delay is not unconscionable.
  • Delay (±) Per Sun Travels, mere passage of time is not enough; delay counts against [debtor] only when coupled with [knowledge] and/or [prejudice to [creditor] or third parties such as the X court]. Simply limiting [the relief] to reflect the delay, as the judge did, is not the answer.
  • Applicant's Silence (−) Per Sun Travels, both sides' conduct matters: if [debtor] knew of [the fraud] before [the X judgment] but stayed silent, the equities may tilt against it.
  • No Closed List (±) Per Sun Travels, a new category needs the equities to favour relief: either a response to [creditor]'s [unconscionable conduct], or [debtor] has not lost equity through [unconscionable delay]. As in Sun Travels (Hilton delayed until after the Maldivian judgment), [debtor]'s [delay] [defeats / does not defeat] the AEI.
  • Alternatives (+) Per Sun Travels, AEIs "should generally be refused", so [debtor] should instead [resist recognition in Singapore (J7.2) / claim damages for breach of [clause] (J1.7) / resist enforcement in [Y]].

An AEI stops the creditor relying on or enforcing the foreign judgment. Run the J6 ASI requirements first, then add the Sun Travels overlay: exceptional circumstances tied to unconscionability. AEIs "should generally be refused".

? WHY IT MATTERS

Why so cautious? An AEI implies the debtor delayed in not seeking an ASI earlier, wastes the foreign court's time, stops other courts deciding recognition themselves, and is akin to nullifying the foreign judgment (Sun Travels). Mere passage of time is not enough to refuse; delay counts against the debtor only with knowledge and/or prejudice. And if the debtor knew of the fraud but stayed silent, the equities may tilt against it. The alternatives: resist recognition here, claim damages for breach of the clause, or resist enforcement abroad.

★ IN PLAIN ENGLISH

An ASI stops a race before it is run. An AEI tries to cancel the result after the race is over, and in every country at once. That is a much bigger step: the foreign court has already done all the work, and other countries never get to decide for themselves whether to recognise the result. So the debtor must explain why it did not ask for an ASI earlier, and show something exceptional, such as a fixed race (fraud) or a race it never knew was being run.

8Put it together: a worked judgment

✎ WORKED EXAMPLE
Hypo: the Ruritanian default judgment

Beta obtained a default judgment for US$2m in Ruritania against Alpha, a Singapore company. Ruritania is not a Contracting State or gazetted. Alpha had a sales office in Ruritania for three years. The contract had a Singapore EJC. Alpha says it was served at the wrong address and knew nothing until judgment.

  1. Enforce or recognise? Beta wants money: enforcement.
  2. Regime: not CCAA (Ruritania was not the chosen court), not REFJA: common law (J7.0.2).
  3. Requirements: a default judgment is on the merits; Alpha was present in Ruritania (a fixed office for three years, Adams v Cape); final under Ruritanian law (proved by expert); a definite sum (J7.1).
  4. Defences: (i) natural justice: no notice of the proceedings (Jacobson v Frachon; Paulus Tannos); (ii) arguably extrinsic fraud if Beta deliberately used a wrong address (Abouloff applies); (iii) breach of the Singapore EJC: promissory, Alpha did not submit, so the defence is arguable though undecided (J7.2.6).
  5. AEI? If Beta tries to enforce in a third country, Alpha could seek an AEI: no knowledge until judgment is an exceptional circumstance (Sun Travels, citing Ecobank) (J7.6).
