Anti-suit injunctions: stopping a party suing abroad

⚡ BIG IDEA

An ASI is an order against a person, not a foreign court, telling it to stop suing abroad. Name your basis first, because the elements differ. Contractual: amenability, breach of the clause, no strong reasons. Vexation: amenability, sufficient interest (usually natural forum), vexation, no undue injustice. Abuse of process: amenability and Singapore's self-evident interest in protecting its own process.

J6.2 SUFFICIENT INTEREST

(Classify first; use only the matching subsection. Natural forum is only a proxy: it proves interest, but Singapore may have interest without it, and there must be a good reason for Singapore, not [foreign court], to stop [foreign proceedings] (BCS v Baker, citing Barclays v Homan). Contractual ASI: natural forum does not arise (Cooperative Muratori); a Singapore clause supplies the connection (J6.2.1), but if Singapore is not chosen the point stays live (J6.2.6–J6.2.7), hence slide 20's "(b)(?)".)

J6.2.1 Jurisdiction Agreement for Singapore
  • Singapore EJC (+) Presently, [clause X] gives the [General Division] exclusive jurisdiction. Per Westbridge v Mittal and C&Y, [clause X] itself supplies the connection, even if [the parties / the events] have no other link with Singapore; no natural forum inquiry is needed.
  • Why (+) Per Cooperative Muratori (citing Turner v Grovit and UniCredit), [applicant] need not show [Singapore] is more appropriate; the agreement does that. It should take more to sanction a breach of [clause X] than to deny [respondent] its natural forum (Yeo, endorsed in Vinmar).
  • Singapore NEJC (+) Presently, [clause X] is a Singapore NEJC governed by [Singapore law]. Arguably, per Shanghai Turbo and C&Y, a defendant sued here must show strong cause not to be held to its submission, so [clause X] works like an EJC and supplies the connection.
    • Contrast: [The NEJC] supplies interest only; suing in [foreign court] is not itself a breach (J6.3.1.2).
  • SICC Clause, Exclusive or Not (+) Presently, [clause X] is a written [exclusive / non-exclusive] SICC agreement and [the claim] is international and commercial. Per C&Y, Spiliada is irrelevant: O 2 r 3 SICC Rules 2021 supplies the connection test (J5.3).
    • Test: The SICC may decline only if exercising jurisdiction is contrary to its [international and commercial character] or [an abuse of its process] (O 2 r 3(2)), and not solely because [the action] is connected to [foreign state] (O 2 r 3(3)).
    • Exception (−): Not so if [the claim is not international and commercial] or [suing in the SICC is abusive, e.g., tactical delay] (J6.3.1.3).
J6.2.2 Protecting Singapore's Own Process
  • Self-Evident Interest (+) Presently, [respondent]'s [foreign claim] abuses Singapore's process by [relitigating [Suit X]]. Per BCS v Baker, natural forum is then irrelevant: Singapore's interest in protecting its processes, jurisdiction and judgments is "generally self-evident", and only it can judge [the need for protection].
  • Concurrent Proceedings (−) (Non-contract cases.) Presently, [Singapore action] and [foreign action] are both pending, so per Baker v BCS (SICC) and C&Y, [applicant] must show Singapore is the natural forum (J6.2.3).
  • Consecutive Proceedings (+) Presently, Singapore has given judgment in [Suit X], so it has interest to protect [the judgment] from collateral attack by [respondent] relitigating res judicata issues abroad (Baker v BCS; C&Y). Per BCS v Baker, the need extends to [anticipated judgments] but is underscored once judgment exists.
  • Which Is It? Per BCS v Baker, ask when [the specific claim] to be enjoined was raised in [foreign court], not when the actions began. Presently, [respondent] raised [claim] on [date], after [judgment / dismissal of the appeal], so it is consecutive. (C&Y's footnote states this the other way; follow the judgment.)
  • Resisting Enforcement (−) Per Baker v BCS (SICC) and C&Y, it is a "fine line": if [respondent] merely resists [applicant]'s enforcement of [Singapore judgment] in [foreign court], Singapore generally leaves [foreign court] to decide that judgment's effect there.
  • Foreign Judgment Already Given (−) Per BCS v Baker (citing ED & F Man v Haryanto), an ASI stopping [respondent] relying abroad on [foreign court]'s [judgment] is ordinarily inappropriate, and [applicant] needs exceptional circumstances (Sun Travels; J7.6).
  • Interest Without Natural Forum (+) Similar to Shell v Coral Oil (per BCS v Baker), where England had a "very strong interest" in shielding a party already protected by its ASI from further vexatious Lebanese claims, Singapore may protect [applicant] from [further claims] after [earlier ASI / Suit X], though [foreign court] is also a natural forum.
J6.2.3 Alternative Fora: Singapore Is One Forum
  • Natural Forum (+) Presently, [the dispute] can be heard in Singapore or [foreign court]. Per BCS v Baker and Airbus, an ASI is normally granted where Singapore is the natural forum and the other requirements are met. Per Lakshmi, [applicant] must show Singapore is "clearly the more appropriate forum", weighing the quality, not quantity, of [the JIO factors] (J4.1.2).
  • Lakshmi: Real Substance (+) Similar to Lakshmi, where a BVI share mattered only because its subsidiaries held 22 Singapore condominium units, [respondent]'s claim to [asset in [foreign state]] is in truth about [Singapore property], so [foreign state]'s link is only [place of incorporation].
  • Lakshmi: Witnesses (+) Per Lakshmi, video links reduce the weight of witness location, but [third-party witnesses, e.g., [bank officers]] compellable here and not shown compellable in [foreign court] matter where [the dispute] is factual.
  • Lakshmi: Overlap (+) Per Lakshmi, different reliefs in [foreign court] do not show different issues: as [respondent]'s [foreign claim] and [Suit X] are binary, proof of one disproves the other.
  • Lakshmi: Little Weight (−) Per Lakshmi, [governing law] matters little where [both systems] are common law and the issues factual. Little weight goes to [a place of performance assuming [respondent]'s claim succeeds], [a place of enforcement offset by [applicant]'s undertaking] or [the place of an oral telephone contract]; [residence in third states] is neutral.
  • Natural Forum Alone Is Not Enough (−) Per SNIA and C&Y, natural forum alone never justifies an ASI, or Singapore could arrogate to itself [a mere difference of view] with [foreign court] on the natural forum. In SNIA, the ASI turned on the lost contribution claim (J6.3.2). Presently, [applicant] must also show [prejudice in [foreign court]].
  • Advantages Are Not Connecting Factors (+) Similar to SNIA, where Texas lawyers' evidence-gathering, the contingency fee system and an early trial were advantages to the plaintiffs, not connecting factors making Texas the natural forum, [respondent]'s [pre-trial steps / lawyers' expertise] in [foreign court] do not shift the natural forum (J6.4).
J6.2.4 Single Forum: Only [Foreign Court] Can Hear the Claim
  • Framing (must write) Presently, [respondent]'s [claim, e.g., [foreign antitrust claim]] lies only in [foreign court], so Singapore can never be the natural forum. Per Airbus and BCS v Baker, the questions are whether Singapore still has sufficient interest and whether pursuing [claim] is unconscionable. Per C&Y, the ASI is especially harsh, denying [respondent] its only redress.
    • Not Spiliada availability: Per C&Y, "single forum" means [Singapore law] gives no cause of action, so the question is [unconscionability], not [appropriateness]; Spiliada availability asks only whether [a court] can hear the claim.
  • Insolvency (+) Per BCS v Baker (citing Stichting Shell), [Singapore] has interest where the ASI protects its jurisdiction in administering [the insolvency of [company]] against [a creditor suing abroad] (J6.3.3).
  • Predominantly Singaporean Dealings (+) Similar to Midland Bank v Laker, where an English bank's dealings with Laker were English banking business under English law, "overwhelmingly English" in character (Airbus), [applicant]'s dealings with [respondent] were [conducted in Singapore / under Singapore law], so the ASI is consistent with comity.
    • Contrast (−): If [applicant]'s dealings were [in [third state] under its law], an ASI merely because [respondent] is amenable here would be "surprising" (Airbus, on a hypothetical Indian bank).
  • Singapore Public Policy (+) Per BCS v Baker and Airbus (citing Laker v Sabena), Singapore has interest where the ASI protects [Singapore public policy on [X]]; in very exceptional cases this may override even [a foreign EJC] (C&Y) (J6.3.4).
  • Not Unconscionable (−) Similar to British Airways v Laker, where BA and BCal, by taking licences to fly UK–US routes, voluntarily submitted to US antitrust law, [applicant] voluntarily [submitted to [foreign regime]], so [respondent]'s suit is not unconscionable and no ASI issues.
J6.2.5 Dual Fora: Singapore Is Neither
  • General Rule (−) Presently, [respondent] sued in [Court A] while the natural forum is [Court B]. Per Airbus, comity generally requires Singapore to have sufficient interest before indirectly interfering. Per Akai, [Court A] and [Court B] may legitimately differ, and Singapore should not be an "international busybody" (C&Y; heeded in R1 International).
  • Natural Forum Powerless (−) Similar to Airbus, where India, the natural forum, could not reach the claimants, [Court B]'s inability to [restrain [respondent]] does not justify Singapore acting: "only the courts of an interested jurisdiction can act". That [Court A] does not apply [forum non conveniens] is also not enough (Airbus).
  • Extreme Cases (+) Per Airbus and C&Y, an ASI may issue without sufficient connection in "extreme cases", e.g., where [foreign state]'s conduct forfeits the respect comity normally requires. Presently, [describe [state interference / sham proceedings] in [Court A]].
  • Strong Reasons by Analogy (+) Per R1 International (HC, obiter; C&Y), a permanent ASI supporting [a foreign arbitration] may issue on strong reasons, e.g., [the seat lacks effective interim measures]. Arguably, by analogy, strong reasons exist where [Court B] [cannot grant ASIs].
  • s 4(10A) CLA (±) Arguably, s 4(10A) now gives power to grant interim relief, possibly [an interim ASI], in aid of [Court B]'s proceedings (slide 18). But it confers power only: Singapore must still justify interfering with [Court A] (Airbus; Akai).
J6.2.6 EJC for a Foreign Court
  • Go to the Chosen Court (−) Presently, [respondent] sued in [Court A] in breach of an EJC for [Court B]. Per C&Y, [applicant] should ordinarily seek the ASI from [Court B]. Similar to Akai, where the insurer had not applied to the English court though Akai, having sued there, was amenable to it, [applicant] has not applied to [Court B].
    • Reply (+): per Chong (seminar), Akai is old and its "busybody" point may carry less force today, especially where [Court B] is in [an HCCCA Contracting State] (J6.5.3).
  • Reasons to Come Here (+) [Applicant] argues [Court B] [does not grant ASIs / lacks the remedy], or that it must shield its [Singapore assets] from [Court A]'s judgment (C&Y). For HCCCA states, see J6.5.3.
    • Rebuttal (−): Per C&Y, Singapore has generally declined even then. As in Akai, where the insurer sued here only to protect its Singapore assets, any later enforcement against [applicant]'s [assets] is "an entirely different matter", met by resisting [recognition] then (J7).
J6.2.7 Arbitration Seated in [Foreign Seat]
  • Busybody Risk (−) Presently, [respondent] sued in [foreign court] in breach of [arbitration agreement] seated in [foreign seat]. Per C&Y, Singapore generally refrains from ASIs supporting foreign arbitration. Per R1 International (obiter), a permanent ASI needs strong reasons, e.g., [the seat lacks effective interim measures]; interim ASIs come from s 12A IAA (J6.0.2).
  • Contract Supplies the Connection (+) Per UniCredit (UKSC, persuasive), Spiliada is not the test: parties should be held to their bargain by any court before which they "can properly be brought" (Enka), and Airbus's sufficient-interest rule was tied to non-contractual cases. An ASI enforcing [an arbitration agreement] always comes from a court that is neither [the natural] nor [the agreed forum].
  • Comity and Connection (+) Per UniCredit, comity has little role where [foreign court's state] and [seat state] are New York Convention states. Presently, [arbitration agreement] is governed by [Singapore law], a substantial connection like the English-law rights in UniCredit; whether [personal jurisdiction alone] suffices was left open (IPOC, noted in UniCredit).
  • Applying Here Is Not a Breach (+) Per UniCredit (cf Model Law art 9), [arbitration agreement] does not impliedly bar applying to [a court] for relief holding the other party to it, though choosing [foreign seat] implies no agreement to sue in [Singapore].
J6.3 LEGITIMATE INTEREST (ANY ONE SUFFICES)
  • Choosing the Basis Presently, [applicant] relies on [breach of [clause X] (J6.3.1) / vexation (J6.3.2) / abuse of process (J6.3.3) / public policy (J6.3.4)]. Per C&Y, the categories are not closed. Per BCS v Baker, abuse invokes the inherent power (disruption to [Singapore's processes]); vexation invokes equity (the personal equities); the overriding test is the interests of justice.
  • Keep Them Apart (must write if run together) Per Cooperative Muratori, a contractual ASI is a distinct species (citing VKC), and though several strands may operate on [the facts], they are conceptually distinct: [applicant] should not argue that breaching [clause X] is itself vexatious. Breach needs no vexation (Sun Travels; C&Y).
J6.3.1 Breach of Agreement
J6.3.1.1 Step 1: Breach
  • Exclusive Clause Breached (+) Per C&Y, breach is a question of interpretation under [proper law]. Presently, [respondent] breached [clause X], an exclusive [Singapore / SICC / arbitration] clause, by suing in [foreign court]. Per Sun Travels, breach is a separate basis: no unconscionability is needed, and the ASI ordinarily issues absent strong reasons (J6.3.1.4).
  • Rationale Per Finaport, the basis is equity's jurisdiction to ensure [contractual bargains] are honoured, on the "clear and simple ground" that [respondent] promised not to sue in [foreign court] (The Angelic Grace, per Sun Travels). Comity matters less, as Singapore enforces [the bargain] rather than claiming [the dispute] (Sun Travels; C&Y).
  • Other Agreements (+) Per C&Y, the same applies to suits in breach of [an arbitration agreement] or [an agreement not to sue], e.g., the settlement in Grover v SetClear (permanent ASI upheld), and in principle [a mediation agreement] valid under its proper law whose enforcement is not contrary to Singapore public policy.
    • Caution (−): Per C&Y (BTN v BTP), suing is not always a breach of [an arbitration agreement]: [a conditional clause] may let parties sue in [court X] until [arbitration is invoked].
  • Multi-Tiered Clause (+) Similar to Finaport, where "shall first attempt" negotiation, then SMC mediation, then litigation was mandatory, [clause X]'s ["shall first"] wording makes [negotiation / mediation] a precondition, so suing in [foreign court] first breaches it. Per Finaport, there is no principled distinction between [tiered clauses] and EJCs or arbitration agreements.
    • Scope: Per Finaport, suing is not a per se breach, so the ASI restrains [respondent] only until [the preconditions in [clause X]] are met, with liberty to apply. A non-contractual ASI was refused there.
    • Impracticality no answer: Per Finaport (citing Don King), [a beneficiary] suing on [the trustee]'s rights cannot complain the tiers are impractical; it takes [the bargain] as it is (J6.5.2).
  • Choice of Law Clause (−) Presently, [applicant] says [respondent] breached [clause Y], choosing [law X], by suing in [foreign court] to apply [foreign law]. Per Ace Insurance, such a clause is ordinarily declaratory, not promissory, so it founds no implied promise not to sue where [law X] will not apply; "very clear language" is needed (C2.5).
    • No breach anyway (−): Similar to Ace, where the Californian court would in fact have applied the chosen Australian law, [foreign court] would apply [law X], so [the premise] fails.
    • Counter (+): Briggs argues that suing to defeat the chosen law "looks like" a breach (considered in Ace). Argue this only if [clause Y] expressly promises [not to sue where [law X] will not apply].
J6.3.1.2 Singapore NEJC: Generally No Breach
  • No Breach (−) Presently, [clause X] is a Singapore NEJC. 1Start here: J6 in plain English

An anti-suit injunction (ASI) is a Singapore court order telling a party (the respondent) to stop, or not start, a lawsuit in a foreign court. It is addressed to the person, not the foreign court, and breaching it is contempt. But it still interferes indirectly with the foreign court, so comity (respect between legal systems) runs through every step (SNIA; C&Y).

★ IN PLAIN ENGLISH

Imagine two neighbours with a written agreement that any quarrel goes to the village elder. One neighbour runs off to the next village's elder instead. You cannot order the other village's elder to stop listening. But you can order your neighbour to stop going there, and punish him if he disobeys. That is an ASI: aimed at the person, effective only if he is within your reach, and used carefully because the other village will feel slighted.

Three stories, three bases

In each story, Beta has started a lawsuit against Alpha in Ruritania, and Alpha asks the Singapore court to stop it.

★ IN PLAIN ENGLISH

Sufficient interest answers the question "why us?" An ASI tells someone to stop using another country's courts, which that country may resent. So Singapore needs a good reason to be the one stepping in: the parties promised to come here, or Singapore is protecting its own judgment, or Singapore is clearly the right home for the dispute. If Singapore has no stake, stepping in would make it the "international busybody" the cases warn against (Akai).

The "negotiate first" clause

Clause: "The parties shall first negotiate in good faith for 30 days, then mediate in Singapore, and only then refer the dispute to the Singapore courts." On day 5 of the dispute, Beta sues Alpha in Ruritania.

  • Breach? Yes: Beta skipped the agreed steps (Finaport).

✎ WORKED EXAMPLE
SNIA in plain words

A helicopter crashed in Brunei. The widow and estate of a passenger sued the French manufacturer, SNIA, in Texas as well as Brunei. In Brunei, SNIA could claim contribution from the Malaysian operator to share the blame, because the operator would submit to Brunei; it would not submit to Texas. Brunei was the natural forum, and letting Texas go ahead would take away SNIA's way to make the operator pay its share. That lost remedy made the Texas suit oppressive, so the ASI issued, with SNIA giving undertakings to preserve the plaintiffs' Texas advantages in Brunei.

The general lesson: vexation is not "the foreign court is worse". It is "the foreign suit does something unfair to the applicant", like stripping a defence or remedy it would have in the natural forum.

✎ WORKED EXAMPLE
Concurrent or consecutive?

Alpha won a Singapore judgment against Beta in June 2025 on whether Beta's shipments were defective.

  • Claim X, filed by Beta in Ruritania in March 2025 (before the Singapore judgment): "Alpha's rejection of the shipments was wrongful." Raised before judgment, so it is concurrent: treat it like a non-contract case and show Singapore is the natural forum (J6.2.3).
  • Claim Y, filed in September 2025 (after judgment): "Alpha conspired to injure Beta by rejecting the shipments." A new label on the decided issue, so it is consecutive relitigation: Singapore's interest is self-evident, and Beta's foreign advantage is no answer (BCS v Baker).
  • Claim Z, filed in September 2025 about a new contract made after the judgment: genuinely new facts, so not restrained.

The date that matters is when each specific claim was raised abroad, not when Beta's Ruritanian action as a whole began.

Follow the judgment, not the footnote

Per BCS v Baker, concurrent v consecutive turns on when the specific claim was raised abroad. C&Y's footnote states this the other way; follow the judgment.

✎ WORKED EXAMPLE
Neutralising the respondent's advantage

Beta says that if its Ruritanian suit is stopped, it loses two things: Ruritania's wider discovery, and a limitation period that has now expired in Singapore. Alpha offers undertakings: to consent to equivalent discovery in Singapore, and not to rely on the Singapore time bar. With those advantages neutralised, nothing unjust is left for Beta, and the ASI may issue (SNIA; Lakshmi).

Had Beta manufactured the advantage, for example by waiting until the Singapore period expired before suing anywhere, it would count for little anyway (BCS v Baker, citing VKC).
