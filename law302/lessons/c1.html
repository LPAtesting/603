Choice of law methodology: characterise, connect, apply

⚡ BIG IDEA

Choice of law is a three-step machine: characterise the issue (contract? tort? property? procedure?), pick the connecting factor for that category, and apply the law it points to. Procedure always stays with Singapore law. Foreign law must be pleaded and proved like a fact; if no one pleads it, Singapore law applies. Two overlays can override the chosen law: Singapore's mandatory rules and its fundamental public policy.

C1.1 STARTER: CHARACTERISATION
C1.1.1 Global Starter

Per Macmillan (endorsed in Philippines v Maler), to find the law governing [the issue, e.g., validity of the contract / liability for the accident / title to the shares], the court (1) characterises it as [category], (2) selects the conflict rule's [connecting factor], and (3) applies [law X]. Stages 2 and 3 are plainly for the lex fori, so [the dispute] turns on stage 1.

  • Which Law: Lex Fori Per HMRC v Shahdadpuri, the lex fori characterises [the issue], as using [the potential leges causae] is circular (they are found only after characterising) and invites [cumulation / gap] (C1.1.4).
  • Enlightened Lex Fori Per Raiffeisen, it characterises in a "broad internationalist spirit", and per Auld LJ in Macmillan, unconstrained by [domestic distinctions], so [an agreement without consideration] is still contractual (Re Bonacina). [Foreign law] may inform [the category] (cf Red Sea).
  • Issue, Not Cause of Action Per Murakami Takako and JIO Minerals, the court looks "beyond the formulation of the claim" to the true issue, so [claimant]'s framing as [restitution / constructive trust] cannot steer [the governing law]. In Macmillan, [the restitution claim] was really about [priority over the shares], for [the lex situs].
  • Categories Are Man-Made; Stages Interact Per Raiffeisen, categories are "man-made, not natural" and may be redefined or extended, and the stages are not taken "in turn and in isolation", so characterising [the issue] as [category A] is tested against [the law it selects].
    • Use (+): [party] argues [category A] yields [an inappropriate law, e.g., the lex situs of a fortuitously located debt], favouring [category B], as Raiffeisen preferred [the insurance contract's law] over [the French lex situs].
    • Use (−): [counterparty] calls this result-driven, so [the established category] applies.
  • Why Foreign Law At All Per Goh Suan Hee, applying [law X] deters [claimant] from forum shopping for [a higher award here], and he who travels to [country X] must accept its law, protecting [the parties'] expectations and uniform outcomes.
  • Jurisdiction-Selecting, Not Rule-Selecting Singapore's rules pick [law X] through [the connecting factor] whatever its content (contrast the US interest analysis in Babcock v Jackson), so [party] escapes [law X] only via [re-characterisation (C1.1) / the tort flexible exception (C3.6) / a mandatory rule (C1.7) / public policy (C1.8)].

C1.1.4 Rule Characterisation, Cumulation and Gap

(use where competing rules clash, e.g., presumptions, caps, formalities)

  • Parallel Exercise Per Auld LJ in Macmillan, issue characterisation needs a parallel classification of the rule, as in Haugesund Kommune, where [the capacity issue] led to [Norwegian law] and the court then asked whether [the Norwegian provision] was a capacity rule.
  • Application Per Re Cohn, [the forum rule] is [substantive / procedural] because it [goes to the existence of the right / merely regulates proof], as is [the foreign rule]. In Re Cohn both [survivorship presumptions] were substantive, so only [the German lex causae's] applied.
    • Cumulation: if [the foreign rule] is substantive and [the forum rule] procedural, both apply and clash, so [party] urges re-characterising [the forum rule] to leave [the lex causae's rule].
    • Gap: if [the foreign rule] is procedural and [the forum rule] substantive, neither applies, so [party] urges re-characterising in a broad internationalist spirit (Raiffeisen) to fill [the gap].

  • Rappo / Perry Per Rappo and Perry, [the fiduciary / trust claims] founded on [the oral agreement / the AMAs] are contractual, so [Swiss law] governs. Per Perry, the quality, not quantity, of connecting factors makes [the contract] outweigh [the situs of the money / the place of incorporation].
  • Caveats Rickshaw left open a separate equitable category for [novel claims], and per Raiffeisen [a new category] may be recognised.
C1.2 SUBSTANCE vs PROCEDURE
C1.2.1 Starter

Per CIFG v Polimet, substance follows the lex causae [law X] and procedure the lex fori, as it is convenient for [the forum] to apply its own procedure, so if [the matter] is procedural Singapore law applies without COL analysis.

  • Test Per Star City, by [the provision]'s effect and purpose, a rule on the existence of [the right] is substantive, while one that regulates proceedings to enforce [it] is procedural.
C1.2.2 Applying the Star City Test

(run both scenarios; critique for the losing party)

  • Scenario A: Substantive (+) Per Star City, [provision, e.g., "no obligation exists to pay sums won on a wager"] negates [the right], so [lex causae] applies.
  • Scenario B: Procedural (−) Per Star City, [provision, e.g., "no action shall be brought"] bars only enforcement of [the right], so Singapore law applies.
C1.2.3 Critique: The Functional Approach

(run for the losing party)

  • Functional Critique Per C&Y, the wide procedural category reflects old judicial parochialism, now tempered by FNC, so [losing party] argues procedure covers only the "mode or conduct of court proceedings" (Goh Suan Hee), as in [Australia (Pfeiffer) / Canada (Tolofson) / Hong Kong (First Laser)], making [the provision] substantive.
  • Consequence [Quantum / remedies / a writing requirement] would then follow [lex causae], curbing forum shopping (Goh Suan Hee), though [party] must plead and prove more [foreign law], at greater cost.
  • Status Per CIFG v Polimet, Star City remains law since Goh Suan Hee left this open, so apply Star City to [the provision] first and the functional view in the alternative for [the losing party].
C1.3 REMEDIES
C1.3.1 Final Remedies

(run traditional then modern)

  • Traditional: Procedural Per Phrantzes v Argenti and Shanghai Electric, the court grants only its own remedies, so [the constructive trust / tracing / specific performance] is [available / unavailable] as Singapore law [recognises / does not recognise] it.
  • Modern: Lex Causae Decides Entitlement Per Goh Suan Hee, it is "untenable in principle to separate remedy from rights", so [lex causae] decides whether, and what kind of, [remedy] lies for [claimant].
  • Rappo / Perry Per Rappo, [the constructive trust / tracing] was unavailable even in Singapore under [Swiss law], remedies being "a function of the law that governs the relevant relationship". Per Perry, [the trust claim] failed as [Swiss law] does not recognise trusts.
    • FNC link: per Rappo, [the remedy's absence in country X] is then no substantial injustice at [Spiliada stage 2] (J4).
  • Synthesis: Functional Equivalent Per C&Y, [lex causae] decides entitlement, but the court grants only remedies known to the lex fori, so for [the foreign remedy] unknown here it grants [the functional equivalent], if any.
C1.3.2 Interim Remedies (ASI / MI)
  • General: Procedural Per C&Y, [the Mareva / interlocutory ASI] protects the court's own processes, so the lex fori governs its grant against [respondent].
  • Exception: Substantive Injunction Per Shanghai Electric, restraining [a call on an on-demand bond] is substantive, as it deprives [the beneficiary] of immediate payment, so [the bond's governing law] decides, subject to [the remedy] being known to the lex fori.
  • Carve-Out: Comity Per C&Y, interim relief raising comity, such as [the ASI against proceedings in country X], stays solely with the lex fori, whatever [the lex causae] says.
C1.4 DAMAGES
  • Traditional Split Per Boys v Chaplin and Harding v Wealands, heads ([pain and suffering / loss of profits / dependency]) are substantive, so [the head] must exist under [lex causae], but quantum is procedural, so [claimant] recovers [the Singapore amount].
  • Critique Per Goh Suan Hee (obiter), the split is "wholly artificial", so if quantum is substantive [claimant] recovers [the amount under lex causae] (cf The "Reecon Wolf").
    • Reasons: it encourages forum shopping for [a higher award], making outcomes a matter of chance, and he who travels to [country X] accepts its law, including quantum.
    • Support: [claimant / defendant] cites Art 15(c) Rome II and Pfeiffer v Rogerson (HCA), which apply [the lex loci delicti] to quantum.
    • C&Y: [the foreign legislature] links liability to quantum, e.g., [a cap tied to insurance costs], so severing them distorts [lex causae], and quantifying [a head unknown here] is hard.
    • Status: left open, the CA cautioning [claimants] against suing here for a larger award; in Goh Suan Hee [the damages gap] was a neutral FNC factor (J4).
  • Law vs Fact (if quantum is substantive) per Goh Suan Hee, [lex causae]'s rules of law ([the statutory cap / case-law scale]) apply, but facts ([local social and economic conditions]) remain for the forum.
  • Remoteness and Mitigation Per D'Almeida Araujo v Becker, remoteness is substantive though it affects quantum, as (per C&Y) is mitigation, so [lex causae] decides whether [the loss] is too remote or [the failure to mitigate] bars it.
  • Contributory Negligence open; it goes to the extent of liability, so [defendant] argues it is substantive on a functional view (cf remoteness), applying [lex causae]'s [apportionment rule].
C1.5 LIMITATION PERIODS
C1.5.1 Common Law Position

(now superseded by the FLPA; context only)

  • Extinguish vs Bar Per BBA v BAZ, a statute extinguishing the right is substantive, following [lex causae], but one barring the remedy is procedural, so Singapore's [limitation period] applied to [the claim].
    • Substantive wording (+): [the foreign statute] reads ["D shall be absolved from liability unless suit is brought within 3 years"].
    • Procedural wording (−): [the foreign statute] reads ["all actions shall be commenced within 3 years"].
    • Counter: per BBA v BAZ, the split breeds gap and cumulation between [the foreign period] and [Singapore's], prompting the FLPA.
C1.5.2 FLPA: General Rule

(applies equally to equitable claims)

  • s 3(1) + s 6(2) Per s 3(1) FLPA, only [lex causae]'s limitation law applies, not Singapore's, and per s 6(2) its [procedural / substantive] label is irrelevant, so [the claim] [is / is not] barred.
    • Renvoi: excluded (ss 3(5), 6(2)), so only [lex causae]'s domestic period applies, not [its COL rules].
C1.5.3 FLPA: Tort Cases
  • s 3(2) Per s 3(2) FLPA, a foreign law considered "for the purpose of actionability" is deemed to apply under s 3(1), a "clear reference" to double actionability (IM Skaugen), so [the claim] [is / is not] barred under [the lex loci delicti's period].
    • Flexible exception: if it selects [a single lex causae], that law's period applies, being the Limitation Act if [Singapore law] governs.
    • Contrast England: per Metall, s 1(2) UK FLPA 1984 applies both [the English] and [the foreign] periods to double actionability matters, whereas Singapore's s 3(2) applies only [the lex loci delicti's period] (C3.9.2).
C1.5.4 Public Policy: s 4(2) Undue Hardship
  • Undue Hardship Per s 4(2) FLPA, [the foreign period] is contrary to public policy if it causes [a party / potential party] undue hardship. Per Recovery Vehicle 1 (adopting The Pegasus), that is hardship "greater than the circumstances warrant", even for [a claimant at fault] where the consequences are disproportionate to its fault.
    • Factors: per Recovery Vehicle 1, the objective reasonableness of [the foreign bar] (being shorter than Singapore's is not enough), the complexity of [the claim], and [claimant]'s justification for letting time run.
  • Limit: Causation Per Recovery Vehicle 1, the hardship must flow from [the foreign period] itself, not from [the assignor's directors' refusal to sue].
  • Self-Induced Hardship (−) Per Recovery Vehicle 1, [the assignee] who [bought the debts] knowing they might be time-barred suffers no undue hardship, so [the plea] fails.
C1.5.5 Equitable Claims and Laches
  • No Laches Carve-Out Unlike s 4(3) UK FLPA 1984, the FLPA has no laches carve-out, consistent with COL for equity (Rickshaw), so [lex causae]'s time bar applies to [the equitable claim].
    • Backstop: per C&Y, if [claimant]'s delay is unacceptably long though within [the foreign period], [the foreign statute] may be disapplied on Singapore public policy.
C1.6 EVIDENCE AND PROOF OF FOREIGN LAW
C1.6.1 Characterisation of Evidential Matters
  • What / How Divide Per C&Y, what must be proved is substantive ([lex causae]) and how is procedural, so admissibility of [the Indonesian notarised deed] is for the lex fori (Banque Indosuez).
  • Burden of Proof Per Re Fuld's Estate (No 3), generally procedural, so [Singapore's allocation] applies despite [lex causae].
  • Writing Requirements s 6 CLA 1909 bars any "action" on [a guarantee / disposition of immovables] without signed writing, so per Leroux v Brown it is procedural and binds [the foreign-law contract] here.
    • Counter: per C&Y, on a functional view (C1.2.3) [the writing rule] may be substantive, following [the proper law] (C2.7).
  • Parol Evidence Discrepancy Per Korner v Witkowitzer, evidence to vary [the contract] is procedural, but per St Pierre evidence to interpret it is substantive, a split C&Y find hard to justify, which [party] can [exploit / attack].
  • SICC Carve-Out Per s 18K SCJA (SICC O 13 r 15), on [party]'s application [the SICC] may disapply Singapore evidence rules and apply [foreign rules], including on privilege and taking of evidence.
C1.6.2 Pleading and Proving Foreign Law
  • Pleading Per Brownlie and The "Chem Orchid", [party] relying on [law X] must plead and prove it, with no judicial notice. Per Goh Chok Tong v Tang Liang Hong, pleading is not obligatory (save for [immovables / registered interests / incorporation]), so [party] pleads only where [law X is more advantageous].
  • Fact of a Peculiar Kind Per EFT Holdings, [law X] is fact "of a peculiar kind", more open to appellate review, so where [law X] resembles Singapore law the appellate court may form [its own view].
  • Two Modes of Proof Per Pacific Recreation, [official statute books / law reports] under s 40 EA, or [expert opinion] on content and effect, including how the foreign court reads [the statute], under s 47 EA.
    • Raw sources: admissible here, unlike the traditional English expert-only rule (but see Brownlie), though per AQCEL Synergies the court need not give weight to [raw HK sources] where [the provision's reach] is unclear.
  • Construing a Foreign Contract Per Pacific Recreation, [the expert] proves [law X]'s rules of construction without pushing personal views, and the court construes [the contract].
  • Expert Preferable Per Pacific Recreation, where [law X] is [from a different tradition], has no Singapore equivalent, or [the issue] is complex or controversial.
  • Similar Laws Per TMT v RBS, it is "odd" to prove [English law] as foreign law, so parties may submit directly on [construction], flagging only "real and relevant" differences.
  • Foreign-Language Statute Per Byers v Saudi National Bank (per C&Y), an expert is crucial for [a foreign-language statute on unfamiliar concepts], but not for [an English-language statute on similar concepts].
  • Criticism of Experts Per Wu Yang, partisan experts are "singularly unhelpful", hence [a common expert] (O 12 r 3(1) ROC 2021; SICC O 14 r 2(4)(a)) or, rarely, [a court expert] (O 12 r 3(3)).
C1.6.3 Default Rule and Presumption of Similarity

(first ask: was foreign law pleaded at all?)

  • Not Pleaded: Default Rule Per Brownlie, if neither party pleads [law X], Singapore law applies in its own right (adversarial procedure, arguably tacit agreement), not by presumption. Per EFT Holdings, no conflicts issue arises, so [the claim] is decided under [Singapore law].
  • Pleaded but Unproved Per Brownlie, once [law X] is pleaded the court must apply it, so [party] failing to prove its case under it may simply lose (EFT Holdings), unless [law X] is presumed similar to Singapore law (The "Chem Orchid").
  • Nature of the Presumption Per Brownlie, a rule of evidence on content, applied only where fair and reasonable to expect [law X] to be materially similar. Per D'Oz, a "rule of convenience", refused where holding [defendant] liable under [unproved law X] is unjust.
  • When (In)Appropriate Per Brownlie, it suits [a common law system], "great and broad" principles and [interlocutory stages, e.g., service out / stay] more than trial, and is less apt for [a statutory rule], though the question is whether [law X] has a materially similar rule (rejecting Shaker v Al-Bedrawi).
  • Limits the presumption of [law X]'s similarity gives way for [party] where:
    • Tactical reliance: [the burdened party] pleads [law X] then rests on the presumption, which cannot improve its position and may be an abuse (Tamil Nadu).
    • Gaps: not a bar; per Brownlie, where [the foreign statute] is proved but not [its interpretation], similar interpretive principles are presumed.
    • Foreign public policy: per PT Jaya Putra v Guthrie, [Indonesian public policy] is best decided by [the Indonesian court], so it is not presumed.
    • Different traditions: per C&Y, not for [tax law] or [a civil law system] lacking universal principles; per AQCEL Synergies, refused where evidence showed [HK law] differs.
    • Intrusive relief: per EFT Holdings explaining OMG Holdings, no [injunction against conduct in country X] on a bare presumption, though per C&Y presumably [damages] may be had.
  • FNC Overlay Per Rickshaw and JIO Minerals, likely differences in unproved [law X] still carry weight at the FNC stage, so [the foreign governing law] points to [country X] (J4).
C1.6.4 Innovation: References to Foreign Courts (O 29 / O 29A ROC 2021)
  • O 29: Specified Courts Per O 29 ROC 2021 (SICC O 15), on [a party's application] the court may order proceedings in [a "specified court", e.g., NSW / Bermuda / DIFC] on [its law], or of its own motion in [a non-specified court], backed by MOUs (also New York).
  • O 29A: China Per O 29A ROC 2021 (SICC O 15A, from 3 April 2022), [a question of PRC law] goes court-to-court to the Supreme People's Court, though who bears [the costs] is unclear.
  • Westacre Origin In Westacre v Yugoimport, as experts diametrically disagreed on whether [the English judgment] was enforceable in England (a RECJA precondition), the CA adjourned for [an English ruling], which Tomlinson J gave.
    • Limits: per C&Y, [foreign courts] may refuse an advisory role, and [the parties] bear foreign costs on top, so few start it voluntarily (in Ng Teck Sim, both appealed against [a Thai-law referral order]).
C1.6.5 SICC: Foreign Law on Submissions
  • Submissions Instead of Proof Per s 18L SCJA (SICC O 16 r 8), [the SICC] may decide [law X] on submissions instead of proof by [counsel / a restricted-registration foreign lawyer / a registered law expert].
    • Advantages: cost-efficient, as in [arbitration], avoids an artificial fact / law split, and suits [the international bench familiar with law X].
    • Extending it generally: [party] weighs [accuracy and cost savings] against the fact-based tradition and [the judge's unfamiliarity with law X].
C1.7 FORUM MANDATORY RULES (overlay regardless of the lex causae; single home for contract (C2.9) and tort)
  • Starter Per C&Y, overriding mandatory rules are so crucial to Singapore's [political / social / economic] interests that parties cannot contract out by choosing [law X] (cf Art 11(1) Hague Principles). Per Skilling, [the contract] breaching [a Singapore licensing statute] is unenforceable to the extent of the conflict, whatever [the proper law].
    • Effect: per C&Y, [the rule] is superimposed, [the proper law] governing the rest, though it may sink the whole contract, e.g., [a foreign gambling contract] under s 5(2) CLA.
    • Tiers: only the international tier overrides [a foreign proper law]; a domestic mandatory rule binds only if [Singapore law] governs.
    • Which laws count: per Sim See Kee, illegality at [the place of contracting / residence / incorporation] is irrelevant, and [the place of performance] enters only via Foster / Ralli / Lemenda (C2.10).
  • Express Scope First Per C&Y, [the statute] may spell out its reach (e.g., s 27(2) UCTA); absent that, whether it overrides [law X] is statutory construction (JIO Minerals).
  • Parliamentary Intention Per JIO Minerals, [the statute] overrides [foreign law] if Parliament clearly intended so, as it may legislate extraterritorially, COL rules being "useful tools" to ascertain [its geographical scope].
  • Two Aspects of the Presumption Per JIO Minerals, the presumption against extraterritoriality has two overlapping aspects, both yielding to clear intention that [the statute] reach [the foreign-law issue]:
    • Choice of law aspect: [the statute] applies only if Singapore law governs [the issue], so as [UAE law (contract)] or [Indonesian law (tort)] governed in JIO, the Misrepresentation Act did not apply.
    • Territorial aspect: [the statute] reaches only [conduct] within Singapore, unless displaced by clear, possibly implied, intention (Parno), as for [the TCM Practitioners Act] (Huang Danmin).
  • Gauging Intention Per JIO Minerals, [a conduct-regulating / protective statute, e.g., s 37 PCA / the TCM Practitioners Act] more readily applies extraterritorially, unlike [one merely supplementing common law remedies, e.g., the MA].
  • Types of Mandatory Rule [the Singapore statute] relied on by [party] is either:
    • Overriding: applies irrespective of the proper law, e.g., [export / import controls] and [s 5(2) CLA on foreign gambling contracts].
    • Conditional: per s 27(2) UCTA, UCTA applies despite [the foreign choice] if (a) [the term] was imposed wholly or mainly to evade it, or (b) [a party] dealt as consumer while habitually resident here and the essential steps for contracting were taken here, [by him or others on his behalf].
      • Contrast bona fide (C2.2.2): "wholly or mainly" is wider than "sole purpose", and [the chosen law] survives subject to the Act, whereas [a non-bona fide choice] is struck out.
      • Flip side: per s 27(1) UCTA, if [Singapore law] governs only by [the parties' choice], ss 2–7 do not apply.
  • Examples
    • s 5(2) CLA (Gambling) per C&Y, overriding, so [the foreign casino's claim] fails whatever [its proper law] (Star City), its "no action" wording also making it procedural (C1.2); for [foreign gambling judgments], see Poh Soon Kiat (J7).
    • Misrepresentation Act per JIO Minerals, the MA reaches [misrepresentations abroad] only if [the contract] has a Singapore proper law, as it merely supplements common law remedies, so [a misrepresentation in Indonesia] under [a UAE-law contract] falls outside it.
    • s 18F SCJA (SICC Exclusivity) where [a non-CCAA SICC agreement] is governed by [foreign law], it is debated whether s 18F applies as a forum mandatory rule or only if [Singapore law] governs (J5).
C1.8 FUNDAMENTAL PUBLIC POLICY OF THE FORUM
  • Fundamental Public Policy Per C&Y, the court refuses [the offending foreign rule] or [the contract] only if it infringes Singapore's fundamental (international) public policy, narrower than domestic policy, so [the rule / contract] being [void / objectionable] domestically is insufficient (cf Art 11(3) Hague Principles: "manifestly incompatible").
  • Examples Per Royal Boskalis, [a contract procured by extreme duress, e.g., a human-shield threat], and per Foster v Driscoll, [a contract to break a friendly state's law], whereas [economic duress] stays with the proper law (Dimskal) (C2.10).
  • Proper Law's Own Rule Per Kahler, a contract illegal under [the proper law] is unenforceable, unless [that illegality rule] itself offends Singapore public policy (The Playa Larga; Re Helbert Wagg).
  • Distinguish Forum Mandatory Rules (C1.7)
    • Direction: per C&Y, [the Singapore statute] applies positively, with its own scope, while public policy negatively filters [the offending foreign rule].

1Start here: C1 in plain English

Question 2 usually begins: "Assume the Singapore court will assume jurisdiction." So there is no jurisdiction fight. The question is: which country's law decides each issue? C1 is the general toolkit you use for every issue; C2 (contract) and C3 (tort) are the specific rules.

★ IN PLAIN ENGLISH

Imagine a sorting office for parcels from around the world. First you read the label on each parcel to sort it into the right bin: contract, tort, property, procedure (characterisation). Each bin has an address rule: contracts go to the law the parties chose; land goes to where the land is (the connecting factor). Then you deliver the parcel to that country's law (apply the law). A few parcels never leave the building: anything about how the court runs its own business (procedure) is always handled by Singapore. And a few foreign laws are sent back as unacceptable (public policy).

✎ WORKED EXAMPLE
One issue through all three stages

Alpha (Singapore) sues Beta (Italy). In Italy, Beta promised in writing to supply spare parts free for five years. Alpha gave nothing in return. Beta says the promise is not binding.

  1. Characterise (stage 1). Is "does this promise bind?" a contract issue? Singapore domestic law would say "no consideration, so no contract", but the court uses an enlightened lex fori, not its domestic rules. A promise intended to bind is still "contractual" for conflict purposes, even without consideration (Re Bonacina). So: contract.
  2. Connect (stage 2). The connecting factor for contract is the proper law. Say the agreement chose Italian law.
  3. Apply (stage 3). Italian law decides whether the promise binds. If Italian law has no consideration requirement, Beta is bound, even though a purely Singapore promise would not be.

Notice that the whole answer turned on stage 1. Had the court characterised with its domestic eyes, it would never have reached Italian law at all.

✎ WORKED EXAMPLE
Gap and cumulation, with time limits

A contract claim is governed by Ruritanian law. It is brought in Singapore. Both countries have a time limit. (This is the old common law position, before the FLPA fixed it: C1.5.)

  • Cumulation. Singapore labels its own time limit procedural, so it applies in every Singapore case. Ruritania labels its time limit substantive, so it applies as part of the governing law. Both apply, and the claimant must beat the shorter one, a result neither country intended.
  • Gap. Now flip the labels. Singapore's limit is substantive, so it applies only when Singapore law governs, which it does not. Ruritania's limit is procedural, so it applies only in Ruritanian courts, which this is not. Neither applies, and the claim has no time limit at all.

The fix in both cases is to re-characterise one rule, in a broad internationalist spirit, so that exactly one time limit applies.

★ IN PLAIN ENGLISH

Two gambling statutes, two answers

C1.5.2 FLPA: General Rule

(applies equally to equitable claims)

  1. Contract. A supply contract governed by Indonesian law; the Indonesian period has run, Singapore's would not have. Under s 3(1), only the Indonesian period applies, however Indonesian law labels it (s 6(2)). The claim is barred in Singapore, unless the claimant shows undue hardship (s 4(2)).

★ IN PLAIN ENGLISH

Foreign law is treated like a fact the parties must prove, the way they prove what happened. If neither side mentions foreign law, the judge simply uses Singapore law: nobody asked for anything else. If a side says "Ruritanian law applies" but never proves what Ruritanian law says, the judge may assume it is the same as Singapore law, but only where that is a fair guess (similar legal traditions, broad principles). Where it is not a fair guess, the side that needed Ruritanian law loses.

Hypo: the Thai bus accident

A Singapore tourist is injured on a tour bus in Thailand run by a Thai company. Thai law caps damages for pain and suffering; Singapore law does not. The claim is brought in Singapore 4 years after the accident; Thailand's limitation period is 1 year.

  3. Limitation: tort, so FLPA s 3(2): the Thai period applies (or the single chosen law's under the flexible exception). The claim is barred unless the claimant shows undue hardship under s 4(2) (C1.5).
  4. Proof: if neither side pleads Thai law, Singapore law applies in its own right; if pleaded but unproved, the presumption of similarity is weak for a statutory cap in a civil law system (C1.6).
