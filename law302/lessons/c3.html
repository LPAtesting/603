Tort choice of law: double actionability and the flexible exception

⚡ BIG IDEA

A tort claim must be actionable under BOTH Singapore law (as if everything happened here) AND the law of the place where the tort was in substance committed. The claimant proves the first limb; the defendant must plead and prove that the foreign law gives no liability. A strictly applied flexible exception can displace either law, or both, where it is purely fortuitous.

C3.1 STARTER: THE GOVERNING RULE
C3.1.1 Global Starter

The issue is which law governs [claimant]'s claim for [negligence / misrepresentation / conspiracy / defamation / conversion] arising from [acts] in [country X]. Per Rickshaw, it is double actionability subject to a flexible exception (Boys v Chaplin; Red Sea).

  • The Two Limbs Per Lord Wilberforce in Boys v Chaplin (adopted in Rickshaw), [the wrong] must be (a) a tort under Singapore law (C3.2) and (b) give civil liability between the actual parties under [law X] (C3.3).
    • Not necessarily tortious: per Ong Ghee Soon, any civil liability under [law X] suffices, e.g. [a civil-law delict / statutory liability].
    • Defences: per C&Y, [a defence] under either law defeats [the claim].
  • Common Denominator absent displacement, [claimant] recovers only what both laws allow (Yeo), as in McElroy v McAllister where [the widow] got only funeral expenses, so [claimant] argues displacement (C3.6).
  • How Many Leges Causae? per C&Y, the rule names no single law: two generally, one if the exception displaces [the lex fori] (Red Sea) or [the lex loci delicti] (Boys v Chaplin), and [a third law] if both go (C3.6.4).
  • Burden of Proof Per EFT Holdings, [claimant] proves limb (a), while [defendant] must plead and prove non-actionability under [law X], limb (b) existing for [defendant]'s benefit.
    • Dominance: per C&Y, this burden model and the default rule (C3.5) make [the lex fori] the dominant limb over [law X].
    • Reversal: where [claimant] invokes the exception to apply [law X] (Red Sea), [claimant] must plead and prove [law X].
  • Characterisation Per C&Y, "tort" is not bound by [domestic categories], though [a domestic tort] normally qualifies, and some equitable wrongs may count (Rickshaw), e.g. [dishonest assistance] (Grupo Torras; C3.11).
    • Foreign law may inform it: where [claimant] relies solely on [law X] for [a liability unknown here], [law X]'s characterisation matters (Red Sea).
  • Framing / Concurrent Liability Per Rickshaw, absent bad faith [claimant] may frame in tort despite [the law Y clause], the claim being characterised as tort though it arises from [the contract].
    • Contrast: [equitable claims] rooted in [the contract] follow contract COL (C1.1.5), and [a "parasitic" tort] may follow [the contract's law] (C3.6.5).
  • Rationale (evaluative) limb (b) spares [defendant] not liable where it acted (EFT) and serves comity, though per Metall comity weakens where [the damage] is caused to [persons here]. Limb (a), from The Halley, is "parochial" (C&Y).
  • Development (evaluative) Phillips v Eyre's "not justifiable" limb, abused in Machado v Fontes ([criminal liability] sufficing), became civil liability in Boys v Chaplin, the proper law of the tort being rejected (Red Sea), so [claimant] cannot plead [the most connected law] directly.

C3.3 LIMB 2: CIVIL LIABILITY UNDER THE LEX LOCI DELICTI (LOCATING THE TORT)
C3.3.1 Starter: The Substance Test

[Defendant] pleads that [law X] imposes no liability because of [no duty for pure economic loss / a damages cap / a statutory defence / a shorter limitation period], so the tort must first be located.

  • The Test Per Distillers and Metall, look back over [the events] and ask where in substance the cause of action arose, finding one single place on [the elements as recognised by the lex fori] (C&Y).
  • As Pleaded per Vibrant Group, the weight of [act / reliance / damage] depends on [the tort pleaded], so [the place of reliance] counts more for [misrepresentation] than for [negligence] (C3.3.2).
  • Single Locality (+) all elements occurred in [country X], e.g. [the place of the motor accident] (C&Y), so ask only whether [law X] imposes liability.
  • Multiple Locality (−) [the act] and [the damage] span [X] and [Y], so apply the tort-specific rules (C3.3.2).
  • Damage Not Determinative per Distillers, the place of damage "may be quite fortuitous", so [claimant]'s loss at [its home base] does not by itself locate [the tort].
  • Policy Favouring the Forum (+) per Metall, the court need not "turn a blind eye" to [damage] caused to [persons here] by acts abroad, so [receipt / loss] in Singapore may be the substance.
  • Civil, Not Necessarily Tortious, Liability per Ong Ghee Soon, [negligent misstatement] was actionable under Art 41 SCO though Swiss law has no "law of negligence" category, so [law X]'s [delictual / statutory] liability suffices.
  • Same Test for Jurisdiction per Metall (citing Armagas), the same locus test serves [the tort gateway]. Whether double actionability applies at [service out] is open: yes per IM Skaugen (HC), no per Nippon Catalyst, left open in Shen Sophie (J3).
    • Divergence: the gateway needs only [an act partly / damage] here (Yeo), so [the gateway] may be met though [the tort] is located in [country X].
    • FNC: per JIO, the place of the tort is prima facie the natural forum unless fortuitous, so locating it in [country X] favours [a stay] (J4).
C3.3.2 Tort-Specific Rules for Multi-Locality Torts

(select the tort pleaded)

  • Negligence: Focus on the Act Per Vibrant Group, the place of [the negligent act] prevails, so [the audit / advice] performed in [country X] locates the tort there despite [reliance and loss] in Singapore.
    • Negligent services / advice: where [the services] were or ought to have been [rendered] (Voth).
    • Trading decisions: where [the decision to speculate] was made, e.g. [Moscow] (Base Metal Trading).
    • Product liability: where [the product] was [marketed without the warning], not where [it was made] or [the damage] occurred (Distillers; Castree v Squibb).
    • Reliance weighs less: in [negligence], reliance is only one route to [a duty] (NTUC Foodfare; Vibrant).
  • Misrepresentation Directed at the Claimant: Receipt + Reliance Per JIO Minerals (tentatively, citing Yeo) and Ong Ghee Soon, [a representation] directed at [claimant] and received and acted on in [Singapore] is a tort there, unless [that place] is fortuitous.
    • Where reliance crystallises: where [claimant] gave [instructions via the Singapore branch], not [administrative execution in Geneva] (Ong Ghee Soon).
    • Fortuitous receipt: receipt in [India] merely because [claimant] is based there is fortuitous (JIO).
    • Receipt and reliance split: the specific rule fails, so use [the substance test], as in JIO where [negotiations and drilling] made [Indonesia] the place.
    • Several countries of making, receipt and reliance: where [the core representations] were made, e.g. [Germany], may prevail, receipt being fortuitous for [global operators] (IM Skaugen v MAN Diesel).
    • Receipt unclear on the pleadings: unevidenced receipt in [Germany] is "speculative", so [Singapore law] applies (Rickshaw).
  • Representation Made at Large per Cordova Land (and JIO), a statement to [the world at large] is a tort where made, unlike [one directed at the claimant] (The Albaforth).
  • Inducing Breach of Contract per Metall, the tort is where [the contract] was broken and [claimant] injured, e.g. [London], not where [the inducement] occurred ([New York]).
  • Conspiracy Per EFT Holdings, weigh [the conspirators' identity and location], where [they combined], [the concerted acts], [claimant]'s location and [where losses arose], as in EFT where [Taiwan] prevailed.
    • Parasitic conspiracy: one "so intimately connected" to [a contract] may follow [its law] without being located (Chen v BHNV; C3.6.5).
  • Defamation / Online / IP / Passing Off see C3.7 for [publication / download / the territorial right] in [the tort pleaded].

C3.3.4 Statutory Deeming: Online Harms and Haze

(claims under OSRAA or THPA)

  • OSRAA s 101: Tort Deemed in Singapore Per s 101 OSRAA 2025, a [Part 10 / 11 / 12] tort is treated as in Singapore if any element was (a) initiated in or from Singapore, (b) perceived here, or (c) caused [damage] here, so [the post viewed here] makes it a local tort.
    • Effect (Yeo): s 101 replaces the substance test with a statutory connecting factor, so both limbs point to [Singapore law], giving [the OSRAA torts] the character of overriding mandatory rules.
    • Jurisdiction (Yeo): it secures [nexus] and a factual presumption that Singapore is [forum conveniens], leaving FNC to sieve out [opportunistic foreign claims].
    • Exception: [defendant] may argue for [law X] where Singapore is so fortuitous that [serious injustice] results, but only exceptionally, given Parliament's intent to protect [Singapore residents] (Yeo).
    • No s 101 connection (rare): the substance test applies, [the tort] is likely abroad, and [claimant] recovers only the common denominator of [the OSRAA] and [law X] (Yeo).
    • Unilateral reading: s 101 may be a unilateral rule with no exception, but Yeo prefers [the multilateral reading] as [the wording] only redefines the connecting factor.
    • Critique (Yeo): if [law X] protects [the victim] better, s 101 still imposes [the lower OSRAA standard].
  • OSRAA s 102: No Contracting Out Per s 102, [exclusion / jurisdiction / arbitration terms] inconsistent with Parts 11 or 12 ([Administrators / Platforms]) are void unless fair and reasonable, Part 10 ([Communicators]) not being covered (C3.8.2).

  • THPA s 6(4): Limb 2 Abrogated Per s 6(4) THPA 2014, [conduct in or outside Singapore] causing haze is actionable "whether or not" actionable abroad, so [defendant] cannot rely on [law X] for [burning in X].

    • Nature: an express statutory displacement of limb 2 for [haze claims], not a use of [the flexible exception]; per Chong (seminar), an overriding forum mandatory rule, so [the THPA claim] lies though [Indonesian law] imposes no liability.
    • Common law claim: per Chong (seminar), the substance test likely locates [the tort] in [Indonesia], where [the burning] occurred, so if [Indonesian law permits smallholder burning], limb 2 fails for [the negligence / nuisance claim] unless displaced (C3.6.3).

C3.6 THE FLEXIBLE EXCEPTION
C3.6.1 Starter

Per Rickshaw, the exception (Boys v Chaplin; Red Sea) may displace [the lex fori], [the lex loci delicti] or both for the law with the most significant relationship to [the issue] and the parties, so [party] argues [country X] is fortuitous because [factors].

  • Threshold Per Rickshaw, courts "must not be quick" to apply it unless [the displaced law] is purely fortuitous and applying it causes [injustice], on "clear and satisfying grounds" (Lord Wilberforce), lest it become [a proper law of the tort].
  • Strictly Applied per Rickshaw, it is "imperative" that [the exception] be strictly applied, favouring certainty over flexibility, so [party] needs [clear fortuity], not [a more favourable law].
  • Fortuity and Injustice: Conjunctive? unclear, but per C&Y applying [a fortuitous law] is itself [the injustice], so fortuity is the real test.
    • Stricter: than Red Sea and Ang Ming Chuang (weighing contacts) and Lord Wilberforce's interest analysis, so [claimant] leads with [fortuity].
    • Critique (C&Y): fortuity is too restrictive and should be one way of showing [an insignificant connection], so [party] argues [the weak connection] suffices.
  • Two Techniques in Boys Lord Hodson weighed [contacts] ("centre of gravity") while Lord Wilberforce ran an issue-specific [interest analysis] (C&Y), and Rickshaw adds fortuity.
  • Factors to Weigh per Restatement §145 (quoted in Red Sea), [the place of injury], [the place of conduct], [the parties' residence / incorporation] and [where the relationship is centred] point to [country Y].
  • Burden on the Invoking Party [the invoking party] must prove [the connecting factors] and [the foreign law] (Red Sea), a "vague and unclear" defence failing (Rickshaw).

  • Whole Claim per Red Sea, [one law] may govern the whole claim where "all or virtually all" factors point to it, as [the Saudi contracts, works, damage and head office] were "overwhelming", avoiding [fragmentation] (C&Y).

  • Counter-Factor that [the forum] applies [foreign law] where its own gives no remedy is a factor, not a bar (Red Sea), [fortuity] deciding under Rickshaw.

C3.7.1 Defamation

C3.7.2 Intellectual Property and Passing Off
  • Territoriality per C&Y, statutory [copyright / patent / trade mark] rights are territorial: conduct abroad cannot infringe [a Singapore right], nor conduct here [a Utopian right].
  • Validity Separate [validity] follows the law of [the place of creation], and [the Singapore court] may lack jurisdiction where infringement turns on [foreign validity] (C&Y).
  • Scenarios (forum Singapore; no conventions) run [the right's origin] against [the place of infringement]:
    • (i) Singapore right infringed abroad: fails both limbs, and [the lex fori] is not fortuitous for [a Singapore right].

1Start here: C3 in plain English

Singapore's tort choice of law rule is double actionability subject to a flexible exception (Rickshaw, adopting Boys v Chaplin and Red Sea). It is an old, unusual rule: the claim must pass two tests, one under Singapore law and one under the law of the place of the tort.

★ IN PLAIN ENGLISH

Imagine a referee who will only award a foul if it is a foul under both the home rulebook (Singapore law) and the rulebook of the stadium where it happened (the place of the tort). If either rulebook says "no foul", there is no foul. That makes the claimant win only what both rulebooks allow. But if one rulebook's involvement is pure accident (the players just happened to be in that stadium in transit), the referee can ignore it. That escape hatch is the flexible exception, and it is used sparingly.

  2. Admissibility (a tort question, double actionability): may a contract clause be raised as a defence to this negligence claim under Singapore law (limb 1) and under the law of the place of the tort (limb 2)? Both must allow it.
  3. Result: the clause defeats the claim only if it passes both steps. If either tort law refuses to let a clause of this kind exclude negligence liability for personal injury, the claim survives, even though the clause is valid in Dutch law.

The two-question split keeps contract questions with the contract's law and tort questions with the tort rule. That is why C&Y (following North) prefer it.
