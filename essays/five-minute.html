The question 2017-18 Q2 · 2020-21 Q2

"Individuals have the right to make the most fundamental decisions about their own lives, such as whether to bear a child and with whom to have an intimate relationship. Therefore, legislators should not enforce morality or regulate private behaviour." Critically comment, and say how far Hart, Devlin, Rawls and Finnis would agree.

The answer in one route

Recall mode hides everything after the crux. Say each part to yourself, then tap it to check.

Crux

Everything turns on "therefore". A right to decide about your own life does not reach every law touching morals. The hidden bridge is that only harm to others justifies coercion, so name it and test it.

Thesis

Define "enforce morality" (using law to secure conduct because it is right) and "private behaviour" (conduct that affects no one who has not consented). The statement is right that private consensual conduct should not be punished for immorality alone. It is wrong that the right bars every morals law, because the right is itself a moral claim, and law can regulate or promote without punishing.

  1. Section 1, "a right to decide"

    Hart gives a strong presumption, not an absolute. Rawls protects liberty as a political value, not autonomy as the highest good. Finnis calls the statement's argument "outright self-contradiction". The right cannot settle cases with a third party, such as a child.

  2. The hinge, "enforce"

    Promote, regulate, punish: the justification needed rises with the coercion used.

  3. Section 2, "harm to others"

    Hart goes wider than Mill (paternalism, public decency); Devlin sets no theoretical limit; Rawls tests reasons, not conduct. Harm is the right presumption, not the only ground, and what counts as harm is itself moral.

  4. Section 3, "morals law"

    Every law enforces some morality. What makes enforcement unjust is its reasons, its coercion and who bears it.

  5. Other side

    Best case: pluralism and humility, which show no doctrine should be official, not that no morals law is allowed. Weak point: Finnis's goods are accessible but not accepted by all.

  6. Repaired

    “Choices about one's intimate life deserve strong protection from punishment, but not immunity from all regulation or from the state's promotion of other goods.”

Memory lineA right to decide is a moral claim; enforcing is a ladder; harm is the bridge you must name.

Use it in a hypo · about a minute

Hypo 3, MP limb An MP's bill makes adultery an offence punishable by up to two years' imprisonment; the Minister says police will not proactively enforce it, as it is "a moral signpost". Grounds: every major religion condemns adultery; children of marriages broken by infidelity fare worse; a divided consultation, but a clear majority for the law. How would you vote?

Start with the right to decide (section 1). Adultery is an intimate choice, but it is not purely private: the spouse and the children are third parties, so the right alone cannot settle the bill. Then use the ladder (the hinge). Punishing is the top rung and needs the strongest reasons; promoting marriage would cost liberty far less. In section 3, attack the "moral signpost" idea. A law kept on the books but not enforced still carries the stigma, and it hands police and prosecutors what Hart calls "formidable discriminatory powers" (E2.3). Vote against the offence.

The question 2016-17 Q2

"Enforcing morality through law is not necessarily unjust or illegitimate. The only valid objections to it are moral and prudential, not sceptical." Discuss, with the theorists' views and current controversies. (2014-15 Q2 form (b): "Majority reasons are not necessarily legitimate or correct. However, the legislature may enforce the morality of society.")

The answer in one route

Recall mode hides everything after the crux. Say each part to yourself, then tap it to check.

Crux

The statement is largely right. Its flaw is that "sceptical" has two senses: denying that there is moral truth defeats itself, but doubting who knows it does not.

Thesis

Define "illegitimate" (power the state has no right to use, whatever its content) and "unjust" (content that wrongs those it binds). Enforcing morality is not illegitimate as such, and no theorist objects on sceptical grounds. But doubt about which moral claims are true is a real reason for restraint. It governs how the state enforces, not whether it may.

  1. Section 1, "enforcing morality"

    No law avoids enforcing some morality. Devlin and Finnis accept it; Hart and Rawls partly. Sin is not crime. What can make enforcement unjust is its reasons, its coercion and who bears it.

  2. Section 2, "not sceptical"

    The statement rightly sets aside the sceptic. Tolerance is itself a moral demand, and "we were wrong before" assumes a right answer. Scepticism does not even favour tolerance: on Devlin's relativism every society may enforce its morality.

  3. Section 3, "sceptical", the second sense

    TSH's second sceptic doubts who knows the moral facts. That doubt is real and works through prudence: move slowly, protect privacy, demand reasons beyond feeling, grade the coercion.

  4. Form (b)

    The majority settles who decides, not what is right. The Attorney-General in Lim Meng Suang relied on public morality, not popular feeling, and judges are no "mini-legislature".

  5. Other side

    Best case against: pluralism and humility. Weak point: denying truth and doubting who knows it can look alike, but the doubter can be shown wrong.

  6. Repaired

    “Enforcing morality is legitimate when its reasons are accessible and sound and its coercion proportionate. Scepticism is no objection, but fallibility is a reason for restraint.”

Memory lineDenying moral truth defeats itself; doubting who knows it counsels care, not silence.

Use it in a hypo · about a minute

Hypo 1, MP limb A bill bans all gambling, including at the two licensed casinos, with long prison terms and no exception save the charity lottery. Grounds: several religions call gambling a sin; problem gamblers fall into debt and crime; 78% "disapprove". How would you vote?

Sin is not crime. Yaacob Ibrahim (2005) noted that drinking and other activities Muslims consider vices are not banned, and Pritam Singh (2022) said that in a secular society sin and crime are separate. A poll in which 78% "disapprove" does not show that they want the conduct made a crime. The harms the MP cites, debt and crime, are real, but they support licensing, limits and help for gamblers, not jail for the patron. If asked for a current controversy, use the casinos (is gambling a form of play, E45.4).

The question Sample Q1 · 2025-26 Q1

"Legislators should treat all moral opinions as equally valid. This can be achieved by restricting liberty only when it is objectively necessary to protect others from a wrongdoer. Only then will the law avoid imposing a fixed conception of the good life." Discuss with reference to Rawls, Finnis, Hart and Devlin, with illustrations.

The answer in one route

Recall mode hides everything after the crux. Say each part to yourself, then tap it to check.

Crux

The statement is a chain: a goal (treat all opinions as equally valid), a means (coerce only to protect others), and a consequence (no fixed conception of the good). Chains are weakest where the links join, so test each link.

Thesis

The statement is right that no doctrine should be made official. It is wrong that a harm-only rule achieves equal validity, since words like "wrongdoer" already carry judgements, and wrong that it is the only route. What the law owes is equal respect for persons, not equal validity for opinions.

  1. Section 1, "equally valid"

    The phrase has two readings. If it means every opinion is equally true, it defeats itself. If it means no doctrine is privileged in advance, that is Rawls's view, but only until unreasonable doctrines are excluded. Devlin and Hart both reject equal validity. Fallibility counsels caution, not neutrality.

  2. Section 2, "only to protect others"

    Hart's harm principle goes wider than Mill's; Devlin rejects the limit; for Rawls a harm-only rule is Mill's comprehensive ideal. Consent is no defence to murder, which Hart explains as paternalism, and paternalism's targets are themselves moral choices.

  3. Section 3, "only then … no fixed conception"

    Devlin answers that some common morality is "the price of society". "Fixed" is a matter of degree: Finnis keeps only peace and justice, and Rawls filters reasons. In 2022 Singapore fixed the definition of marriage in Article 156 without imposing a whole way of life.

  4. Other side

    Best case: pluralism and humility. Weak point: Finnis's goods are accessible to reason but not accepted by everyone.

  5. Repaired

    “Legislators should treat all citizens, not all opinions, as equal, and coerce only on reasons every citizen can examine. For the legislator, that means giving reasons, not claiming neutrality.”

Memory lineEqual respect for persons, not equal validity for opinions; "fixed" is a matter of degree.

Use it in a hypo · about a minute

Hypo 16, MP limb Men of one minority faith may take up to four wives; everyone else faces seven years for bigamy. An MP's bill allows any three or more consenting adults to register a plural marriage. Grounds: the state has no business preferring one form of family life; plural unions harm no one and studies are inconclusive; the present law is arbitrary. An opponent says monogamy is "built into the house we live in", citing a 74% poll. How would you vote?

"No business preferring" one form of marriage asks for neutrality of outcome, which is impossible, since permitting and refusing both take a side. Rawls says monogamy "as such" is a comprehensive view, but the equality of women is a political reason the state may use. Devlin's "house we live in" is relativist, and it assumes one house when the law already recognises two forms. "Harms no one" raises the question of who counts: wives and children are third parties. Finally, separate the steps: stopping punishment, recognising a union and promoting it are three different moves (E45.10).

The question FQ1 · 2018-19 Q1 · 2012-13 Q1

"Legislators should follow majority opinion on moral questions. Otherwise, they merely impose their own subjective views." Or: "Enforcing the moral judgements of certain groups must be avoided, lest minority rights be trampled." Or: "A pluralist democracy should not endorse moral values, so legislators should not try to resolve intractable moral conflicts."

The answer in one route

Recall mode hides everything after the crux. Say each part to yourself, then tap it to check.

Crux

"Otherwise" sets up a false choice between following the majority and imposing one's own subjective view. In other forms, "lest" disarms minorities, and "should not resolve" forgets that leaving the law in place is itself a resolution.

Thesis

Define "majority opinion" (what a poll would find) and "subjective" (a view backed by no reason others could examine). The majority settles who decides, not what is right. Every theorist supplies a third option: reasons every citizen can examine.

  1. Section 1, "majority opinion"

    Devlin does not count heads; he asks what twelve people would accept after discussion. Rawls wants public reasons, not numbers. In 2007 the 70% who "frowned on" homosexuality were never asked whether it should be a crime (Hri Kumar). A head-count binds only because each person counted has equal dignity, which is itself a moral premise.

  2. Section 2, "subjective views"

    A view is subjective only if no reason can be given for it. Hart tests the majority's morality by critical morality. Finnis says the basic goods are self-evident to reason.

  3. Section 3, "neutral" or "no group"

    No law is neutral: permitting decides too. Moving the 1969 Abortion Bill, Chua said no one could know whether the foetus is human life, then answered the question. TSH's hunter should not fire when unsure. Protecting minorities is itself a moral judgement.

  4. If the statement runs the other way

    "No law is neutral, so enact what is true": concede the premise, then require reasons the minority can examine and coercion graded to them.

  5. Other side

    Best case: democracy treats citizens as equals. Weak point: demanding public reasons is contested.

  6. Repaired

    “The majority decides, but must decide on reasons it can defend to the minority.”

Memory lineNot the crowd, not my taste: reasons anyone can examine. Not deciding is a decision.

Use it in a hypo · about a minute

Hypo 7 A bill reserves housing grants, baby bonuses and tax reliefs for married parents, and makes every school teach that children are best raised by a married mother and father. Grounds: the main faiths hold marriage sacred; children of married parents do better on average; the Constitution invites Parliament to promote marriage. An opposition MP wants a referendum instead. How would you vote on the bill and on the referendum?

On the call for a referendum, a head-count is not a reason, and MPs must decide (Masagos). The bill is low on the ladder, since it promotes rather than punishes, which counts in its favour. But its school mandate reaches religious schools and the children of single parents, so its reasons must be ones those families can examine and answer, not simply the majority's view.

Crux

Thesis

Define legitimacy as a right to rule that grounds a moral duty to obey. Will makes law only through rules that bind it. Legitimacy is argued, not proved. Obedience is a strong moral default, not an automatic duty.

  1. Section 1, "what the majority wills"

  3. Section 3, "simply be obeyed"

  4. Other side

    Best case: order and copying, plus Hoerster's warning that conscience may be wrong. Weak point: legitimacy is argued, not proved, so some will remain unconvinced.

  5. Repaired

Crux

The statement treats identifying law as neutral, and treats the two schools as a division of labour: positivists describe, natural lawyers prescribe.

Thesis

Define natural law: law's validity or authority depends in part on morality, either throughout (strong) or only at the extreme (weak). Both schools ask what law is. Positivism's description involves a choice, and weak natural law speaks only at the extremes and needs no faith. The debate is real but narrow.

  1. Section 1, "cross purposes" and "neutral"

    Both schools answer the same question, what law is (TSH). "No necessary connection" still allows contingent links, so a rule with moral content troubles no positivist. Positivism calls Nazi law law so that it can be condemned; Radbruch denies the name to its worst statutes.

  2. Section 2, "for the devout" and "a licence"

    Natural law is grasped by reason, not faith, and Grotius made it secular. Radbruch's formula applies only to intolerable injustice. Strong natural law would void every unjust rule; weak natural law strips legal status only at extreme divergence, such as a law to execute all redheads. TSH admits "no easy answer" on who pronounces natural law, but her method is not fiat.

  3. Section 3, Fuller and Dworkin

    They are allies of natural law, not natural lawyers. Hart and Fuller arguably talk across purposes, since one may agree with Fuller on Nazi Germany and with Hart or Radbruch on the grudge informer. Hart and Radbruch do not.

  4. Other side

    Best case: positivism's clarity. Weak point: the narrow link at the extreme is contested.

Crux

"Rule of law" can be formal or substantive. One thesis answers every form: the rule of law is more than efficiency and less than justice.

Thesis

Define the rule of law in its formal sense: Fuller's eight requirements (general, public, prospective, clear, consistent, possible, constant, and applied as written). Total failure of one means no legal system; short of that, legality comes in degrees. It is a real but thin morality: it binds rulers, treats subjects as agents and exposes aims, but it cannot make bad laws just.

  1. Section 1, moral or not

    For Fuller legality is an internal morality; for Hart it concerns efficacy. Legality binds even a self-seeking ruler, yet unjust aims can still run through clear, public rules.

  2. Section 2, "mere efficiency"

    The charge, chiefly Hart's, is that legality is like good carpentry or a knife's sharpness, serving good and bad ends alike. Fuller replies that law governs people, so its craft cannot be morally idle.

  3. Section 2, "a ruling-class slogan"

    On the Marxist view law is the ruling class's instrument, and the crits say doctrine presents hierarchy as neutral. But law has its own character, and a slogan binds no one.

  4. Section 3, "a guarantee of justice"

    Formal justice is compatible with very iniquitous law (Hart). The Nazi regime needed secret laws and a retroactive statute ratifying its own lawless acts, and Ordinance 11 had legal form. Legality is a condition of justice, not a guarantee.

  5. Other side

    Weak point: legality is thin, and Fuller argued from the Nazi regime's collapse of legality, not from the content of its laws.

  6. Repaired

    “The rule of law is a real but thin morality that binds rulers and comes in degrees; it cannot make bad laws just.”
