F1 GapF4 Equivocal2017-18 Q22020-21 Q2≈ 5 min read + 4 min quiz

How to spot it

The statement starts with a right to make your own most personal life choices. Then it says "therefore" the law must not enforce morality or control private behaviour.

Words that give it awayright to decideintimateprivate behaviourthereforeenforce morality
Look-alikes: use the other essay if…
  • E5the statement sets the majority's morality against private choice, and uses harm as the dividing line
  • E6the statement says liberty is "inevitably sacrificed" whenever the law pursues moral values
  • E3the statement has three linked steps: all opinions are equally valid, so use force only to prevent harm, so no fixed idea of the good life
If the question is worded differently
  • If the statement ends with neutrality (the state must not take sides) and not with "do not enforce": in section 3, use block E1.5.
  • If it says liberty is “inevitably” sacrificed: go to E6.
  • If it only asks legislators to “respect” liberty in intimate matters, and asks how far each theorist would agree: use E1.1 with all four theorists, then E1.2, then E1.5, under the second intro on the sheet.

The question 2017-18 Q2 · 2020-21 Q2

"Individuals have the right to make the most fundamental decisions about their own lives, such as whether to bear a child and with whom to have an intimate relationship. Therefore, legislators should not enforce morality or regulate private behaviour." Critically comment, and say how far Hart, Devlin, Rawls and Finnis would agree.

The answer, step by step

Recall mode blurs everything after the crux. Say each part from memory, then tap it to check.

Crux the key point

The weak word is "therefore". A right to run your own life does not rule out every law that touches morals. To get from the right to the conclusion, the statement needs a hidden step: that the law may force people only to stop harm to others. Say that hidden step out loud, then test it.

Thesis your answer in short

First define two phrases. "Enforce morality" means using law to make people act in a certain way because it is right. "Private behaviour" means conduct that affects only people who have agreed to it. The statement is right about one thing: nobody should be punished for private, consensual conduct just because it is thought immoral. It is wrong to say the right blocks every morals law. The right is itself a moral claim. And the law can do less than punish: it can regulate, or simply encourage.

  1. Section 1, "a right to decide"

    Hart: liberty is a strong starting rule, but it can be outweighed. Rawls: the law protects liberty as a political value that everyone can accept, not because choosing for yourself is the highest good in life. Finnis: the statement's argument is "outright self-contradiction". It treats choices as mere personal preference, yet it relies on the claim that choosing for yourself is truly valuable. Also, the right cannot decide a case where someone else is affected, such as a child.

  2. The hinge, "enforce"

    "Enforce" covers three different things: promote (encourage), regulate (set rules) and punish. Think of a ladder. The more force the law uses, the stronger the reasons it needs.

  3. Section 2, "harm to others"

    The hidden step is that only harm to others justifies force. Hart allows more than Mill did: he accepts paternalism (protecting people from themselves) and laws on public decency. Devlin sets no limit in theory on what the law may enforce. Rawls does not test the conduct at all; he tests the reasons given for the law. So harm is the right starting rule, but it is not the only ground. And deciding what counts as "harm" is itself a moral judgement.

  4. Section 3, "morals law"

    Every law enforces some morality. So the real question is not "is this a morals law?" Ask three things: what are its reasons, how much force does it use, and who has to bear it?

  5. Other side the best argument against you, and your weak point

    The best case for the statement is that people disagree about how to live (pluralism), and that we should be humble about being right. But that only shows no single doctrine should be made official. It does not show that every morals law is ruled out. The weak point on your own side: Finnis says anyone can understand his basic goods by reasoning, but not everyone accepts them.

  6. Repaired a better version of the statement

    “Choices about one's intimate life deserve strong protection from punishment, but not immunity from all regulation or from the state's promotion of other goods.”

Memory lineA right to decide is a moral claim; enforcing is a ladder; harm is the bridge you must name.

Worked example: use it in a hypo · about a minute

Hypo 3, MP limb An MP's bill makes adultery an offence punishable by up to two years' imprisonment; the Minister says police will not proactively enforce it, as it is "a moral signpost". Grounds: every major religion condemns adultery; children of marriages broken by infidelity fare worse; a divided consultation, but a clear majority for the law. How would you vote?

Start with the right to decide (section 1). Adultery is an intimate choice, but it is not purely private. The spouse and the children are affected and did not agree to it, so the right alone cannot decide the bill. Next use the ladder (the hinge). Prison is the top rung, so it needs the strongest reasons. Encouraging marriage would cost far less liberty. In section 3, attack the "moral signpost" idea. A law that stays on the books but is not enforced still marks people as criminals. It also gives police and prosecutors what Hart calls "formidable discriminatory powers" (E2.3): they get to choose whom to go after. Vote against the offence.

The question 2016-17 Q2

"Enforcing morality through law is not necessarily unjust or illegitimate. The only valid objections to it are moral and prudential, not sceptical." Discuss, with the theorists' views and current controversies. (2014-15 Q2 form (b): "Majority reasons are not necessarily legitimate or correct. However, the legislature may enforce the morality of society.")

The answer, step by step

Recall mode blurs everything after the crux. Say each part from memory, then tap it to check.

Crux the key point

The statement is mostly right. Its one mistake is the word "sceptical", which can mean two things. (1) "There is no moral truth." That view defeats itself. (2) "There may be moral truth, but who can be sure they know it?" That doubt is sensible and still counts.

Thesis your answer in short

Define two words. "Illegitimate" means the state has no right to use this power at all, whatever the law says. "Unjust" means the content of the law wrongs the people it binds. Enforcing morality is not illegitimate in itself, and no theorist objects to it on sceptical grounds. But doubt about which moral claims are true is a real reason to be careful. It affects how the state should enforce, not whether it may.

  1. Section 1, "enforcing morality"

    No law can avoid enforcing some morality. Devlin and Finnis accept this fully; Hart and Rawls accept it in part. But sin is not the same as crime. What can make enforcement unjust is its reasons, how much force it uses, and who has to bear it.

  2. Section 2, "not sceptical"

    The statement is right to dismiss the first kind of sceptic. "Be tolerant" is itself a moral demand. "We were wrong before" assumes there is a right answer. And scepticism does not even lead to tolerance: if morality is only what each society believes (Devlin's starting point), then every society may enforce its own.

  3. Section 3, "sceptical", the second sense

    TSH's second sceptic does not deny moral truth. He doubts who knows it. That doubt is real, and it works through prudence (practical caution): change the law slowly, protect privacy, ask for reasons that go beyond feeling, and match the force to the strength of the reasons.

  4. Form (b)

    For form (b): a majority vote settles who decides. It does not settle what is right. In Lim Meng Suang the Attorney-General relied on public morality, not on popular feeling. And the court said judges are not a "mini-legislature".

  5. Other side the best argument against you, and your weak point

    The best case against you is again pluralism and humility. The weak point: "there is no moral truth" and "I doubt anyone knows it" can sound alike. The difference is that the doubter can be shown to be wrong, with reasons.

  6. Repaired a better version of the statement

    “Enforcing morality is legitimate when its reasons are accessible and sound and its coercion proportionate. Scepticism is no objection, but fallibility is a reason for restraint.”

Memory lineDenying moral truth defeats itself; doubting who knows it counsels care, not silence.

Worked example: use it in a hypo · about a minute

Hypo 1, MP limb A bill bans all gambling, including at the two licensed casinos, with long prison terms and no exception save the charity lottery. Grounds: several religions call gambling a sin; problem gamblers fall into debt and crime; 78% "disapprove". How would you vote?

Sin is not crime. Yaacob Ibrahim (2005) pointed out that drinking, and other things Muslims consider vices, are not banned. Pritam Singh (2022) said that in a secular society sin and crime are separate. Next, the poll. 78% "disapprove" of gambling, but that does not show they want it made a crime. The harms the MP points to, debt and crime, are real. But they support licensing, limits and help for problem gamblers. They do not support jail for the ordinary gambler. If you need a current controversy, use the casinos (is gambling a form of play? See E45.4).

The question Sample Q1 · 2025-26 Q1

"Legislators should treat all moral opinions as equally valid. This can be achieved by restricting liberty only when it is objectively necessary to protect others from a wrongdoer. Only then will the law avoid imposing a fixed conception of the good life." Discuss with reference to Rawls, Finnis, Hart and Devlin, with illustrations.

The answer, step by step

Recall mode blurs everything after the crux. Say each part from memory, then tap it to check.

Crux the key point

The statement is a chain with three links: a goal (treat all opinions as equally valid), a method (force only to protect others) and a result (no fixed idea of the good life). A chain breaks where the links join. So test each join: does the goal really lead to the method, and the method to the result?

Thesis your answer in short

The statement is right that no single doctrine should be made the official one. It is wrong in two places.   1. Section 1, "equally valid"

If it means no doctrine gets a head start, that is Rawls's view, but even Rawls first excludes unreasonable doctrines. Devlin and Hart both reject equal validity. Knowing we can be wrong is a reason for caution, not for neutrality.

  2. Section 2, "only to protect others"

    Hart's harm principle is wider than Mill's. Devlin rejects the harm limit altogether. For Rawls, a harm-only rule is just Mill's own complete philosophy of life (a "comprehensive" view), so it is not neutral either. Example: consent is no defence to murder. Hart explains this as paternalism (protecting people from themselves). But choosing what to protect people from is itself a moral choice.

  4. Other side the best argument against you, and your weak point

    The best case for the statement is pluralism and humility. The weak point on your side: Finnis's basic goods can be understood by reasoning, but not everyone accepts them.

Crux the key point

"Neutral" has two meanings, and the statement needs the impossible one. A law cannot be neutral in its results. It can be neutral in its reasons, but only weakly, and that is itself a moral position.

Thesis your answer in short

Define "rival views of the good life" as comprehensive doctrines: complete views of how to live, religious or secular. No law is neutral in its effects. A law can try to rest on reasons all citizens could accept, but that aim is a moral commitment too. The state should be even-handed between citizens, not between opinions.

  1. Section 1, "neutral"

    There are two kinds of neutrality: of results and of reasons. Even a "secular" school curriculum reflects a national position on the family (Masagos). Rawls asks only for neutrality of aim, not of effect, and liberal neutrality can favour the doctrines that are already established. Finnis is not neutral about ends (he says what is good), but he is liberal about means: the state only keeps peace and justice.

  2. Section 2, "as possible"

    Allowing something is a decision too. So the question is never whether to take a side. It is how much of a side to take, for what reasons, and with how much force.

  3. Section 3, what replaces neutrality

    Match the force to the strength of the reasons: promoting needs less justification than punishing. Each theorist offers a standard to replace neutrality: Hart's critical morality, Rawls's public reasons, Finnis's basic goods. The answer, step by step

Recall mode blurs everything after the crux. Say each part from memory, then tap it to check.

Crux the key point

The starting point is a claim about what morality is ("just opinion"). But the conclusion ("so the law must tolerate") is a moral demand. Someone who doubts all moral claims cannot make that demand.

Thesis your answer in short

Define moral truth: moral facts hold whatever anyone believes, so when two people disagree, one of them is wrong (TSH). Disagreement, and the lack of proof, do not show there is no moral truth. "So tolerate" defeats itself. Taking moral truth seriously calls for discussion and reasons, not for forcing views on people.

  1. Section 1, "just opinion"

    First say which sceptic you are facing. The relativist says morality differs from culture to culture. The sceptic doubts we can know it. The non-cognitivist says moral claims are only feelings, like a taste for durian. Then take the exact starting point the statement uses ("a social construct", "nobody can agree") and show what it assumes.

  2. Section 2, "therefore tolerate"

    Tolerance is itself a moral claim. Hart says moral opinions can be better or worse. TSH says refusing to act on a judgement is acting as if the opposite were true. And if morality is just each society's belief (Devlin's starting point), that gives no reason for tolerance at all.

  3. Section 3, facts and nature

    Hume says wrongness lies in our feelings, not in the act. But that claim arguably fails its own test. His gap between "is" (facts) and "ought" (what should be done) is only a point of logic: if we agree on a moral starting point, facts plus that starting point do give conclusions.

  4. Section 4, if the statement offers a substitute

    If the statement offers something in place of morality: utility (maximising happiness) cannot weigh goods that have no common scale. And "law is whatever is enacted" rests on a moral claim too.

  5. Other side the best argument against you, and your weak point

    The best case for the statement: humility, because we have been wrong before. The weak point: the real problem is how we come to know moral truth, not whether it exists.

  6. Repaired a better version of the statement

    “Moral claims can be true or false, but our judgements of them are fallible, so the law should rest on them provisionally, demanding more of them the more coercive it is.”

Crux the key point

"Share" does not mean what every citizen actually accepts. It means what every citizen may reasonably be expected to accept. And Rawls's neutrality is about reasons (the aim of the law), not about results.

Thesis your answer in short

Rawls is right to ask for reasons each citizen could accept. But allowing something still takes a side. The better test is whether reasons can be understood and are sound, not whether they are accepted. (Finnis faces the same difficulty.) And Rawls's method rests on real moral values.

  2. Then choose one section by the statement

    Pick one, to match the statement. For "grounds all can share": say who is bound (officials, candidates, citizens when voting, but not churches), and that the duty of civility is moral, not legal. For "modus vivendi": an overlapping consensus is accepted by each doctrine for its own moral reasons. It is not a bargain, so it survives changes in who holds power. For "freestanding yet overriding": the political conception is not value-free. Its "very great values" are what do the overriding.

  3. Soundness, if asked

    If asked about soundness: say which job each theory does best. Finnis is the main rival.

The answer, step by step

Recall mode blurs everything after the crux. Say each part from memory, then tap it to check.

Crux the key point

A false choice. Finnis does not dictate a whole way of life, and he does not leave the law-maker with nothing. He takes a third path.

Thesis your answer in short

Define natural law as Finnis's basic goods and his requirements of practical reasonableness (rules for choosing well). It is not a set of divine commands. Seven goods and nine requirements produce a few absolute limits, and a common good that is a framework, not a life plan. Inside that framework, practical judgement decides, and reasonable people differ.

  1. Section 1, the basic goods

    There are seven basic goods: life, knowledge, play, aesthetic experience (beauty), friendship, practical reasonableness and religion. They are self-evident: you grasp them by reflecting, not by proof. None ranks above another.

  2. Section 2, practical reasonableness

    Three requirements matter most for law. Do not prefer some persons over others without reason. Respect every basic good in every act. Serve the common good. Some acts are ruled out absolutely, such as intending death. What counts is intention, and intention is fixed by the plan the person adopts.

  3. Section 3, the law-maker

    Every society takes a public stand, and the state may favour the basic goods. But the state only keeps peace and justice. Its permission to restrict conduct is not a programme to do so. And its reasons must be ones everyone can understand. So Finnis is liberal about means, while his absolutes rule some laws out.

  4. Soundness

    On soundness: the goods have to be grasped; they are not obvious. There is nothing magic about the number seven. The weak point is access: anyone can reach them by reasoning, but not everyone accepts them.

  5. Other side the best argument against you, and your weak point

    For "dictates", Rawls's best case is that even a thin theory of the good is still a theory of the good. For "no guidance", the point is that goods with no common scale decide few statutes.

  6. Repaired a better version of the statement

    “Finnis's natural law gives the law-maker firm limits and a direction, not a blueprint; within the limits reasonable people may differ, and coercion needs reasons all can examine.”

Thesis your answer in short

Crux the key point

Thesis your answer in short

Define two terms. "Legal philosophy" is a view of what law is and how to read it. "Personal morality" is your own moral view, as opposed to the morality the law itself adopts.   2. Section 2, personal morality

Broad historical consensus (what people across eras have agreed) checks personal ideology. And refusing a plainly unjust law is loyalty to law, not disloyalty (TSH).

  3. Section 3, "always sufficient"

  5. Other side the best argument against you, and your weak point

    The best case for Dworkin: Riggs. The weak point: whose political morality decides?

Crux the key point

The key is the difference between predicting a ruling and justifying it. The statement comes in three forms (rules do not decide cases; doctrine is coherent; critique is useless). Each form uses its own two sections.

Thesis your answer in short

Thesis your answer in short

  2. Section 2, "no further legitimacy"

  3. Section 3, "simply be obeyed"

    If no legitimacy can be shown, then nothing shows that valid law must be obeyed. So the statement's own second step defeats "must obey". Keep apart the legal duty to obey, the moral duty to obey and the moral duty to disobey. The pigeon feeder shows that fair play, not fear, is the reason to obey, for positivists too.

Crux the key point

The statement assumes that identifying law is a neutral task. It also treats the two schools as doing different jobs: positivists describe, natural lawyers recommend.

Thesis your answer in short

Define "natural law": the view that a law's validity or authority depends partly on morality. The strong version says this of all law. The weak version says it only at the extreme. Both schools ask the same question: what is law? The positivist's "description" involves a choice. Weak natural law speaks only at the extremes and needs no religious faith. So the debate is real, but narrow.

  1. Section 1, "cross purposes" and "neutral"

    Both schools answer the same question, what law is (TSH). "No necessary connection" between law and morality still allows connections in particular cases. So a rule with moral content does not trouble a positivist. Positivism calls Nazi law "law" so that it can be condemned clearly. Radbruch refuses the name to its worst statutes.

  2. Section 2, "for the devout" and "a licence"

    Natural law is grasped by reason, not by faith. Grotius made it secular. Radbruch's formula applies only to intolerable injustice. Strong natural law would treat every unjust rule as void. Weak natural law removes legal status only at the extreme, such as a law to execute all redheads. TSH admits there is "no easy answer" to who gets to say what natural law is. But her method is not "because I say so".

  3. Section 3, Fuller and Dworkin

    Fuller and Dworkin are allies of natural law, not natural lawyers. Hart and Fuller arguably do talk past each other: you can agree with Fuller about Nazi Germany and with Hart or Radbruch about the grudge informer. Hart and Radbruch do not talk past each other.

    The best case for the statement: positivism is clear. The weak point: the narrow link at the extreme is disputed.

The answer, step by step

Recall mode blurs everything after the crux. Say each part from memory, then tap it to check.

Crux the key point

"Rule of law" can mean a matter of form (how rules are made and applied) or of substance (whether they are just). One answer deals with every version: the rule of law is more than efficiency and less than justice.

Thesis your answer in short

Define the rule of law in its formal sense: Fuller's eight requirements. Rules must be general, public, prospective (not backdated), clear, consistent, possible to obey, stable, and applied as written. If one fails completely, there is no legal system. Short of that, legality comes in degrees. It is a real but thin morality. It binds rulers, treats people as agents who can plan, and brings the ruler's aims into the open. But it cannot make bad laws just.

  1. Section 1, moral or not

    For Fuller, legality is a morality built into law itself. For Hart, it is about making law work. Legality binds even a selfish ruler. Yet unjust aims can still be pursued through clear, public rules.

  2. Section 2, "mere efficiency"

    The charge, mainly Hart's, is that legality is like good carpentry or a sharp knife: it serves good and bad ends alike. Fuller replies that law governs people, so its craft cannot be morally empty.

  3. Section 2, "a ruling-class slogan"

    On the Marxist view, law is a tool of the ruling class. The crits say legal doctrine makes hierarchy look neutral. But law has a character of its own, and a mere slogan binds nobody.

  4. Section 3, "a guarantee of justice"

    Formal justice (treating like cases alike) is compatible with very wicked law (Hart). The Nazi regime needed secret laws, and a backdated statute to approve its own lawless acts. And Ordinance 11 had the form of law. So legality is a condition of justice, not a guarantee of it.

  5. Other side the best argument against you, and your weak point

    The weak point: legality is thin. And Fuller argued from the Nazi regime's breakdown of legality, not from what its laws said.

  6. Repaired a better version of the statement

    “The rule of law is a real but thin morality that binds rulers and comes in degrees; it cannot make bad laws just.”

How to spot it

Use G1 only when no row of the Index fits the statement. Each type below has a ready-made plan on the sheet, built from blocks of other essays.

The types, and where each one goes
  • Same result, so the theory is idle.Example: “Hart and Devlin reach the same result, so their quarrel makes no difference.” Use G1.1. Agree that they reach the same result. Then show that their reasons still decide four things: the hard case, the next case, what the losing side is owed, and what survives a change of government. The sheet's "fills" give you the material for one family of essays.
  • A label made to do moral work.Example: “Positivists must be deferential judges and amoral lawyers.” One move answers it. A theory of what law is only tells you what counts as law. Every further step (how to judge, how to practise) needs a moral starting point, and either school can supply one.
  • Who decides, for every actor.Each role hands the question to someone else: the legislator to the majority, the judge to the legislature, the lawyer to the client, the citizen to the law. §1 the legislator, §2 the judge, §3 the lawyer and the citizen. Ending: handing over settles who answers a moral question. It never settles whether the answer is right.
  • The role answers for its wrongs.Example: “Whoever acts within a legitimate role is not responsible.” §1 the case for it, §2 its limit, §3 the extreme case. Ending: a just role takes responsibility for ordinary results, never for intolerable ones.
  • A Constitution chain on who decides.Go to E36. If the statement starts from "moral reasoning is inevitable", use E23’s §1, then E36’s §2 and §3.
  • One theorist under test.Use that theorist's own essay: Hart E14, Devlin E15, Rawls E16, Finnis E17, Dworkin E31, Radbruch E43. If the statement has E14's shape (admit a problem, say it is not fatal, say the theory is still the best) but is about someone else, use E14's plan.
  • A morals law the citizen thinks wrong.Use E1.4, then E38.1 and E38.2.
