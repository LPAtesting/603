# Family 3: Judges, lawyers and the Constitution (E20-E36)

@card E20
say: With a supreme written Constitution that sets the tests of law and lists rights, judges and lawyers have no practical need for legal philosophy or personal morality. Obeying the law always gives them enough moral guidance.
verdict: disagree
answer: Disagree: the statement is itself a philosophy. The Constitution settles who decides, not what its open words mean. Following the law is necessary, but never always enough.
step: Philosophy: the Constitution's open words (like "law" in Article 9) need a theory to read. Even Article 4's supremacy rests on officials accepting it.
step: Morality in judging is disciplined, not private: it is tested on appeal and checked by broad historical consensus.
step: "Always enough": the law leaves many lawful choices, such as pleading a time bar against a debt the client admits. The law is a floor, not a ceiling.
remember: The Constitution settles who decides, not how; following the law is necessary, never always enough.
spot: A supreme written Constitution, so judges and lawyers need no philosophy or morality; complying is enough.
Q: Which statement is E20?
* "With a supreme Constitution, judges need no philosophy or morality." || Right.
- "Article 4 settles the debate between natural law and positivism once and for all." || That is what law is under the Constitution: E35.
- "Judges should apply only what the law is, and that is desirable in a democracy." || About judges alone: E21.
Q: "The law is a floor, not a ceiling." What does that mean for a lawyer?
* Obeying the law is the minimum, not the whole job. || Right: above the floor there are still moral choices.
- Anything lawful is automatically the right thing to do. || That treats the floor as the ceiling.
- Lawyers may break the law when the cause is good enough. || The floor still binds.
Q: What does the Constitution settle, on E20's answer?

- Every legal question, so no theory is needed || Words like "law" and "equal" still need reading.
- Nothing at all: it is only a piece of paper || Too far: it settles who decides.

@card E21
say: Judges do not, and should not, use any moral judgement; they deal only with what the law is. In a constitutional democracy that is a good thing.
verdict: partly
answer: Partly agree: judges should not decide by personal views. But "no moral judgement at all" is neither true nor desirable: morality is always in the background and decides hard cases.
step: "Any": even Hart's judge must use judgement in unclear cases. Easy cases rest on a shared theory.
step: "Only what the law is": working out what the law is needs a theory. In Riggs the judges agreed the statute bound them, and still split over what it required.
step: "Desirable": aim for a judge whose morality is always present and rarely decisive. Avoid two vices: taking over Parliament's job, and rubber-stamping.
remember: Always in the background, rarely decisive: disciplined morality, not none.
spot: Judges use no moral judgement, only "what the law is", and that is desirable.

@card E22

@card E23
say: Moral reasoning in judging is unavoidable. Therefore judges have no reason in principle to refuse to read a right to abortion into the Constitution, whatever the text says, if equality requires it.
verdict: partly
answer: Agree with sentence one, disagree with the "therefore". Judges must reason morally, but they are bound by the legal record and their role, and may set the text aside only for intolerable injustice.
step: Concede quickly: moral reasoning is unavoidable. But unavoidable is not a licence.
step: "Whatever the text says": even Dworkin binds the judge to the legal history (fit). Yong Vui Kong (2015) refused to make rights out of "whole cloth".
step: Equality cuts both ways: the woman's equal standing and the unborn's equal worth. Which wins depends on who counts, a question for Parliament (Lim Meng Suang). Singapore already allows abortion up to 24 weeks.
remember: Unavoidable is not a licence: fit, role and a high bar bind judges' moral reasoning.
spot: "Moral reasoning is inevitable. Therefore..." judges may read a right (such as abortion) into the Constitution.

@card E24
say: In an immoral legal system, the only right course for a judge or lawyer is to resign. Anything else either supports injustice or betrays the law.
verdict: disagree
answer: Disagree: a false choice. Refusing an intolerably unjust law from inside is loyalty to law, not betrayal. Resigning is sometimes right, never the only right course, and staying can protect victims.
step: Ask what is immoral: the whole system, one rule, or one result? Refusing an intolerable rule openly is fidelity to law (Radbruch, Alexy); Hart says be honest about it.
step: Judges: Kennedy gives five options short of resigning; quitting just leaves "the dirty work" to someone else. No hidden tricks (candour).
step: Lawyers: Thunder's lawyer stays to protect the victims, patiently, without going along.
remember: "Condone or betray" hides a third option: refuse from within, openly. Resigning is one option, never the only one.
spot: An immoral system; resign, stay or refuse; a judge or a lawyer.
Q: Which statement is E24?

- "A good lawyer can be a good person only as far as the legal system allows." || An ordinary system: E33.
- "Once the highest court calls an unjust rule law, then it is law, and that is that." || Radbruch: E43.
Q: What is the third option the statement hides?
* Stay, and refuse the unjust law openly. || Right.

- Leave the country and practise elsewhere. || Not the option meant.

@card E25
say: Judges claim to apply the law, but judging is politics. So legal theory is useless, and the lawyer simply serves the client.
verdict: disagree
answer: Disagree: each "so" has a gap. Judging is political only in the sense of political morality, not party politics; lawyers cannot practise without some theory; and serving the client happens above the floor of the law.
step: Rules have a settled core and fuzzy edges (Hart). In fuzzy cases judges reason from political morality, not preference (Dworkin). Even Kennedy sometimes "can't budge" a rule.
step: Theory is not idle: lawyers predict most outcomes because they share a theory. The choice is examined theory or unexamined theory.
step: "Just serve the client" is itself a theory of the role. Pepper's own limits keep conscientious objection. The law is the floor; above it, dialogue.
remember: Political, not partisan; examined theory, not none; serve the client above a floor.
spot: A cynic's chain: judging is politics, so theory is idle, so the lawyer just serves the client.

@card E27
say: Judging according to the law serves justice. So judges should avoid lofty moral ideas, which bring too much subjectivity.
verdict: disagree
answer: Disagree: the two sentences cannot both stand. If "law" means just the rules, it gives only formal justice (treating like cases alike). If law includes a moral floor, judges cannot avoid morality. The cure for subjectivity is discipline, not abstaining.
step: The positivist reading: applying the rules secures only formal justice. Hart and Fuller both admit rules can be unfair.
step: The natural-law reading: extreme injustice is not law (Radbruch, Alexy). The court that refused Nazi Ordinance 11 applied the law and could not avoid morality.
step: Subjectivity: moral reasoning in court is disciplined by fit, public reasons and appeals.
remember: No reading saves both sentences; discipline, not abstinence, cures subjectivity.
spot: "According to the law" serves "justice", so avoid "lofty" morality and "subjectivity".
