★ Liberty against morals law

Question 2017-18 Q2 · 2020-21 Q2

"Individuals have the right to make the most fundamental decisions about their own lives, such as whether to bear a child and with whom to have an intimate relationship. Therefore, legislators should not enforce morality or regulate private behaviour." Critically comment, and say how far Hart, Devlin, Rawls and Finnis would agree.

Hypo Hypo 3, MP limb

An MP's bill makes adultery an offence punishable by up to two years' imprisonment; the Minister says police will not proactively enforce it, as it is "a moral signpost". Grounds: every major religion condemns adultery; children of marriages broken by infidelity fare worse; a divided consultation, but a clear majority for the law. How would you vote?

Outline
Crux
"Therefore": a right to decide does not reach every law touching morals. Harm to others is the hidden bridge; name it. [F1] [F4]
Thesis
Define "enforce morality" (securing conduct because it is right) and "private behaviour" (affects no one who has not consented). Right that private consensual conduct should not be punished for immorality alone; wrong that the right bars every morals law, because the right is itself a moral claim and law can regulate or promote without punishing.
§1 a right to decide
Opener: "not wrong" hides a positive judgment†. Finnis: "outright self-contradiction"; Rawls: autonomy as highest value is comprehensive; Devlin: no private sphere in principle, toleration in practice. Close: bars punishment, not third-party cases; begetting or rearing a child involves another (TSH), so surrogacy and adoption stay open. Abortion: E1.10's third bullet.
Hinge enforce
Punish, regulate, promote: justification rises with coercion (E1.2, without its last sentence).
§2 harm to others
Hart: wider than Mill (paternalism, decency); Rawls tests reasons, not conduct. Close: harm is a presumption, not the only ground; LMS called harm contested and left it to Parliament; the trader ruined by a fair competitor is harmed by no wrongdoer. 2022: crime needs a public order or public interest issue (E1.9).
§3 morals law
Devlin and Finnis (E1.4): every law enforces some morality; Hart's target is narrower. Close: unjust only by reasons, coercion and who bears it; Art 156 came with repeal to block the inference of neutrality.
Other side
Steelman: pluralism and humility (E1.6) shows no doctrine made official, not no morals law. Own side: Finnis's goods accessible, not accepted (E1.7).
Repaired
Choices about one's intimate life deserve strong protection from punishment, but not immunity from all regulation, or from the state's promotion of other goods. For the legislator: regulate or promote before punishing.
If
"Respect liberty" form: second intro, all four on E1.1, then E1.2, then E1.5. Neutrality conclusion: §3 uses E1.5. "Inevitably sacrificed": E6.
In the hypo
§1: intimate choice, but the spouse and children are third parties. Hinge: punishing is the top rung; promoting marriage is cheaper. §3: non-enforcement keeps the stigma and hands police "formidable discriminatory powers" (Hart, E2.3). Vote against the offence.

E5

The majority against private choice

Question exam-style

Form (a): "In a democracy the majority may enforce its morality against private choices it condemns." Form (b): "Private choices must prevail over the majority's morality unless they harm others." Discuss.

Hypo new, same Ruritania

An MP's bill fines unmarried adult couples who live together. A poll finds 72% "disapprove" of cohabitation; she cites studies that children of cohabiting parents fare worse on average. A second MP says private choices are no business of the state. How would you vote?

Outline
Crux
A false dilemma [F5]: both sides overstate, and the harm line between them is itself a moral judgement.
Thesis
The majority may decide, but its disapproval alone is no reason to punish; the right to private choice is itself a moral claim; harm is the right line for punishment, not a neutral one.
§1 majority
E2.3 (Devlin, Rawls): majority settles who decides, not what is right; Devlin does not count heads. Close: the majority is not always for punishment (one MP cited it for both repeal and Art 156); a majority morality that derogates from fundamental rights may not be enforced (TSH).
§2 private choice
E1.1 (Hart, Rawls; Finnis if the statement grounds it in autonomy). Close: bars punishment, not third-party cases; the choice must be truly free (Hart: clouded judgement, subtle pressure).
§3 harm
E1.3 (Hart, Devlin) with E1.2's first sentence. Close: distress at bare knowledge is not harm (Hart); what counts as harm is moral; 2022: crime needs a public order or public interest issue.
Other side
Steelman: democracy (E2.5) for form (a); liberty and humility (E1.6) for form (b). Own side: demanding public reasons is contested (E2.6).
Repaired
The majority decides, but may punish private choices only on reasons it can defend to the minority, and harm to others is the strongest of them. Pluralism disciplines the reasons, the coercion and the forum (a trustee, not a stenographer).
In the hypo
The 72% is evidence, not a mandate (Hri Kumar's 70%). Children's outcomes are "on average", so they support promotion and support for families, not fines. Vote against; offer promotion instead.

■ Whose morality may the law enforce

Question 2016-17 Q2

"Enforcing morality through law is not necessarily unjust or illegitimate. The only valid objections to it are moral and prudential, not sceptical." Discuss, with the theorists' views and current controversies. (2014-15 Q2 form (b): "Majority reasons are not necessarily legitimate or correct. However, the legislature may enforce the morality of society.")

Hypo Hypo 1, MP limb

A bill bans all gambling, including at the two licensed casinos, with long prison terms and no exception save the charity lottery. Grounds: several religions call gambling a sin; problem gamblers fall into debt and crime; 78% "disapprove". How would you vote?

Outline
Crux
Largely right; the flaw [F4] is that "sceptical" has two senses: denying moral truth defeats itself, doubting who knows it does not.
Thesis
Define "illegitimate" (power the state has no right to use, whatever its content) and "unjust" (content that wrongs those bound). Enforcing morality is not illegitimate as such, and no theorist objects on sceptical grounds; but doubt about which moral claims are true is a real reason for restraint, governing how, not whether.
§1 enforcing morality
E1.4 (Devlin, Finnis; Hart and Rawls partly), then E2.8's first bullet (sin is not crime). Close: unjust only by reasons, coercion and who bears it; repeal too is a moral stand; Devlin separates passing judgment from enforcing it in a given case.
§2 not sceptical
E2.2 from "If all moral opinions" (tolerance is a moral demand; the planets; "wrong before"; subjectivism only "inconclusive"), then E2.1 (Hart, Rawls). Close: scepticism does not even favour tolerance (Devlin's relativism).
§3 sceptical, second sense
TSH's second sceptic doubts who knows the moral facts: a real doubt†, working through prudence: slowness and privacy, reasons beyond feeling, reasons others accept, graded coercion. Concede it has no end point. Then E2.7's first bullet.
Form (b)
Second intro: the majority settles who decides, not what is right. §1 [however the majority feels] E2.3 then E2.4 from "Devlin, the theorist" (closer: the AG in LMS relied on public, not popular, morality). §2 [the legislature may decide] E1.4; closer: judges are no "mini-legislature". Cases if asked: Tan Seng Kee (E2.9), E1.9, E1.10.
Other side
Form (a): E1.6 as "the best case against"; own side: denying truth and doubting who knows can look alike, but the doubter can be shown wrong. Form (b): steelman E2.5; own side E2.6.
Repaired
Enforcing morality is legitimate when its reasons are accessible and sound and its coercion proportionate; scepticism is no objection, but fallibility is a reason for restraint. (b): the majority decides, but must decide on reasons it can defend to the minority.
In the hypo
Sin is not crime (Yaacob, Pritam Singh); 78% "disapprove" is not "make it a crime". Debt and crime support licensing, limits and help, not jail for the patron. Controversy: casinos (is it play?, E45.4).

■ Majority, neutrality and whose morality

Question FQ1 · 2018-19 Q1 · 2012-13 Q1

"Legislators should follow majority opinion on moral questions. Otherwise, they merely impose their own subjective views." Or: "Enforcing the moral judgements of certain groups must be avoided, lest minority rights be trampled." Or: "A pluralist democracy should not endorse moral values, so legislators should not try to resolve intractable moral conflicts."

Hypo Hypo 7

A bill reserves housing grants, baby bonuses and tax reliefs for married parents, and makes every school teach that children are best raised by a married mother and father. Grounds: the main faiths hold marriage sacred; children of married parents do better on average; the Constitution invites Parliament to promote marriage. An opposition MP wants a referendum instead. How would you vote on the bill and on the referendum?

Outline
Crux
[F5] [F3] "Otherwise" is a false dilemma; "lest" disarms minorities; "should not resolve" slides from not endorsing to not resolving, and leaving the law in place is itself a resolution. "Free and equal persons" borrows Rawls's premise for a conclusion he rejects.
Thesis
Define "majority opinion" (what a poll would find) and "subjective" (no reason others could examine). The majority settles who decides, not what is right; each theorist supplies a third standard: reasons every citizen can examine.
§1 majority opinion
E2.3 (Devlin, Rawls), E2.4 cut. Close: the 70% never asked about crime (Hri Kumar); a head-count binds only because each counted has equal dignity, a moral premise†.
§2 subjective views
E2.1 (Hart to "critical morality", Finnis from "the basic goods are self-evident"); with "otherwise", E4.2 from "Devlin's law-maker". Close: subjective only if no reason can be given; Rawls: officials explain by the conception they think most reasonable, citizens vote as if legislators.
§3 neutral / no group
E1.5 (Rawls, Devlin) with E4.1 (Chua 1969, TSH's hunter, repeal as a verdict, Rawls's concession). Close: minority protection is a moral judgment; TSH's dilemma.
Other way round
"No law is neutral, so enact what is true": fourth intro; concede with E4.1; E2.4; E1.2; E1.5 Finnis; E1.7's last sentence; steelman E2.5.
Other side
Steelman: democracy (E2.5) or, for neutrality, liberty and humility (E1.6). Own side: E2.6.
Repaired
The majority decides, but must decide on reasons it can defend to the minority. (Lest, decline or no-law-is-neutral forms: the legislature may take a moral stand, but only on reasons the minority can examine, with coercion graded to them.)
In the hypo
Referendum: a head-count is not a reason; MPs decide (Masagos). The bill promotes rather than punishes (low on the gradient) but the school mandate reaches religious schools and single parents' children: reasons must be ones they can examine.

E9

Moral scepticism as its own topic

Question exam-style

"Morality is just a matter of opinion; nobody can prove one moral view better than another. The law should therefore tolerate every choice and stay out of moral questions." (Or: a social construct; unprovable.)

Hypo Hypo 14

A Law Reform Commission debates commercial surrogacy. A: "Morality is just opinion, like durian. Who are we to judge?" B: "Nobody agrees, and we were wrong about slavery, so stay neutral." C: "Hume shows the studies on children tell us nothing." D: "Maximise happiness; human nature drives people to want children." G: "It is consensual and harms no one, so it is not wrong." Evaluate each.

Outline
Crux
[F3] The premise is about morality; the conclusion is a moral demand the sceptic cannot ground.
Thesis
Define moral truth (moral facts hold whatever anyone believes; where two disagree, one is wrong, TSH). Disagreement and unprovability do not show there is no moral truth; "so tolerate" defeats itself; realism demands deliberation, not imposition.
§1 just opinion
Map the sceptic (E9.1): relativism, scepticism, non-cognitivism (durian), ideology†. Then the one E9.2 row that names the premise (social construct; cannot be proved; wrong for me, the bent stick; nobody can agree, from "TSH explains").
§2 therefore tolerate
E2.2 with E2.1 (Hart, Rawls). Close: refusing to act on a judgment is acting as if its opposite were true (TSH); Devlin's relativism draws no tolerance.
§3 facts and nature
Hume's two arguments (E9.3): sentiment claim arguably fails its own test; is–ought gap only logical; the Stoics; Finnis infers nothing from urges.
§4 if a substitute
Utility is senseless across incommensurable goods; experience machine (E17.3); whatever is enacted (E37.6). Close: if law may have any content, some become ultimate lawgivers over others†.
Other side
Steelman: humility (E1.6). Own side: access, not existence; 2007's rival certainties.
Repaired
Moral claims can be true or false, but our judgements of them are fallible, so the law should rest on them provisionally, demanding more of them the more coercive it is.
In the hypo
A: durian, and "tolerate" is moral. B: "wrong before" presupposes a right answer; slowness, not abstention. C: the gap is only logical. D: urges are not goods; happiness is not one scale. G: hidden premise, autonomy's primacy, rests on equal worth (TSH).

★ The liberal chain (Sample Q1)

Question Sample Q1 · 2025-26 Q1

"Legislators should treat all moral opinions as equally valid. This can be achieved by restricting liberty only when it is objectively necessary to protect others from a wrongdoer. Only then will the law avoid imposing a fixed conception of the good life." Discuss with reference to Rawls, Finnis, Hart and Devlin, with illustrations.

Hypo Hypo 16, MP limb

Men of one minority faith may take up to four wives; everyone else faces seven years for bigamy. An MP's bill allows any three or more consenting adults to register a plural marriage. Grounds: the state has no business preferring one form of family life; plural unions harm no one and studies are inconclusive; the present law is arbitrary. An opponent says monogamy is "built into the house we live in", citing a 74% poll. How would you vote?

Outline
Crux
[F1] [F2] A chain (goal, means, consequence) is weakest where the links join. Test each link.
Thesis
Right that no doctrine should be official; wrong that a harm-only rule achieves equal validity ("objectively", "wrongdoer" presuppose judgements) or is the only route. Equal respect for persons, not equal validity for opinions.
§1 equally valid
Opener: two readings; equally true defeats itself; none privileged in advance is Rawls's, until unreasonable doctrines go; Finnis allows a range. E2.1 (Devlin; Hart a sentence) with E2.2's first two sentences. Close: fallibility counsels caution; calling a view subjective also ends debate†; 2007's rival certainties.
§2 only to protect others
E1.3: Hart, Devlin's first clause, Rawls last as the bridge (harm is Mill's comprehensive ideal). Close: consent no defence to murder (Hart: paternalism, E45.9); paternalism's targets are moral (TSH: dangerous sports v a healthy limb). E1.9's first bullet.
§3 only then … fixed
Devlin rejects the hope: a common morality is "the price of society". E1.5 Finnis with E3.1 ("fixed" is a matter of degree; Rawls's no-alien-imposition reply†; live and let live is itself a conception). Close: Rawls filters reasons, Finnis keeps peace and justice; 2022 fixed marriage in Art 156 without a life-plan.
Other side
Steelman E1.6, cut short. Own side E1.7.
Repaired
Legislators should treat all citizens, not all opinions, as equal, and coerce only on reasons every citizen can examine. For the legislator: give reasons, not a claim of neutrality.
