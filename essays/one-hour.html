Find the right essay, in one hour

You cannot memorise 45 essays, and you do not have to. The sheet holds them. This lesson trains the one skill the exam needs first: reading a statement and going straight to the right row of the Index. About 74 minutes of reading, or 59 if you keep to the 21 likely essays.

ℹ How to use this lesson

Each section sorts one group of look-alike essays. It gives a Rule for telling them apart, a Flowchart of the same rule, and then Statements written in the exam's style. The method: three questions, in this order

~3 min

You do not need to remember 45 essays. You need to sort a statement into one of five families, and then tell two or three look-alikes apart. This page teaches the sorting. The sheet does the remembering.

Rule

Question 1. Is it facts, or a statement? A set of facts with a bill and its grounds, asking how you would vote or rule, is the problem question, E45.

Question 2. Who is told what to do? Look at the last clause. If it instructs the legislator, or says what the law should enforce, you are in E1 to E13. If it puts the theory of Hart, Devlin, Rawls or Finnis on trial, you are in E14 to E19. Dworkin, Radbruch and Fuller have their own essays elsewhere: E31, E43 and E44. If it instructs judges or lawyers, or reads the Constitution, you are in E20 to E36. If it says what law is, or whether a citizen must obey, you are in E37 to E44.

Question 3. Where does it start, and where does it end? Every essay on the sheet is named "premise → demand". Look-alikes share one half and differ in the other. So read the first clause and the last clause before you choose.

Then confirm on the sheet. Find the row in the Index, check its bold finder words against the statement, and glance at the rows next to it. If no row fits, go to G1.

Four shapes repeat, and each has a standard reply. A chain joined by "therefore", "so" or "only then": test each join. An absolute built on "only", "never", "all" or "necessarily": agree with the core and reject the absolute. A dilemma built on "otherwise", "either … or" or "lest": name the third option. One claim in depth, often with "to what extent": split its key word into two senses.

1

The statement starts from a right, or from liberty

~4 min

Essays hereE1E5E6

Rule

Three essays open with the individual: a right to decide, a private choice, or liberty. What stands on the other side tells them apart.

E1 has no majority in it. A right to decide intimate matters comes first, then "therefore" the law must not enforce morality or regulate private conduct. E5 puts the majority's morality on the other side and uses harm as the line between them. E6 is a single sentence with no right and no "therefore": when law pursues moral values, liberty is "inevitably sacrificed".

If the right to decide leads on to legislators, judges and lawyers all refraining from acting on moral truth, the essay is E13, in section 2.

🧩 Statement

"Every adult is entitled to settle for himself the most personal questions of his life, such as whom to love and whether to have children. It follows that Parliament has no business enforcing morality or regulating what people do in private."

Critically comment. How far would Hart, Devlin, Rawls and Finnis agree?

E1Intimate right → no morals law

Index row 1 · intimate · regulate · harm · legislator

Deciding wordsentitled to settle (a right to decide)most personalit follows (therefore)enforcing moralityin private

Why this essay. The statement starts from a right over intimate matters and jumps by "it follows" to no morals law. There is no majority in it and no doubt about moral truth, so it is not E5 and not an essay from section 2.

What it argues. The weak word is "therefore", here "it follows". To get from the right to the conclusion the statement needs a hidden bridge: that the law may use force only to stop harm to others. Name that bridge and test it. The right is itself a moral claim, and "enforce" covers three rungs of a ladder: promote, regulate and punish.

Memory lineA right to decide is a moral claim; enforcing is a ladder; harm is the bridge you must name.

🧩 Statement

"In a democracy the majority is entitled to enforce its moral standards, even against private conduct that it merely finds repugnant."

Discuss. The reverse form also belongs here: "Private choices must prevail over the majority's morality unless they harm others."

E5Majority's morality v private choice, harm the line

Index row 5 · majority · intimate · harm

Deciding wordsthe majorityprivate conductrepugnant (condemns)unless they harm others

Why this essay. The majority has entered, and it is set against private choice. That is E5. It is not E4, which asks whose morality the legislator should follow and turns on "otherwise" or "lest".

What it argues. The statement offers a false choice between the majority and the individual. The majority decides who makes the law, but its disapproval alone is no reason to punish. Harm is the strongest reason for punishment, yet it is not a neutral line, because deciding what counts as harm is a moral judgement.

🧩 Statement

"Whenever law-makers pursue moral ends, freedom inevitably pays the price."

Do you agree?

E6Moral values → liberty inevitably sacrificed

Index row 6 · liberty · regulate

Deciding wordspursue moral endsfreedominevitablydo you agree

Why this essay. One sentence, no right and no "therefore", and the give-away word is "inevitably". The task "do you agree?" confirms it.

What it argues. The statement treats liberty and moral values as opposites, as if liberty were not a moral value too. Pursuing a value need not mean punishing. A limit backed by reasons as strong as the force it uses is a limit, not a sacrifice.

Memory lineLiberty is a moral value too; limited, not sacrificed, when the reasons match the force.

"People should be free to live as they choose in private. The community's moral disapproval can justify interference only where the conduct injures somebody else." Which essay?

  1. Correct. The community's morality is set against private choice, and injury to others is the line. This is the reverse form of E5.

  4. E6 is the one-sentence claim that liberty is "inevitably sacrificed".

  2. Nothing here says liberty is inevitably sacrificed.
  3. E8 is about neutrality between rival views of the good life, not about a right in intimate matters.
  4. E13 needs three actors, legislators, judges and lawyers, who all refrain from acting on moral truth.
2

The statement starts from doubt about morality

~7 min

Essays hereE3E7E9E2E13

Rule

Five essays mention scepticism: nobody can know what is right, all opinions are equally valid, morality is just opinion, there is no moral truth. Four start from it and one dismisses it. The opening words will not separate the four. The ending will.

E3 makes "equally valid" the legislator's goal, adds a method (force only to protect others) and a result ("only then" no fixed idea of the good life). E7 adds a step about who decides, each person or the majority, and ends on whether enforcement is legitimate. E9 goes straight from doubt to tolerance or neutrality. E13 ends with legislators, judges and lawyers all refraining, and asks whether that is "sound and workable". One neighbour starts from a narrower doubt: if nobody can say when life begins, so the law should take no side, the essay is E19 in section 5.

E2 is the odd one out. Its statement does not rely on scepticism. It dismisses scepticism and says enforcing morality is "not necessarily" unjust or illegitimate.

🧩 Statement

"A legislature ought to regard every moral outlook as equally valid. It can do so by limiting freedom only where that is strictly needed to protect other people from a wrongdoer. Only in this way does the law avoid imposing one fixed vision of the good life."

Discuss with reference to Rawls, Finnis, Hart and Devlin.

E3Equally valid → harm only → no fixed good

Index row 3 · sceptic · harm · neutral · good life · legislator

Deciding wordsequally validonly … to protect otherswrongdoeronly in this way (only then)fixed vision of the good life

Why this essay. Three links: a goal, a method and a result. The scepticism is the legislator's goal, not a bare premise as in E9, and harm is a stated middle link here, not the unstated bridge it is in E1.

What it argues. A chain breaks where its links join, so test each join. A harm-only rule does not treat all opinions as equally valid, because a word like "wrongdoer" already holds a moral judgement. Nor is it the only way to avoid a fixed idea of the good. What the law owes people is equal respect for persons, not equal validity for opinions.

Memory lineEqual respect for persons, not equal validity for opinions; "fixed" is a matter of degree.

Open the 5-minute card for E3 →

🧩 Statement

"Nobody is in a position to know moral truth better than anyone else. The majority must therefore decide, and so its enforcement of its own morality is legitimate."

Discuss. The other form: "… so each person must decide for himself, so enforcing morality is illegitimate."

E7Cannot know → each or majority decides → legitimacy

Index row 7 · sceptic · who decides · enforce

Deciding wordsnobody can knowthe majority must decide (or each person)legitimate (or illegitimate)

Why this essay. A chain from doubt, through who decides, to legitimacy. E9 has no "who decides" step. E41 also talks of the majority and legitimacy, but it starts from what law is and ends with the citizen obeying.

What it argues. Even if the doubt is granted, it decides nothing about who decides. "Each person decides" and "the majority decides" are both moral claims, and a sceptic cannot supply one. Whether enforcement is legitimate depends on its reasons and the force it uses. Answer only the form in front of you.

Memory lineScepticism decides nothing about who decides; every later link needs a moral premise the sceptic cannot supply.

Open the 5-minute card for E7 →

🧩 Statement

"Moral views are social constructs that nobody can prove. The law should therefore tolerate every way of life and keep out of moral questions."

E9Morality just opinion → tolerate, stay neutral

Index row 9 · sceptic · neutral

Deciding wordssocial constructsnobody can provetherefore toleratekeep out

Why this essay. Doubt runs straight to tolerance. There is no step about who decides (E7) and no three-link method (E3).

What it argues. "So tolerate" is itself a moral demand, and someone who doubts all moral claims cannot make it. Disagreement and the lack of proof do not show that there is no moral truth. Doubt is a reason for care, not for silence.

Open the 5-minute card for E9 →

🧩 Statement

"There is nothing necessarily unjust or illegitimate in using the law to enforce morality. The only objections worth hearing are moral and prudential ones. Sceptical objections fail."

Discuss, with the theorists' views and current controversies.

E2Enforcing morality is not illegitimate, only moral objections count

Index row 2 · enforce · sceptic · majority

Deciding wordsnot necessarily unjust or illegitimatethe only objectionsmoral and prudentialsceptical

Why this essay. The statement rejects scepticism. It asks whether enforcing morality can be legitimate and which objections count. Its second form does the same job: "Majority reasons are not always legitimate or correct. Even so, the legislature may enforce the morality of society."

What it argues. The statement is mostly right. Its one mistake is the word "sceptical", which has two senses. Denying that there is any moral truth defeats itself. Doubting who knows it is sensible, and it is a reason for care in how the state enforces, not a reason against enforcing at all.

Memory lineDenying moral truth defeats itself; doubting who knows it counsels care, not silence.

Open the 5-minute card for E2 →

🧩 Statement

"Limited government depends on suspending moral judgement. Each citizen then decides her own destiny, and justice is best served when legislators, judges and lawyers all refrain from acting on moral truth."

Is this sound and workable?

E13No moral truth → own destiny → every actor refrains

Index row 13 · sceptic · intimate · role · legislator

Deciding wordssuspending moral judgementown destinylegislators, judges and lawyersrefrainsound and workable

Why this essay. Three actors are named and the task is "sound and workable". E9 concerns the legislator only. E34 also lists several actors, but it is about the limits of their authority, not about moral truth.

What it argues. Setting moral truth aside is itself a moral commitment, so the starting point is unsound. No actor can act without moral judgement, so the rule is unworkable. Give each of the three actors a paragraph.

⚡ Quick check

"Since no moral view can be shown to be better than another, every citizen must be left to judge for himself, and a law that enforces morality has no legitimacy." Which essay?

  1. Correct. Doubt, then who decides (each citizen), then legitimacy. This is form (a).
  2. E9 jumps from doubt to tolerance. It has no step about who decides and no claim about legitimacy.
  3. E3 needs a method, force only to protect others, and "only then" no fixed idea of the good life.
  4. E2's statement dismisses scepticism. This one relies on it.

⚡ Quick check

"Scepticism about morality is no reason for the law to stay its hand. If enforcing morals is ever wrong, it is wrong for moral reasons." Which essay?

  1. Correct. The statement says sceptical objections do not count and only moral ones do. That is E2.
  2. E9's statement starts from scepticism and ends in tolerance. This one rejects scepticism.
  3. There is no chain here from doubt to who decides.
  4. E4 tells the legislator whose morality to follow. Nothing here does.

The statement starts from the majority

~5 min

Essays hereE4E10E12

Rule

The finder word "majority" sits on seven rows of the Index, and two more essays use the word. Ask what the majority is doing in the statement.

In E4 the legislator is told whose morality to follow. It has four versions: follow the majority, "otherwise" you impose your own subjective views. Enforce no group's morality, "lest" minorities suffer. Endorse no values at all, and leave intractable conflicts unresolved. Or the reverse: no law is neutral, so enact the morality you believe true.

In E10 the statement first says what law is, "simply an expression of majoritarian will", and "then" gives the legislator one job, to record it. In E12 the statement names s 377A, 2007 and 2022. In E5 the majority is set against private choice (section 1). In E15 the words are Devlin's: shared morality, disintegrate, the reasonable man (section 5). In E41 the citizen must obey (section 11). In one form of E7 nobody knows better, so the majority decides, and in one form of E2 majority reasons are not always right, yet the legislature may enforce society's morality (section 2).

🧩 Statement

"On moral issues a legislator should vote as most of his constituents think. If he does not, he is simply imposing his private opinions on everyone else."

Discuss. What would Hart, Devlin, Rawls and Finnis say?

E4Majority or own views → whose morality to follow

Index row 4 · majority · sceptic · neutral · consensus · legislator

Deciding wordsas most think (majority opinion)if he does not (otherwise)imposing his private opinions (subjective views)

Why this essay. The legislator is told whose morality to follow, and "otherwise" offers him only two choices. The other three versions in the rule above are the same essay.

What it argues. "Otherwise" is a false choice between the crowd and the legislator's own taste. Each theorist supplies a third source: reasons that every citizen can examine. The majority settles who decides, not what is right. And declining to decide is a decision too, because it leaves the law as it is.

Memory lineNot the crowd, not my taste: reasons anyone can examine. Not deciding is a decision.

Open the 5-minute card for E4 →

🧩 Statement

"Law is nothing more than the will of the majority put into words. A legislator's only task, then, is to register that will as faithfully as he can."

E10Law is majority will → legislator only records it

Index row 10 · majority · will · consensus · legislator

Deciding wordsnothing more than (simply)will of the majorityonly task (sole concern)thenas faithfully as he can

Why this essay. The first sentence says what law is. The second gives the legislator one job. Because it ends with the legislator it sits in the law-making family, not with E41. Because it starts from what law is, it is not E4.

What it argues. "Then" slides from a description to a duty. Will makes law only through rules, and those rules bind the majority too. Majority will settles who may make law, not what the law should say. And "as faithfully as he can" assumes one fixed will to read, which no poll can find.

Memory lineWill makes law only through rules; "then" slides from is to ought; trustee, not stenographer.

Open the 5-minute card for E10 →

🧩 Statement

"Parliament kept s 377A in 2007 without enforcing it, and repealed it in 2022 while protecting marriage in the Constitution. Together these decisions show that the harm principle has finally won in Singapore."

E12s 377A in 2007 and 2022 → which theory won

Index row 12 · s 377A · majority · harm

Deciding wordss 377A20072022Article 156show that

Why this essay. The statement is about Singapore's own controversy and what it shows. If s 377A is only an example inside a statement about the right to decide, stay with E1. If the question is the general one of courts or Parliament, use E36.

What it argues. Neither decision was a win for one theory. In 2007 majority feeling prevailed, softened by not enforcing the law in private. In 2022 punishment came down and promotion went up, through Article 156, and the question stayed with Parliament. That is the ladder at work, not neutrality.

Open the 5-minute card for E12 →

⚡ Quick check

"A state that contains many moral communities should not endorse any moral values. Its legislators should therefore leave intractable moral conflicts unresolved." Which essay?

  1. Correct. This is E4's third version: follow no one's morality. The reply is that not deciding is a decision.
  2. Close, but E8 examines one claim, "as neutral as possible". Here the legislator is told not to decide at all.
  3. E9 needs the premise that morality is just opinion.
  4. E11 is about religious arguments and secular reasons.

⚡ Quick check

"Law is simply what the greater number want. Parliament's job is to find out what that is and to enact it, nothing more." Which essay?

  1. Correct. It starts from what law is and ends with the legislator's sole task.
  2. E4 does not start from what law is. It starts from the legislator's choice of whose morality to follow.
  3. E41 ends with the citizen obeying. This ends with Parliament.
  4. E37 is about social fact and says nothing of the legislator's task.

Neutrality and religion

~4 min

Essays hereE8E11

Rule

"Neutral" sits on five rows. In E8 neutrality is the whole claim, examined in depth: lawmakers should be as neutral as possible between rival views of the good life. The question often adds "assess the soundness of the theories you apply".

Elsewhere neutrality is only a part. It is the last link of the harm chain in E3, the conclusion drawn from "morality is just opinion" in E9, and the refusal to endorse any values in E4. When Rawls's own theory is on trial ("political liberalism is neutral"), use E16.

E11 is the religion essay: religious arguments have no place in law-making, laws must rest on secular reasons alone, or the reverse, a religious argument is as good as any other. If the statement tests public reason as Rawls's theory, that is E16 again.

🧩 Statement

"Where citizens hold rival ideas of the good life, those who make the law should stay as neutral between them as they can."

Discuss, assessing the soundness of the theories you apply.

E8Be as neutral as possible

Index row 8 · neutral · regulate · good life · legislator

Deciding wordsrival ideas of the good lifeas neutral … as they cansoundness

Why this essay. One claim with no chain and no mention of harm, and a soundness limb. Neutrality is the subject of the statement, not a step in an argument.

What it argues. "Neutral" has two meanings, and the statement needs the impossible one. No law is neutral in its results. A law can be neutral in its reasons, but only weakly, and that aim is itself a moral position. The state should be even-handed between citizens, not between opinions.

Memory lineNeutral in outcome: impossible. Neutral in reasons: possible, but moral. Even-handed between citizens, not opinions.

Open the 5-minute card for E8 →

🧩 Statement

"In a society of many faiths, an argument drawn from religion has no place in the making of law. Statutes must rest on secular reasons and nothing else."

Discuss. The reverse form: "In Parliament a religious argument is as good as any other."

E11Multi-religious → no religious arguments

Index row 11 · religion · public reason

Deciding wordsmany faiths (multi-religious)religious argumentno placesecular reasons

Why this essay. The subject is religious arguments in law-making. Rawls will be used in the answer, but his theory is not what is on trial, so this is not E16.

What it argues. The statement treats religious arguments as one kind of thing, all banned or all as good as any other. No theorist sorts arguments that way. A faith may give a legislator a reason to speak. On its own it cannot justify forcing people outside that faith.

Memory lineSpeak from faith, coerce on reasons others can examine; sort by merit or kind, never by motive.

"Political liberalism claims to stand free of every comprehensive doctrine and yet to override them all. That is a contradiction." Which essay?

  1. Correct. "Freestanding yet overriding" tests Rawls's own theory.
  2. E8 is about neutrality in general, not about whether Rawls's theory hangs together.
  3. Nothing here is about religious arguments in law-making.
  4. E9 starts from "morality is just opinion".

⚡ Quick check

"The law cannot avoid taking sides altogether. But in a plural society it should take as few sides as it can between ways of living." Assess the soundness of the theories you use. Which essay?

  1. Correct. One claim about neutrality "as far as possible", with a soundness limb.
  2. E3 is a three-link chain from equal validity through harm.
  3. E4 tells the legislator whose morality to follow, or to decide nothing.
  4. Rawls is not named and his theory is not the subject.

One theory is on trial

~7 min

Essays hereE18E14E15E16E17E19

Rule

When a statement names one theorist's theory, or uses his trademark words, and asks you to test it, use that theorist's own essay. Hart is E14, Devlin E15, Rawls E16 and Finnis E17. Three more sit in other families: Dworkin E31, Radbruch E43, and Fuller with the rule of law E44.

Two topic essays lean on Rawls and Finnis: euthanasia is E18, which is limited to those two, and abortion is E19, which usually names them. Abortion has a twin. If the law is asked to leave the choice to each woman, use E19. If a judge is asked to read a right into the Constitution, use E23.

E14 has a shape worth knowing: admit a problem, say it is not fatal, say the theory is still the best. If a statement has that shape but is about another theorist, use E14's plan with that theorist's material.

🧩 Statement

"The common good is best served by respecting autonomy. It follows that the law must permit voluntary euthanasia, the ending of life to relieve pain and suffering, so that each person may decide how and when his life should end."

Discuss with reference to Rawls and Finnis, assessing the soundness of each.

E18Autonomy → euthanasia → free choice (Rawls, Finnis)

Index row 18 · euthanasia · Rawls · Finnis

Deciding wordsautonomycommon goodit follows that the law must (therefore demands)euthanasiahow and whenRawls and Finnis

Why this essay. Euthanasia, with two named theorists and a soundness limb. If the same topic arrives as a bill to vote on, it is the problem question, and its table sends you back to this essay for the content.

What it argues. The statement moves from a value to a statute to a right, and the weak point is "therefore demands". Autonomy deserves great weight, but neither theorist puts it first. Rawls leaves the result to public reasons and a vote. Finnis is against. And "how and when" reaches beyond relieving pain and suffering, to the healthy and the despairing.

Memory lineAutonomy weighs heavily but is not primary; a framework needs inputs, so nothing "demands" this law.

Open the 5-minute card for E18 →

🧩 Statement

"Hart's account of the limits of the criminal law cannot be applied without moral judgement. That does not destroy it. It is still the best means of keeping the criminal law to a minimum."

E14Hart: needs moral judgment → not fatal → best minimum

Index row 14 · Hart · harm

Deciding wordsHart's accountmoral judgementdoes not destroy it (not fatal)the best meansminimum

Why this essay. Hart's theory is tested in three sentences: a problem, not fatal, still the best.

What it argues. Agree, agree, then agree only in part. Needing moral judgement sinks only a theory that promised to do without it, and Hart never promised that. His theory rules out punishing immorality as such, but it is not a full account of what law may do. One trap: "the minimum" is Devlin's phrase.

Open the 5-minute card for E14 →

🧩 Statement

"A society is held together by its common morality. It may therefore punish immorality just as it punishes treason, for otherwise it will fall apart."

The other form: is the reasonable man's feeling a sound guide to what the law should punish?

E15Devlin: enforce shared morality or society disintegrates

Index row 15 · Devlin · enforce · majority · cohesion

Deciding wordscommon (shared) moralitytreasonfall apart (disintegrate)reasonable man

Why this essay. These are Devlin's own terms, so Devlin is on trial. E2 and E4 also speak of society's morality, but they ask whether or whose morality the legislature may enforce, not whether Devlin's theory holds.

What it argues. The statement gives Devlin's starting point and leaves out his safeguards. The claim that society would disintegrate is either empty or unproven, and his test lets feeling decide, not reason. Read fairly, Devlin permits far less than the statement suggests.

Open the 5-minute card for E15 →

🧩 Statement

"The state may rightfully use its power only on grounds that all citizens can share."

Other forms: political liberalism is neutral, a contradiction ("freestanding yet overriding"), or a mere modus vivendi.

E16Rawls: only grounds every citizen can share

Index row 16 · Rawls · public reason · neutral · consensus

Deciding wordsrightfullygrounds all citizens can sharepublic reasonfreestandingmodus vivendi

Why this essay. Rawls's theory itself is the subject. For neutrality in general use E8, and for religious arguments use E11.

What it argues. "Share" means what every citizen may reasonably be expected to accept, not what each in fact accepts. Rawls's neutrality is about the reasons for a law, not its results, and allowing something still takes a side. The better test is whether reasons can be examined and are sound.

Open the 5-minute card for E16 →

🧩 Statement

"Finnis's natural law either tells citizens how they must live, or it tells the law-maker nothing at all."

The task form: explain the basic goods and practical reasonableness, and how they bear on law-making.

E17Finnis: dictates how to live, or gives no guidance

Index row 17 · Finnis · natural law · good life

Deciding wordsFinnisbasic goodspractical reasonablenesseither dictates … or no guidance

Why this essay. Finnis is named and his theory is tested. A statement about natural law in general ("for the devout", a licence for judges) is E42.

What it argues. A false choice. Seven basic goods and nine requirements of practical reasonableness yield a few absolute limits and a common good that is a framework, not a life plan. Inside it practical judgement decides, and reasonable people differ.

🧩 Statement

"No one can say when human life begins. The law should therefore take no side and leave the decision to each woman."

Discuss with reference to Rawls and Finnis. The other form: "Public reason settles the question in favour of choice."

E19Nobody knows when life begins → leave abortion to choice

Deciding wordswhen life beginstake no sideeach womanpublic reason settlesRawls and Finnis

What it argues. A law that allows abortion does not "take no side". Permission answers the question of whether the unborn counts. Rawls narrows the reasons that may be used. Finnis answers the question itself. Parliament cannot stand aside, so it must argue for the limits it sets.

⚡ Quick check

"Moral reasoning cannot be kept out of adjudication. So a court may recognise a constitutional right to end a pregnancy even where the text is silent." Which essay?

  1. Correct. A judge is asked to read a right into the Constitution because moral reasoning is inevitable.
  2. E19 is the law-maker's abortion essay, with Rawls and Finnis. Here the actor is a court.
  3. E36 asks who should decide in general. It is a neighbour, but the "moral reasoning is inevitable, therefore" chain belongs to E23.
  4. E21's statement says judges bring no moral judgement at all, which is the opposite premise.

The problem question

~5 min

Essays hereE45E18

Rule

E45 is not an essay statement. You get facts: a bill in Ruritania, usually resting on a faith, a study about harm and a poll. You vote as an MP, then say whether you would enforce it as a judge. The two hats ask different questions, so different answers are no contradiction.

Each ground has its block. The faith goes to E1.4, the harm to E1.3, the poll to E2.3, and the judge's hat is E45.1. For any other kind of bill, find the topic in table E45.3, which names the blocks to use.

Three rules sit on top. A bill on a topic that has its own essay borrows that essay. An intolerable law sends the judge to E24, never to the judge's ladder in E45.1. And if there is no bill at all, go to the essay for the role: the lawyer is E33, a judge or lawyer in an immoral system is E24, and the citizen is E38.

🧩 Hypothetical

Ruritania's Parliament is debating a bill that makes it a crime, punishable by up to three years in prison and with no exception, to sell or play violent video games. The MP who moved it gives three grounds. A faith followed by a large minority teaches that taking pleasure in violence corrupts the soul. Studies suggest that heavy players tend to become more aggressive. And 82% of those polled "somewhat support" a ban.

How would you vote as an MP? If the bill passes, would you enforce it as a judge?

E45Ruritania bill: vote as MP, rule as judge

Index row 45 · religion · harm · majority

Deciding wordsa billthree groundsa faithstudiesa pollvote as an MPenforce as a judge

Why this essay. A bill with grounds and two hats. Video games have no row of their own in E45.3, so use E45 as written and sort each ground into its block.

What it argues. As an MP, vote against and move narrower measures, because none of the grounds justifies prison terms with no exception. The faith is a voice, not a ground. The harm is a reason to regulate, and in principle even to penalise the user, but severity is a separate question that it cannot carry here. The poll is a gauge of opinion. As a judge, enforce the law if it is validly passed: for Hart it is valid, and on Radbruch's formula it is harsh but not intolerable.

One word, several rows

~4 min

Essays hereG1

Rule

In the exam you will often spot a word before you see the shape. The sheet's word finder then gives you several rows. This table shows how to choose between them. The row numbers are the ones printed in the Index.

Three forms are not caught by the finder words. "Adjudication is politics, and fidelity is a pretence", with no further step, is E23. Who decides under the Constitution, or whether an amendment is valid, is E36. "Why should lawyers care about theory?" is E33.

When no row fits

G1 is for statements that no row of the Index holds. Do not force them into a near miss. Name the type, then use its ready plan on the sheet. There are four main types. Same result, different reasons: two theorists agree on the outcome, so the statement says their quarrel does not matter. A label made to do moral work: a theory of what law is, is said to dictate how to judge or practise. Who decides, for every actor: each role hands the question to someone else. The role answers for its wrongs: whoever acts within a legitimate role is not responsible.

What it argues. Agree that they reach the same result. Then show that their reasons still decide four things: the hard case, the next case, what the losing side is owed, and what survives a change of government.

Mixed drill

~3 min

Twelve statements in no order, as the exam will give them. For each, run the three questions, name the essay, then tap. Aim for under thirty seconds each.

  1. "Majority support does not make a law right. Nevertheless Parliament is entitled to give legal force to the moral standards of the community."

    E2Enforcing morality is not illegitimate, only moral objections count

    The second form of E2: majority reasons are not always correct, yet the legislature may enforce society's morality.

  2. "No one can prove that one way of life is better than another. The law must therefore tolerate all of them."

    E9Morality just opinion → tolerate, stay neutral

    Doubt runs straight to tolerance, with no step about who decides.

  3. "Judges should leave moral philosophy to the philosophers. Deciding according to the law is justice enough."

    E27Judging by law serves justice → avoid lofty morality

    Judging according to the law is tied to justice, and morality is waved away.

  4. "A good advocate leaves his conscience at the office door. That is not a failing. It is the job."

    E33Good lawyer, good person only as far as the system allows

    Lawyers alone, and whether the job leaves room to be a good person.

  5. "Enforcing the moral views of any one group must be avoided, or the rights of minorities will be trampled."

    E4Majority or own views → whose morality to follow

    The "lest" form of E4. Do the neutrality section first.

  6. "The repeal of s 377A shows that the state has at last become neutral on sexual morality."

    E12s 377A in 2007 and 2022 → which theory won

    Singapore's own controversy and what it shows. The word "neutral" is only the claim being tested.

  7. "Theories of law are necessarily in play whenever a court interprets a statute or develops the common law."

    E28Theory and morality necessarily in common law and statutes

    Judges, common law and statutes, "necessarily".

  8. "Whether the death penalty should be kept is a moral question for Parliament, and judges have no business answering it."

    E36Moral questions are for Parliament, not the courts

    Who decides a contested moral question. No s 377A in it, so it is not E12.

  9. "Legislators should treat every conviction as equally valid, and they do so by leaving each person a right to settle her own destiny. Only then is no single idea of the good life imposed."

    E3Equally valid → harm only → no fixed good

    Still the three-link chain. The method is a right to decide, not harm, so use E3 with its right-to-decide section.

  10. A bill bans the sale of sugary drinks, with prison for repeat sellers. Its grounds are a faith's teaching on gluttony, studies on diabetes, and a poll showing 77% support. How would you vote, and would you enforce it?

    E45Ruritania bill: vote as MP, rule as judge

    E21Judge makes no moral judgment, only applies the law → desirable

    Judges alone: described, then praised.

  12. "Because nobody knows better than anybody else what is right, the question must go to the majority, whose decision is then legitimate."

    E7Cannot know → each or majority decides → legitimacy

    Doubt, then who decides, then legitimacy. This is form (b).
